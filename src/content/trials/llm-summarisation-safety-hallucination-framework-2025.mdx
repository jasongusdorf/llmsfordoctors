## Study Design

The title matters here, because this paper is routinely cited as "the GPT-4 note hallucination rate study" and it is broader than that. Asgari and colleagues set out to build a framework with four parts: an error taxonomy for classifying LLM outputs, an experimental structure for iterative comparison within a document-generation pipeline, a clinical safety framework for evaluating the harm of errors, and a graphical interface called CREOLA to run the process.

They ran 18 experiments, each on 25 primary care consultation transcripts, mostly from the publicly available PriMock dataset, with one experiment on ACI Bench. That gave 450 transcript-note pairs, 49,590 transcript sentences, and 12,999 clinical note sentences. Fifty medical doctors annotated, paid £5 per note, with at least two clinician reviewers per input-output pair and discrepancies consolidated by a senior clinician with over 20 years of experience.

**One model was tested.** GPT-4, specifically GPT-4-32k-0613, with seed 210, temperature 0, and top-p 0.95. The 18 experiments vary prompts and workflows, not models. The authors list this as a limitation.

The taxonomy defines hallucinations as "instances of text unsupported by the associated clinical documentation," subdivided into fabrication, negation, causality, and contextual types; and omissions as "instances where relevant details are missed in the supporting evidence," subdivided into current issues, past medical and family and social history, and information and plan. Errors were graded major or minor, where major "can impact on the diagnosis or the management of the patient if not corrected." The harm framework was modeled on medical device certification, combining likelihood of occurrence with potential impact on clinical outcome.

## Key Findings

- **Hallucination rate 1.47%:** 191 of 12,999 note sentences.
- **Omission rate 3.45%:** 1,712 of 49,590 transcript sentences. Note the denominators differ; these are not directly comparable per-note rates.
- **The severity inverts the frequency.** 84 of 191 hallucinations (44%) were graded major. Only 286 of 1,712 omissions (16.7%) were. As the authors put it, hallucinations were "much more likely to be classified as a 'major' error compared to omissions (44% to 16.7%, respectively)."
- **Hallucination types:** fabricated 82 (43%), negations 56 (30%), contextual 33 (17%), causality-related 20 (10%).
- **Location matters.** Major hallucinations occurred in every section but concentrated in the Plan (21%), Assessment (10.5%), and Symptoms (5.2%) sections. Fabrications appeared primarily in the planning section.
- **Negations are the most alarming subtype.** These "mostly appeared in the planning section and contradicted what was said during the consultation." A note that records the opposite of the plan discussed is a different class of problem than a note that invents a detail.
- **Major omissions clustered in current issues (55%),** then past medical, family and social history (35%), then information and plan (10%).
- **Prompt and workflow changes moved the numbers substantially.** Between two experiments, major hallucinations fell 75% (from 4 to 1) and major omissions 58% (from 24 to 10). A function-call series eliminated major omissions entirely, from 61 to 0, and cut minor omissions 58% from 130 to 54. One workflow change in the opposite direction raised major hallucinations from 4 to 25.
- **Best-performing configurations, per 25 notes:** one experiment produced 1 major hallucination and 10 major omissions; another produced 2 major hallucinations and 0 major omissions.
- **A clinician-written comparison arm was run.** In one experiment, clinicians manually wrote notes from the same transcripts. The authors report "slightly more hallucinations in clinician-written notes but fewer omissions," with the numeric detail in supplementary material.

<Callout type="evidence">
  The authors quote external benchmarks for context: prior work reported 3.9 errors and 6.6 omissions per note from a BART model, against 1 error and 4 omissions per human-written note. Their claim is that prompt engineering brought major errors below previously reported human note-taking rates. That is a claim about their best configurations, not about the aggregate 1.47% and 3.45%.
</Callout>

## Clinical Implications

1. **The error you should expect is the missing one.** Omissions were more than twice as common as hallucinations. That inverts the usual anxiety, which is entirely about fabrication. When reviewing an AI-generated note, the harder task is noticing what is not there, and it is harder precisely because nothing on the screen prompts you to look.

2. **But per error, hallucinations are worse.** Forty-four percent were major against 16.7% for omissions. The practical synthesis is that omissions dominate the volume of errors and hallucinations dominate the severity, so a review process needs to catch both, by different means.

3. **Read the Plan section twice.** That is where major hallucinations concentrated, and it is the section that carries direct instructions to colleagues and patients. The authors make exactly this point: it "often contains direct instructions or actions to colleagues or patients that can impact clinical safety."

4. **Negation errors will not look like errors.** A fabricated finding often reads as slightly off. A negation reads as a perfectly ordinary clinical statement that happens to be the reverse of what was said. Thirty percent of hallucinations were of this type, and no amount of scanning for implausibility will catch them. Only comparison against what actually happened will.

5. **The pipeline is a bigger lever than the model.** Holding GPT-4 constant, prompt and workflow changes eliminated major omissions in one configuration and quintupled major hallucinations in another. If your institution is evaluating an ambient documentation product, the configuration is at least as much the object of evaluation as the underlying model, and the vendor's configuration is not static.

<Callout type="pitfall">
  All seven authors were employed by Tortus AI at the time of writing, and the senior author is its CEO. The paper proposes a framework and a platform that the authors' company builds, and it reports that iterative use of that platform drove errors below human baselines. The taxonomy and the annotation methodology are sound and reusable; the claim that this pipeline achieves sub-human error rates comes from the vendor.
</Callout>

## Limits Worth Naming

The sample is small by design, chosen to balance annotation volume against the number of experiments. Only one model was tested. Only direct prompting was used, with no retrieval-augmented generation, chain of thought, or knowledge graphs. Inter-rater reliability is described procedurally, two reviewers plus senior consolidation, but no agreement statistic is reported. The severity distribution beyond major and minor exists only in figures. And the Plan-section hallucination percentage is printed as 21% in the results and 20% in the discussion.

---

## My Takeaway

This paper's real contribution is the taxonomy and the severity framework, not the two headline percentages. Splitting errors into fabrication, negation, causality, and context, and then grading each for whether it would change diagnosis or management, gives you something you can actually run against a vendor's output during a pilot. Most published error rates are a single number that conflates a missing social history with a fabricated medication.

The finding I would hand to a colleague is the inversion. We worry about the model making things up. It does, at about 1.5% of sentences, and those errors are disproportionately serious and disproportionately located in the plan. But it leaves things out at more than twice that rate, and leaving things out is the failure that a busy reviewer, reading a fluent and complete-looking note, is structurally least equipped to catch.

The vendor authorship is a real caveat on the optimistic conclusions and not much of one on the descriptive findings. Take the framework; hold the sub-human-error-rate claim loosely.
