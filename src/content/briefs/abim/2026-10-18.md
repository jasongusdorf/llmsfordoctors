1. **Cardiology**. Think of atrial flutter whenever a regular narrow-complex tachycardia runs at about 150 beats/min. Typical flutter is a macroreentrant circuit around the right atrium that depends on the cavotricuspid isthmus, with an atrial rate near 300 and 2:1 AV conduction; the sawtooth flutter waves are negative in II, III, and aVF and are easily hidden in the T wave. Adenosine or vagal maneuvers transiently increase the block and unmask the flutter waves but do not terminate the rhythm. Stroke risk is managed exactly as in atrial fibrillation, using the same risk score and the same pericardioversion anticoagulation rules. Catheter ablation of the cavotricuspid isthmus succeeds in more than 90% of typical flutter and is a reasonable first-line option for recurrent or symptomatic flutter, but many patients later develop atrial fibrillation, so ablation does not by itself settle the anticoagulation question.

2. **Pulmonary medicine**. Lymphangioleiomyomatosis (LAM) belongs in the differential when a woman of reproductive age has recurrent pneumothorax, a chylous pleural effusion, or dyspnea with diffuse thin-walled round cysts on CT, and especially when she has tuberous sclerosis complex or a renal angiomyolipoma. In a patient with a compatible CT, a serum VEGF-D of at least 800 pg/mL establishes the diagnosis without lung biopsy (ATS/JRS 2016 guideline). Sirolimus is the treatment for abnormal or declining lung function: in the MILES trial (2011) it stabilized FEV1 over a year, whereas FEV1 kept falling on placebo, and the decline resumed after the drug was stopped. Because estrogen is thought to drive the disease, estrogen-containing contraception is avoided.

3. **Gastroenterology**. Hepatocellular carcinoma is one of the few solid tumors that can be diagnosed without a biopsy. In a high-risk patient (cirrhosis, or chronic hepatitis B), a lesion of at least 1 cm on multiphase contrast CT or MRI showing arterial-phase hyperenhancement followed by washout is diagnostic (LI-RADS 5), and biopsy is reserved for indeterminate lesions. That shortcut does not apply to a noncirrhotic liver without hepatitis B, where the imaging pattern is less specific. Surveillance in cirrhosis is ultrasound, with or without AFP, every 6 months. An AFP alone is not adequate surveillance, and a lesion found on surveillance ultrasound gets multiphase imaging, not an immediate biopsy.

4. **Infectious disease**. Leptospirosis follows exposure to fresh water or soil contaminated by animal urine: floods, freshwater swimming, triathlons, rodent-infested housing, farm and sewer work. The biphasic febrile illness brings abrupt fever, headache, severe myalgia (classically in the calves), and conjunctival suffusion, which is redness without exudate. Severe disease (Weil disease) combines jaundice, acute kidney injury, and bleeding, and pulmonary hemorrhage is a leading cause of death. The kidney injury is characteristically nonoliguric with potassium wasting, so hypokalemia alongside a rising creatinine is a clue. Do not wait for serology, which is often negative early: treat mild disease with oral doxycycline and severe disease with intravenous penicillin or ceftriaxone.

5. **Rheumatology**. IgA vasculitis (Henoch-Schönlein purpura) in an adult is not the benign childhood illness. The tetrad is palpable purpura over the legs and buttocks with a normal platelet count, arthralgia or arthritis, colicky abdominal pain or gastrointestinal bleeding, and glomerulonephritis. Skin biopsy shows leukocytoclastic vasculitis, and direct immunofluorescence shows IgA deposits in the vessel walls; that IgA staining is what separates it from other small-vessel vasculitides. Adults develop nephritis more often than children and have a worse renal prognosis, so check urinalysis, creatinine, and urine protein at diagnosis and repeat them during follow-up. Kidney biopsy looks like IgA nephropathy. Adult-onset disease has been linked to solid tumors, so check that age-appropriate cancer screening is up to date.

6. **Endocrinology**. Screen for acromegaly with a serum IGF-1, interpreted against age- and sex-specific norms. A random growth hormone level is not useful because secretion is pulsatile. If IGF-1 is elevated or equivocal, confirm by showing that GH fails to suppress below 1 ng/mL after an oral glucose load (Endocrine Society 2014; lower cutoffs are used with ultrasensitive assays), then image the pituitary with MRI. At diagnosis, look for the comorbidities that drive mortality: hypertension, cardiomyopathy, sleep apnea, and diabetes. Screening colonoscopy is advised because of the higher risk of colonic polyps. Transsphenoidal surgery is first-line. When pegvisomant, a GH-receptor antagonist, is used, monitor IGF-1 rather than GH, because GH levels rise on the drug.

7. **Hematology**. In pernicious anemia, autoimmune atrophic gastritis removes intrinsic factor. Anti-intrinsic factor antibodies are highly specific but positive in only about half of patients; anti-parietal cell antibodies are more sensitive but nonspecific. When the serum B12 is borderline, an elevated methylmalonic acid supports B12 deficiency, whereas homocysteine rises in both B12 and folate deficiency. High-dose oral cyanocobalamin (1,000 to 2,000 mcg daily) works even without intrinsic factor, because about 1% is absorbed by passive diffusion, so intramuscular injection is not obligatory once patients are adherent and neurologic deficits are not severe. Never treat with folate alone, which can correct the anemia while the neurologic damage progresses. The chronic hypergastrinemia predisposes to gastric neuroendocrine tumors and adenocarcinoma, so endoscopy at diagnosis is reasonable. Associated autoimmune thyroid disease is common.

8. **Oncology**. Hairy cell leukemia classically presents in a middle-aged man as pancytopenia with marked splenomegaly but little lymphadenopathy. Monocytopenia is a characteristic clue and a reason for susceptibility to atypical mycobacterial infection. Bone marrow aspiration is often a "dry tap" because of reticulin fibrosis, so the diagnosis rests on biopsy and flow cytometry: B cells with hairlike projections that coexpress CD11c, CD25, and CD103. Almost all classic cases carry BRAF V600E, which the variant form lacks. Treatment with a purine analog (cladribine or pentostatin) yields high and durable complete-remission rates. Asymptomatic patients with adequate counts can be observed.

9. **Nephrology**. Focal segmental glomerulosclerosis (FSGS) is a histologic pattern, and the first task is to separate primary from secondary disease, because only primary disease is treated with immunosuppression. Primary FSGS presents as full nephrotic syndrome with low serum albumin and diffuse podocyte foot-process effacement on electron microscopy. Maladaptive secondary FSGS (obesity, reduced nephron mass, reflux nephropathy, sickle cell disease) presents with proteinuria, often in the nephrotic range, but typically with a normal serum albumin and only segmental effacement; it is managed with RAAS blockade and treatment of the cause. The collapsing variant is linked to HIV, other viral infections, and high-risk APOL1 genotypes in people of West African ancestry, and has the worst prognosis. Primary FSGS can recur after kidney transplant.

10. **Neurology**. Narcolepsy causes chronic daytime sleepiness despite adequate nighttime sleep. Type 1 is defined by cataplexy or a low CSF hypocretin-1 (at most 110 pg/mL). Cataplexy is brief, bilateral loss of muscle tone triggered by emotion, usually laughter, with consciousness fully preserved. That distinguishes it from syncope and seizure. Confirm with overnight polysomnography followed by a multiple sleep latency test showing a mean sleep latency of 8 minutes or less and at least two sleep-onset REM periods (a sleep-onset REM period on the preceding night's study can count as one). Test only after adequate sleep and, where safe, after stopping REM-suppressing drugs such as antidepressants, which distort the result. Sodium oxybate treats both sleepiness and cataplexy; modafinil, pitolisant, and solriamfetol treat sleepiness.
