1. **Cardiology**. Wellens syndrome is a pain-free ECG pattern of deeply inverted or biphasic T waves in the anterior precordial leads after recent angina, usually signaling critical proximal left anterior descending disease. Troponin may be normal or only mildly elevated. Do not send the patient for exercise or pharmacologic stress testing, which can precipitate infarction; admit, treat as an acute coronary syndrome, and arrange urgent coronary angiography.

2. **Pulmonary medicine**. Ventilate acute respiratory distress syndrome with a lung-protective strategy: use tidal volume based on predicted rather than actual body weight and limit plateau pressure. A common target is about 6 mL/kg predicted body weight with plateau pressure no higher than 30 cm H2O. Permissive hypercapnia is generally safer than increasing tidal volume enough to cause ventilator-induced lung injury.

3. **Gastroenterology**. In acute pancreatitis, feed enterally as soon as tolerated rather than waiting for pain or pancreatic enzymes to normalize; a low-fat solid diet is acceptable without first advancing through clear liquids. Prophylactic antibiotics do not prevent infection of sterile necrosis and should not be given solely because pancreatitis is severe. Antibiotics are reserved for suspected or confirmed infection or another infectious indication.

4. **Infectious disease**. Corticosteroids and other immunosuppression can convert chronic *Strongyloides* infection into fatal hyperinfection with pulmonary and gastrointestinal disease plus gram-negative bacteremia or meningitis. Screen patients with relevant residence or travel exposure before planned immunosuppression when feasible, and treat documented infection with ivermectin. In suspected hyperinfection, do not wait for serology before starting therapy; examine stool and respiratory specimens for larvae and reduce immunosuppression if possible.

5. **Rheumatology**. Urate-lowering therapy need not be stopped during an acute gout flare, and when indicated it can be started during the flare if effective anti-inflammatory treatment is also given. Allopurinol is first-line even in chronic kidney disease, but begin at a low dose and titrate to a serum-urate target rather than leaving a renally adjusted dose permanently subtherapeutic. Continue flare prophylaxis during the early months of urate lowering.

6. **Endocrinology**. Central diabetes insipidus causes hypotonic polyuria because arginine vasopressin secretion is deficient; nephrogenic diabetes insipidus reflects renal resistance. Desmopressin markedly concentrates the urine in complete central disease but has little effect in complete nephrogenic disease. Excess desmopressin plus unrestricted water intake can cause dangerous hyponatremia, so treatment requires sodium monitoring and periodic opportunity for free-water excretion.

7. **Hematology**. Suspected acute promyelocytic leukemia is a hematologic emergency because disseminated intravascular coagulation can cause catastrophic bleeding before the diagnosis is confirmed. Start all-trans retinoic acid immediately when morphology and presentation suggest APL while urgently obtaining PML::RARA testing and supporting platelets and fibrinogen. Do not wait for cytogenetic confirmation to begin retinoic acid; stop it if APL is subsequently excluded.

8. **Oncology**. High-risk febrile neutropenia requires prompt empiric intravenous antipseudomonal beta-lactam therapy after cultures are obtained, without waiting for a source or culture result. Cefepime, piperacillin-tazobactam, or an antipseudomonal carbapenem are standard monotherapy options. Vancomycin is not routine; add it for a specific indication such as hemodynamic instability, pneumonia, suspected catheter infection, skin or soft-tissue infection, or known resistant gram-positive infection.

9. **Nephrology**. Cholesterol crystal embolization often follows arterial instrumentation or anticoagulation and causes subacute kidney injury with livedo reticularis or blue toes despite palpable pulses; eosinophilia and low complement may occur. Unlike contrast-associated kidney injury, onset is often delayed and systemic findings are common. Management is supportive and emphasizes cardiovascular risk reduction; anticoagulation does not dissolve cholesterol emboli and should be continued only for a separate compelling indication.

10. **Neurology**. Cerebral venous sinus thrombosis commonly presents with headache, seizures, papilledema, focal deficits, or hemorrhagic venous infarction. Diagnose it with CT or MR venography and anticoagulate with heparin even when the venous infarct contains intracerebral blood, unless another major contraindication exists. Endovascular therapy is reserved for selected patients who deteriorate despite anticoagulation rather than used routinely.
