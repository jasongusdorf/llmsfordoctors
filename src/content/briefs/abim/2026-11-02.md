1. **Cardiology**. Think of Chagas cardiomyopathy in a patient from rural Latin America with a cardiomyopathy and conduction disease. *Trypanosoma cruzi* is spread mainly by the feces of triatomine ("kissing") bugs, and also congenitally, by transfusion, and by organ transplant. Most chronically infected people never develop disease. A minority, often quoted as 20 to 30%, develop heart disease decades later. The classic ECG is right bundle branch block with left anterior fascicular block. Other features are segmental wall-motion abnormalities, an apical aneurysm that can hold thrombus and cause stroke, ventricular tachycardia, and sudden death. Chronic infection is diagnosed serologically with two different IgG assays, because parasitemia is low and PCR is insensitive at that stage. Antiparasitic therapy (benznidazole or nifurtimox) is clearly indicated for acute, congenital, and reactivated infection. In BENEFIT (2015), benznidazole did not slow clinical progression in established cardiomyopathy.

2. **Pulmonary medicine**. One positive sputum culture for *Mycobacterium avium* complex is not a diagnosis. The 2020 ATS/ERS/ESCMID/IDSA guideline requires three things: symptoms, compatible imaging (nodular bronchiectasis or fibrocavitary disease), and microbiology. The microbiology must be positive cultures from at least two separate expectorated sputum samples, or one bronchoscopic wash or lavage, or a biopsy showing compatible histology. The classic patient is a thin, older woman with no smoking history and nodular bronchiectasis of the right middle lobe and lingula. Test macrolide susceptibility before treating, and never use a macrolide alone. For macrolide-susceptible disease, give azithromycin, rifampin, and ethambutol: three times weekly for noncavitary nodular-bronchiectatic disease, and daily, with amikacin, for cavitary or severe disease. Continue for at least 12 months after sputum cultures turn negative.

3. **Gastroenterology**. Pill esophagitis is sudden odynophagia and retrosternal pain after taking a tablet with too little water or just before lying down. The usual culprits are doxycycline and other tetracyclines, bisphosphonates, potassium chloride, NSAIDs, iron, and quinidine. Injury is most often in the mid-esophagus, where the aortic arch or an enlarged left atrium narrows the lumen. Endoscopy, if done, shows discrete ulcers with normal surrounding mucosa. The injury heals over days to weeks once the drug is stopped or taken correctly. Prevention is to take the pill with a full glass of water and stay upright afterward; for oral alendronate that means at least 30 minutes. In an immunocompromised patient with odynophagia, think of *Candida*, HSV, and CMV first.

4. **Infectious disease**. Q fever (*Coxiella burnetii*) is caught by inhaling dust contaminated by birth products of sheep, goats, or cattle, so ask about farms, veterinary work, and slaughterhouses. No tick bite is needed. Many infections are asymptomatic. Acute illness is high fever and headache with atypical pneumonia, hepatitis (with "doughnut" granulomas on biopsy), or both. Diagnosis is by serology against phase I and phase II antigens: phase II antibodies dominate in acute infection, and high phase I IgG points to chronic (persistent focal) infection. Acute Q fever is treated with doxycycline for 14 days. Chronic infection is classically a culture-negative endocarditis in a patient with valve disease, a prosthetic valve, or a vascular graft or aneurysm. Per CDC 2013, treat it with doxycycline plus hydroxychloroquine for at least 18 months for a native valve and 24 months for a prosthetic valve.

5. **Rheumatology**. Eosinophilic fasciitis looks like scleroderma but is a different disease. It causes rapid, symmetric, painful swelling and then hardening of the arms and legs, sometimes after unusually hard physical exertion. Two signs are a "groove sign" (veins that look sunken when the limb is raised) and peau d'orange skin. The tell is what is missing: the hands, feet, and face are usually spared, and there is no Raynaud phenomenon, sclerodactyly, or nailfold capillary change. Peripheral eosinophilia, hypergammaglobulinemia, and a high ESR are common, but the eosinophilia can be transient. Confirm with a full-thickness skin-to-muscle biopsy that includes fascia, or with MRI showing fascial thickening. Look for an associated hematologic disorder, such as aplastic anemia, myelodysplasia, or lymphoma. Treatment is glucocorticoids, often with methotrexate.

6. **Endocrinology**. Polycystic ovary syndrome is the most common cause of hirsutism. Tempo and virilization tell you when to look harder. Rapid onset or progression, clitoromegaly, a deepening voice, or a markedly high testosterone suggest an androgen-secreting ovarian or adrenal tumor. A high DHEAS points to an adrenal source. Screen for nonclassic congenital adrenal hyperplasia with an early-morning 17-hydroxyprogesterone in the follicular phase. Also check TSH and prolactin. For premenopausal women not seeking pregnancy, the Endocrine Society 2018 guideline recommends a combined oral contraceptive first. Add an antiandrogen such as spironolactone if the response is inadequate after about 6 months. Do not use antiandrogens without reliable contraception, because they can feminize a male fetus.

7. **Hematology**. When a patient becomes hypoxemic with bilateral infiltrates during or after a transfusion, separate TRALI from TACO. TRALI starts within 6 hours. It is noncardiogenic, caused mostly by donor anti-HLA or anti-neutrophil antibodies, with no left atrial hypertension. Hypotension, fever, and transient leukopenia favour TRALI. Treatment is supportive, and diuretics do not help. TACO is hydrostatic overload, with onset up to 12 hours under the 2018 ISBT criteria. Hypertension, a raised JVP, positive fluid balance, an elevated BNP, and a response to diuretics favour TACO, and older patients and those with heart failure or CKD are at higher risk. Both are leading causes of transfusion-related death, and both should be reported to the blood bank. Male-predominant plasma lowers TRALI risk. Transfusing one unit at a time at slower rates, and reassessing in between, lowers TACO risk.

8. **Oncology**. Under the AUA 2020 microhematuria guideline, microhematuria is 3 or more red cells per high-power field on a single properly collected urinalysis with microscopy. A positive dipstick alone is not enough and needs microscopic confirmation. First exclude benign causes such as infection, menstruation, vigorous exercise, and recent instrumentation, and repeat the urinalysis after treating them. Dysmorphic red cells, casts, or proteinuria point to a glomerular cause and to nephrology. Otherwise, the patient's risk of urothelial cancer sets the workup: age, sex, smoking pack-years, degree of hematuria, and prior gross hematuria. Low-risk patients can have a repeat urinalysis within 6 months or cystoscopy, by shared decision. Intermediate-risk patients get cystoscopy and renal ultrasound. High-risk patients get cystoscopy and CT urography. Gross hematuria always needs a urologic evaluation, and anticoagulation does not explain away hematuria at either level.

9. **Nephrology**. Creatinine-based eGFR fails when muscle mass is unusual: sarcopenia, frailty, amputation, cirrhosis, very high muscle mass, or a vegetarian diet. The 2021 NKF-ASN task force recommended the race-free CKD-EPI 2021 equation and wider use of cystatin C. KDIGO 2024 advises a combined creatinine-cystatin C eGFR when the creatinine estimate is thought to be inaccurate and the result affects a decision, such as drug dosing or CKD staging. Cystatin C has its own confounders: thyroid dysfunction, high-dose glucocorticoids, inflammation, and obesity. Also know the drugs that raise serum creatinine by blocking tubular secretion without lowering true GFR: trimethoprim, cimetidine, dolutegravir, and the boosters cobicistat and ritonavir. A small, stable rise after starting one of them is not AKI.

10. **Neurology**. Medication-overuse headache (ICHD-3) is headache on 15 or more days a month in someone with a preexisting headache disorder, with regular overuse for more than 3 months. Overuse means 10 or more days a month of triptans, ergots, opioids, combination analgesics, or more than one drug class, or 15 or more days a month of simple analgesics such as acetaminophen or NSAIDs. Opioids and butalbital combinations carry the highest risk and should be avoided in migraine. Treatment is withdrawal of the overused drug together with starting a preventive. Simple analgesics and triptans can be stopped abruptly. Butalbital needs a taper because abrupt withdrawal can cause seizures, and opioids usually need a taper too. Count the patient's medication days at every headache visit, because the overuse is easy to miss.
