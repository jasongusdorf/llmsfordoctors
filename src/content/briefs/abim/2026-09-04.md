1. **Cardiology**. Mechanical complications of MI. Classically days 2–7 after a late-presenting or unreperfused infarct. Papillary muscle rupture: acute severe MR and flash pulmonary edema, usually an inferior MI, because the posteromedial papillary muscle has a single blood supply from the PDA; the murmur may be soft or absent since the left atrium equalizes pressure fast. Ventricular septal rupture: harsh holosystolic murmur with a thrill and an oxygen saturation step-up from right atrium to right ventricle on right heart cath. Free wall rupture: sudden tamponade and PEA arrest. All three are surgical. Afterload reduction and an intra-aortic balloon pump are bridges, not treatment, and a vasodilator is contraindicated once the patient is hypotensive.

2. **Pulmonary**. Obesity hypoventilation syndrome is BMI ≥30 plus awake daytime hypercapnia (PaCO2 >45 mmHg) with no other explanation. The screening clue is an unexplained elevated serum bicarbonate; a bicarbonate below about 27 mEq/L makes OHS unlikely and spares the blood gas. Roughly 90% of these patients also have obstructive sleep apnea, so the usual first-line therapy is CPAP with weight loss; noninvasive ventilation is for those without significant concurrent OSA or who fail CPAP. Missing it is costly: untreated OHS carries higher mortality and pulmonary hypertension than OSA alone.

3. **Gastroenterology**. Hepatorenal syndrome (HRS-AKI) is a diagnosis of exclusion made only after diuretics and beta-blockers are stopped and volume is expanded with albumin 1 g/kg daily for 2 days. It requires no shock, no recent nephrotoxin, and no evidence of structural kidney injury (bland sediment, minimal proteinuria). Treatment is a vasoconstrictor plus albumin: terlipressin is first-line (CONFIRM), with norepinephrine as the ICU alternative and midodrine-octreotide the weakest option. Terlipressin causes respiratory failure, so avoid it in advanced acute-on-chronic liver failure or with high baseline creatinine. The only definitive therapy is liver transplantation.

4. **Infectious disease**. Staphylococcus aureus bacteremia is never "just a positive culture." Repeat blood cultures every 48–72 hours until documented clearance, get an echocardiogram in everyone (TEE is preferred, especially with a prosthesis, pacemaker, or persistent bacteremia), remove the intravascular catheter, and hunt for metastatic foci. Infectious disease consultation independently lowers mortality. Uncomplicated disease — no endocarditis, no prosthetic material, cultures clear within 2–4 days, fever resolved within 72 hours, no metastatic infection — gets at least 14 days of IV therapy from the first negative culture; anything else gets 4–6 weeks. For MSSA use cefazolin or an antistaphylococcal penicillin, not vancomycin, which performs worse.

5. **Rheumatology**. Dermatomyositis is the inflammatory myopathy with the strongest cancer association, so proximal weakness plus Gottron papules, a heliotrope rash, or a shawl/V-sign rash earns age-appropriate cancer screening plus CT of chest, abdomen, and pelvis. Antibodies do the risk stratification: anti-TIF1-γ and anti-NXP2 carry the highest malignancy risk, anti-MDA5 means clinically amyopathic disease with rapidly progressive interstitial lung disease, and the antisynthetase antibodies (anti-Jo-1 and relatives) predict ILD, mechanic's hands, Raynaud, and arthritis rather than cancer.

6. **Endocrinology**. Thyroid storm has an order of operations. Beta-blocker for symptoms, then a thionamide, and only then iodine, given at least an hour after the thionamide — iodine first supplies substrate for new hormone synthesis. Add a glucocorticoid, which both treats relative adrenal insufficiency and blocks peripheral T4-to-T3 conversion. Propylthiouracil is preferred over methimazole in storm because it also blocks that peripheral conversion, and in the first trimester of pregnancy because methimazole is teratogenic; switch back afterwards, since PTU carries the hepatotoxicity risk.

7. **Hematology**. Acute chest syndrome is a new pulmonary infiltrate in a patient with sickle cell disease plus fever or respiratory symptoms, and it is the leading cause of death in that population. Treat with oxygen, analgesia, and antibiotics covering typical and atypical organisms (a cephalosporin plus a macrolide); give simple transfusion for hypoxia or a falling hemoglobin, and exchange transfusion for severe or rapidly progressive disease. Two prevention points: incentive spirometry during a vaso-occlusive crisis reduces the incidence, and hydroxyurea reduces recurrence. Avoid over-hydration and over-sedation, both of which make it worse.

8. **Oncology**. Colorectal cancer screening now starts at 45 for average-risk adults and runs to 75, with individualized decisions from 76 to 85 (USPSTF 2021). Any of these strategies is acceptable: colonoscopy every 10 years, annual FIT, stool DNA-FIT every 1–3 years, CT colonography every 5 years, or flexible sigmoidoscopy every 5 years. Family history moves the goalposts: a first-degree relative with colorectal cancer or an advanced adenoma means colonoscopy starting at age 40, or 10 years before the youngest affected relative's diagnosis, repeated every 5 years. A positive noninvasive test must be followed by colonoscopy, not by repeating the stool test.

9. **Neurology**. Dementia with Lewy bodies: fluctuating cognition, recurrent well-formed visual hallucinations, REM sleep behavior disorder, and parkinsonism. The timing rule separates it from Parkinson disease dementia — dementia appearing before or within a year of the motor signs is DLB; motor signs preceding dementia by more than a year is PD dementia. The clinical trap is severe neuroleptic sensitivity: a typical antipsychotic can cause marked worsening of parkinsonism and autonomic instability. Cholinesterase inhibitors help cognition and hallucinations more here than in most dementias.

10. **Psychiatry**. Serotonin syndrome versus neuroleptic malignant syndrome. Serotonin syndrome comes on within about 24 hours of a serotonergic drug being added or increased, with hyperreflexia, clonus that is worse in the legs, mydriasis, and hyperactive bowel sounds; treat by stopping the drug plus benzodiazepines, adding cyproheptadine if severe. NMS develops over days to weeks after a dopamine antagonist (or dopaminergic withdrawal), with lead-pipe rigidity, hyporeflexia, and a markedly elevated CK; treat by stopping the agent plus benzodiazepines, with dantrolene or bromocriptine in severe cases. Reflexes and time course are the discriminators, not fever.
