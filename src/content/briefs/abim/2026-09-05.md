1. **Cardiology**. Constrictive pericarditis vs restrictive cardiomyopathy. Both give elevated JVP, edema, and small ventricles with big atria, but constriction is surgically curable and restriction is not. Constriction: pericardial knock, Kussmaul sign, respiratory septal shift with ventricular interdependence on echo, and a preserved or *increased* medial mitral annular e' with medial exceeding lateral ("annulus reversus"). Restriction: markedly elevated BNP, low tissue Doppler velocities at both annuli. On catheterization the most specific finding for constriction is discordance of right and left ventricular systolic pressures across the respiratory cycle. Definitive therapy for chronic constriction is pericardiectomy.

2. **Pulmonary**. Alpha-1 antitrypsin deficiency. Test the serum alpha-1 antitrypsin level once in every patient diagnosed with COPD, regardless of age or smoking history — this is a guideline recommendation, not a niche one, and the disease is badly underdiagnosed. Clues that should raise suspicion further: emphysema before 45, a *basilar* panacinar distribution rather than the usual apical centrilobular pattern, unexplained cirrhosis, or a family history. A low level goes on to genotyping/phenotyping (PiZZ is the classic severe form). Associated extrapulmonary disease includes necrotizing panniculitis and ANCA-associated vasculitis. Smoking cessation matters more than anything else; augmentation therapy is for emphysema with confirmed severe deficiency.

3. **Gastroenterology**. Achalasia. The discriminating history is dysphagia to solids *and* liquids from the very beginning; a mechanical stricture or ring causes solid-food dysphagia first. Barium swallow shows the dilated esophagus with a bird's-beak taper, but the diagnosis is made on high-resolution manometry: absent peristalsis plus failure of the lower esophageal sphincter to relax (elevated integrated relaxation pressure). Upper endoscopy is mandatory in every case to exclude pseudoachalasia from a gastroesophageal junction malignancy — suspect that when the patient is older, symptoms are short-lived, and weight loss is out of proportion. Definitive options are pneumatic dilation, laparoscopic Heller myotomy, or peroral endoscopic myotomy; botulinum toxin is for poor procedural candidates because the effect wanes.

4. **Infectious disease**. Clostridioides difficile. The 2021 IDSA/SHEA focused update moved fidaxomicin 200 mg twice daily for 10 days ahead of oral vancomycin for an initial episode and for recurrence, on the strength of lower recurrence rates; vancomycin 125 mg four times daily remains an acceptable alternative, and cost often decides. Metronidazole is no longer first-line for any standard episode. Fulminant disease — hypotension or shock, ileus, or megacolon — is different: high-dose oral or nasogastric vancomycin 500 mg four times daily plus intravenous metronidazole, with rectal vancomycin added if there is ileus. Two testing rules that get tested: do not send formed stool, and never test for cure, since carriage persists for weeks.

5. **Rheumatology**. Antiphospholipid syndrome. Diagnosis requires a clinical event (thrombosis or defined pregnancy morbidity) plus a persistently positive antibody — lupus anticoagulant, anticardiolipin, or anti-beta-2 glycoprotein I — confirmed on repeat testing at least 12 weeks apart, because transient positivity after infection is common. The classic paradox: a prolonged aPTT that fails to correct on mixing study, in a patient who clots rather than bleeds. Anticoagulation is warfarin to an INR of 2–3, and this is one of the few remaining places where DOACs are the wrong answer: the TRAPS trial of rivaroxaban in triple-positive patients was stopped early for excess thrombotic events, and guidelines advise against DOACs in triple positivity or arterial thrombosis. A false-positive RPR is a well-known incidental clue.

6. **Endocrinology**. Pheochromocytoma. Screen with plasma free metanephrines or 24-hour urinary fractionated metanephrines, not catecholamines, and draw plasma samples supine after rest since upright sampling generates false positives; acetaminophen, tricyclics, and labetalol also interfere. Imaging comes after biochemical confirmation, never before. The management rule that boards love: alpha blockade first, beta blockade only afterwards — a beta-blocker given alone leaves alpha-mediated vasoconstriction unopposed and can precipitate hypertensive crisis. The old "rule of 10s" is obsolete; roughly a third to 40% of these tumors carry a germline mutation, so refer everyone for genetic testing.

7. **Hematology**. B12 versus folate deficiency. Both cause megaloblastic anemia with hypersegmented neutrophils, but only B12 deficiency causes neurologic disease — subacute combined degeneration of the dorsal columns and lateral corticospinal tracts, which can appear before any anemia or macrocytosis. When the B12 level is low-normal and the clinical suspicion is real, methylmalonic acid is the discriminator: it is elevated in B12 deficiency and normal in folate deficiency, while homocysteine rises in both. Practical consequence: never replete folate alone in an unclarified megaloblastic anemia, because it corrects the blood count while the neurologic damage progresses.

8. **Oncology**. Tumor lysis syndrome. The metabolic signature is hyperkalemia, hyperphosphatemia, and hyperuricemia with a *low* calcium, and the hypocalcemia is a consequence of the phosphate load. Do not correct asymptomatic hypocalcemia with calcium — it promotes calcium-phosphate precipitation in the kidney; treat only symptomatic hypocalcemia or arrhythmia. Aggressive intravenous fluids are the backbone; urinary alkalinization is no longer recommended. Allopurinol prevents new urate formation but does nothing to the urate already there, so high-risk disease (Burkitt lymphoma, ALL, a very high white count, bulky chemosensitive tumor) or established hyperuricemia calls for rasburicase, which is absolutely contraindicated in G6PD deficiency because it triggers hemolysis and methemoglobinemia.

9. **Dermatology**. DRESS versus Stevens-Johnson syndrome. DRESS declares itself late, typically 2 to 8 weeks after the drug is started — a longer latency than SJS/TEN — and the giveaway triad is facial edema, eosinophilia or atypical lymphocytes, and internal organ involvement, most often transaminitis. The rash is morbilliform and confluent rather than the dusky targetoid lesions with skin detachment and mucosal erosions of SJS/TEN. Usual culprits: allopurinol, aromatic anticonvulsants (carbamazepine, phenytoin, lamotrigine), sulfonamides, vancomycin, and minocycline; herpesvirus reactivation, classically HHV-6, is part of the pathophysiology. Stop the drug, give systemic glucocorticoids with a slow taper because the syndrome relapses on rapid withdrawal, and check thyroid function months later, since autoimmune thyroiditis is a recognized late sequela.

10. **General internal medicine**. Who gets treated for osteoporosis. Pharmacologic therapy is indicated for a T-score of −2.5 or below at the femoral neck, total hip, or lumbar spine; for any prior hip or vertebral fragility fracture, regardless of the T-score; or for osteopenia (T-score −1.0 to −2.5) with a FRAX 10-year probability of at least 3% for hip fracture or 20% for major osteoporotic fracture. An oral bisphosphonate is standard first-line therapy, but at very high risk an anabolic agent (teriparatide, abaloparatide, or romosozumab) first, *followed by* an antiresorptive to lock in the gains, produces greater benefit. The high-yield trap is denosumab: stopping it without transitioning to a bisphosphonate causes rebound bone loss and multiple vertebral fractures, so it is not a drug to let lapse.
