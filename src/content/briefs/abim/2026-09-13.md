1. **Cardiology**. In atrial fibrillation with hemodynamic instability attributable to the arrhythmia, perform immediate synchronized electrical cardioversion. In a stable patient with atrial fibrillation lasting at least 48 hours or of unknown duration, elective cardioversion requires either adequate therapeutic anticoagulation beforehand or transesophageal echocardiography to exclude atrial thrombus, followed by anticoagulation afterward. Long-term anticoagulation is determined by thromboembolic risk, not by whether sinus rhythm was restored; direct oral anticoagulants are preferred over warfarin except with a mechanical heart valve or moderate-to-severe rheumatic mitral stenosis.

2. **Pulmonary medicine**. A patient with suspected asthma but normal baseline spirometry still needs objective evidence of variable expiratory airflow limitation. Repeat spirometry when symptomatic or use bronchoprovocation; a negative methacholine challenge makes current asthma unlikely. Vocal-cord dysfunction is a common mimic: inspiratory noise, throat tightness, abrupt attacks, and flattening of the inspiratory flow-volume loop point toward inducible laryngeal obstruction, which is confirmed by laryngoscopy during symptoms rather than treated by escalating inhaled glucocorticoids.

3. **Gastroenterology**. Suspected acute variceal hemorrhage should trigger three treatments before endoscopic confirmation: a vasoactive drug such as octreotide, short-course antibiotic prophylaxis, and restrictive red-cell transfusion unless another condition demands a higher target. Perform urgent endoscopy after initial resuscitation; esophageal varices are treated with band ligation. If bleeding is uncontrolled, balloon tamponade or an esophageal stent is only a bridge to definitive therapy, usually rescue transjugular intrahepatic portosystemic shunt.

4. **Infectious disease**. A painful vesicular rash in one or more dermatomes is herpes zoster, but lesions on the nose or around the eye suggest ophthalmic division involvement and require prompt antiviral therapy plus urgent ophthalmologic evaluation. The classic Hutchinson sign—vesicles on the tip or side of the nose—raises the risk of ocular disease but its absence does not exclude it. Disseminated cutaneous disease, visceral involvement, or severe immunocompromise calls for intravenous acyclovir and airborne plus contact precautions until lesions have crusted.

5. **Rheumatology**. Relapsing polychondritis causes recurrent inflammation of cartilaginous structures: auricular chondritis characteristically spares the noncartilaginous earlobe, while nasal disease can produce a saddle-nose deformity. Hoarseness, stridor, or wheezing may reflect laryngotracheal cartilage involvement rather than asthma and can become life-threatening. Ocular inflammation, inflammatory arthritis, audiovestibular disease, and aortic-valve or large-vessel involvement support the diagnosis; biopsy is reserved for atypical cases.

6. **Endocrinology**. Screen for primary aldosteronism with an aldosterone-to-renin ratio in patients with resistant hypertension, hypertension plus spontaneous or diuretic-induced hypokalemia, an adrenal incidentaloma, or other high-risk features. Hypokalemia is not required and is absent in many cases. Suppressed renin is the essential clue; correct hypokalemia and account for medications that distort renin or aldosterone before interpreting the test. Unilateral disease is treated surgically when appropriate, whereas bilateral adrenal hyperplasia is treated with a mineralocorticoid-receptor antagonist.

7. **Hematology**. Heparin-induced thrombocytopenia is a prothrombotic immune reaction: a platelet fall beginning several days after heparin exposure, new thrombosis, or skin necrosis matters more than the absolute platelet count, and bleeding is uncommon. Use the 4Ts score before ordering laboratory tests; a low-probability score effectively excludes HIT. With intermediate or high probability, stop every source of heparin—including flushes—and start a nonheparin anticoagulant while testing. Do not start warfarin until platelets recover because early protein C depletion can cause venous limb gangrene.

8. **Oncology**. New back pain in a patient with cancer plus weakness, sensory loss, gait difficulty, or bladder or bowel dysfunction is malignant spinal-cord compression until proved otherwise. Give glucocorticoid promptly when neurologic compromise is suspected and obtain contrast-enhanced MRI of the entire spine, because clinically silent noncontiguous lesions are common. Treatment is urgent surgical decompression with stabilization followed by radiation for appropriate operative candidates; radiation alone is used when surgery is unsuitable. Do not wait for loss of ambulation, which predicts poorer neurologic recovery.

9. **Nephrology**. Nephrotic syndrome creates a hypercoagulable state through urinary loss of endogenous anticoagulants and increased hepatic procoagulant synthesis. Membranous nephropathy carries particularly high risk, and renal-vein thrombosis may present with flank pain, hematuria, worsening proteinuria, or an unexplained decline in kidney function—but is often silent. Confirm suspected renal-vein thrombosis with venous-phase CT or MR imaging and anticoagulate unless contraindicated; prophylactic anticoagulation without documented thrombosis is individualized according to albumin level, bleeding risk, and the underlying glomerular disease.

10. **Neurology**. In myasthenic crisis, respiratory failure can develop despite a normal oxygen saturation and without marked distress. Follow forced vital capacity and inspiratory force serially, assess bulbar function, and intubate electively when weakness is progressing rather than waiting for an abnormal blood gas. Treat with either intravenous immunoglobulin or plasma exchange and address triggers such as infection or medication exposure; glucocorticoids and other immunosuppressants provide longer-term control but do not act quickly enough to rescue the crisis. Noninvasive ventilation is only for carefully selected patients without severe bulbar weakness or rapidly worsening respiratory failure.
