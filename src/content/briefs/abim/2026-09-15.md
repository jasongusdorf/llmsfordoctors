1. **Cardiology**. Cardiac tamponade is a hemodynamic diagnosis: hypotension, elevated jugular venous pressure, tachycardia, pulsus paradoxus, and electrical alternans may occur, but none is required. Echocardiographic clues include right-atrial systolic collapse, right-ventricular diastolic collapse, marked respiratory variation in transvalvular flow, and a plethoric inferior vena cava. An unstable patient needs urgent pericardial drainage; a normal blood pressure does not exclude tamponade, especially when effusion accumulates slowly.

2. **Pulmonary medicine**. Hypersensitivity pneumonitis results from an immune response to an inhaled antigen, commonly birds, mold, or occupational exposure. High-resolution CT often shows centrilobular ground-glass nodules, mosaic attenuation, and expiratory air trapping; fibrosis, traction bronchiectasis, and upper- or mid-lung predominance suggest chronic disease. Exposure avoidance is the essential treatment. Serum precipitins document exposure but do not prove causation, and their absence does not exclude the diagnosis.

3. **Gastroenterology**. Hepatorenal syndrome–acute kidney injury is diagnosed in cirrhosis with ascites after excluding shock, nephrotoxins, structural kidney disease, and persistent kidney injury despite appropriate volume resuscitation when indicated. The urine sediment is typically bland, but urine sodium indices are unreliable in advanced cirrhosis. Treat with albumin plus a vasoconstrictor, with terlipressin preferred when appropriate; evaluate for liver transplantation because drug therapy is a bridge, not a cure.

4. **Infectious disease**. Nocardiosis should be considered in a patient with impaired cell-mediated immunity who has a subacute pulmonary infection, especially with nodules, cavitation, or failure of ordinary antibacterial therapy. Nocardia is a branching, weakly acid-fast aerobic bacterium that can disseminate to the brain, sometimes without neurologic symptoms, so confirmed pulmonary disease generally warrants brain imaging. Trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole is a treatment backbone; severe or disseminated infection initially requires combination therapy tailored to susceptibility testing.

5. **Rheumatology**. ANCA-associated vasculitis can cause rapidly progressive glomerulonephritis, pulmonary capillaritis, neuropathy, skin disease, and upper-airway inflammation, but ANCA testing neither proves nor excludes the diagnosis. Obtain tissue when feasible, while treating immediately if organ-threatening disease makes delay unsafe. Severe granulomatosis with polyangiitis or microscopic polyangiitis is induced with glucocorticoids plus rituximab or cyclophosphamide; plasma exchange is not routine solely for kidney failure but may be considered in selected patients with life-threatening pulmonary hemorrhage.

6. **Endocrinology**. Thyroid storm is a clinical diagnosis in a severely thyrotoxic patient with fever, marked tachycardia, central nervous system dysfunction, gastrointestinal or hepatic dysfunction, and often heart failure. Treat immediately with a beta-blocker when hemodynamically tolerated, a thionamide, iodine given after the thionamide, glucocorticoid, supportive care, and treatment of the precipitant. Propylthiouracil also reduces peripheral conversion of thyroxine to triiodothyronine, but methimazole is generally less hepatotoxic; severe hepatic injury changes that choice.

7. **Hematology**. Acute chest syndrome in sickle cell disease is a new pulmonary infiltrate accompanied by fever or respiratory symptoms and may worsen rapidly even when the initial radiograph is subtle. Treat with oxygen as needed, careful analgesia, incentive spirometry, antibiotics covering typical and atypical organisms, and transfusion when clinically indicated. Use exchange transfusion for severe or progressive hypoxemia, extensive infiltrates, or deterioration despite simple transfusion; avoid excessive intravenous fluid because pulmonary edema can compound the lung injury.

8. **Oncology**. Leukostasis is a clinical emergency caused by extreme leukocytosis, most often in acute myeloid leukemia, with respiratory distress, hypoxemia, headache, confusion, visual symptoms, or focal neurologic deficits. Begin urgent cytoreduction and tumor-lysis prophylaxis while arranging definitive leukemia therapy; leukapheresis may be used selectively but must not delay chemotherapy. Avoid reflexive red-cell transfusion before cytoreduction when the patient is stable, because increasing viscosity can worsen leukostasis.

9. **Nephrology**. Contrast-associated acute kidney injury usually appears within the first few days after iodinated contrast and is diagnosed only after considering other causes such as sepsis, hypotension, embolization, or nephrotoxic drugs. The best-supported prevention for a high-risk patient who truly needs contrast is appropriate isotonic intravenous volume expansion and use of the lowest necessary contrast dose. N-acetylcysteine and prophylactic dialysis do not prevent injury, and a necessary contrast study should not be withheld reflexively when its diagnostic benefit is substantial.

10. **Neurology**. Suspected Wernicke encephalopathy requires immediate parenteral thiamine; do not wait for the full triad of confusion, gait ataxia, and ophthalmoplegia, because most patients do not have all three. Give thiamine before or with glucose, but never delay emergency glucose for hypoglycemia. Alcohol use disorder is only one risk factor—malnutrition, prolonged vomiting, bariatric surgery, cancer, and hyperemesis gravidarum also confer risk—and untreated disease can progress to irreversible Korsakoff syndrome.
