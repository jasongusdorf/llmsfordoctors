1. **Cardiology**. In an inferior myocardial infarction, hypotension with elevated jugular venous pressure and clear lungs suggests right-ventricular infarction. Obtain right-sided precordial leads and assess volume status. The right ventricle is preload dependent, so give cautious intravenous fluid when appropriate and avoid nitrates and other preload-reducing drugs in a hypotensive patient. Persistent shock despite preload optimization requires reperfusion and hemodynamic support, not repeated fluid boluses that provoke pulmonary edema.

2. **Pulmonary medicine**. Hypoxemia from ventilation-perfusion mismatch usually improves substantially with supplemental oxygen; a true right-to-left shunt improves little because blood bypasses ventilated alveoli. Diffusion limitation generally worsens with exercise as capillary transit time shortens, whereas hypoventilation produces hypoxemia with an elevated arterial carbon dioxide and a normal alveolar-arterial oxygen gradient when the lungs themselves are normal. These patterns are more useful than memorizing a disease list.

3. **Gastroenterology**. Acute cholangitis is biliary obstruction plus infection: fever, jaundice, and right-upper-quadrant pain are classic, while hypotension or altered mental status signals severe disease. Start intravenous antibiotics and resuscitation, but antibiotics alone are insufficient when obstruction persists. Urgent biliary decompression, usually by endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography, is the decisive treatment; cholecystectomy does not decompress an obstructed common bile duct during the acute emergency.

4. **Infectious disease**. When infective endocarditis is suspected in a hemodynamically stable patient, obtain multiple blood-culture sets from separate venipunctures before antibiotics. Do not wait for fever spikes. Begin empiric therapy immediately after cultures when illness is acute or complicated, and before cultures if instability makes delay unsafe. A negative transthoracic echocardiogram does not exclude endocarditis when pretest probability is high; proceed to transesophageal echocardiography, especially with a prosthetic valve or intracardiac device.

5. **Rheumatology**. Polymyalgia rheumatica causes bilateral shoulder and hip-girdle aching with prolonged morning stiffness, usually with elevated inflammatory markers, but it does not cause true muscle weakness or elevate muscle enzymes. A brisk response to low-dose glucocorticoid supports the diagnosis but is not specific. Always ask about new headache, jaw claudication, scalp tenderness, and visual symptoms because coexisting giant-cell arteritis requires immediate high-dose glucocorticoid to prevent irreversible blindness.

6. **Endocrinology**. Adrenal crisis presents with hypotension or shock, volume depletion, abdominal symptoms, hyponatremia, and often hyperkalemia in primary adrenal failure; hypoglycemia may occur. Draw cortisol and adrenocorticotropic hormone if this does not delay care, then give intravenous hydrocortisone and isotonic fluid immediately. Dexamethasone is useful when cortisol testing must remain interpretable because it does not significantly cross-react with most cortisol assays. Treat the precipitating illness as well as the hormone deficiency.

7. **Hematology**. Suspected thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura is a treatment-before-confirmation emergency. Microangiopathic hemolytic anemia with thrombocytopenia, neurologic or renal abnormalities, fever, or no better explanation should prompt an ADAMTS13 sample followed immediately by plasma exchange and glucocorticoid; caplacizumab is added in appropriate acquired disease. Do not wait for the full classic pentad or the assay result. Platelet transfusion can fuel microvascular thrombosis and is reserved for life-threatening bleeding or an urgent invasive procedure.

8. **Oncology**. Superior vena cava syndrome causes facial or upper-extremity swelling, venous distention, dyspnea, cough, and symptoms that worsen when supine. Elevate the head and secure a tissue diagnosis before cancer-directed therapy when the patient is stable, because lymphoma, lung cancer, thrombosis, and benign device-related obstruction require different treatment. Airway compromise, cerebral edema, or hemodynamic instability demands urgent intervention; endovascular stenting provides the fastest relief for severe obstruction.

9. **Nephrology**. Acute interstitial nephritis is often drug induced, with beta-lactam antibiotics, proton-pump inhibitors, nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, and immune checkpoint inhibitors among common causes. The classic triad of fever, rash, and eosinophilia is uncommon, and urine eosinophils are neither sensitive nor specific. Stop the suspected drug and exclude other causes of acute kidney injury; kidney biopsy is the diagnostic standard when uncertainty would change management, while glucocorticoids are considered for significant disease that fails to improve after withdrawal.

10. **Neurology**. Convulsive status epilepticus is treated clinically without waiting for imaging or electroencephalography. Stabilize the airway and circulation, check bedside glucose, give a benzodiazepine promptly, and follow with a longer-acting antiseizure medication because benzodiazepine effect is brief. Persistent altered consciousness after movements stop may represent nonconvulsive status and requires urgent electroencephalography. Search simultaneously for reversible triggers such as hypoglycemia, electrolyte disturbance, infection, toxin exposure, medication withdrawal, or stroke.
