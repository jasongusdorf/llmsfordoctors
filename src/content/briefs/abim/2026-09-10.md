1. **Cardiology**. Digoxin toxicity is usually precipitated by acute kidney injury or a drug interaction, and the serum level does not reliably track toxicity in chronic exposure. Gastrointestinal symptoms, confusion, color-vision changes, and almost any dysrhythmia can occur; the classic combination is atrial tachycardia with atrioventricular block. Hypokalemia increases digoxin binding and predisposes to toxicity, whereas hyperkalemia in an acute overdose signals severe sodium-potassium ATPase inhibition. Give digoxin-specific antibody fragments for life-threatening arrhythmia, hemodynamic instability, severe hyperkalemia, or a large acute ingestion. Electrical cardioversion can provoke ventricular arrhythmia and should be reserved for immediately life-threatening rhythms when antibody fragments are unavailable or have not worked.

2. **Pulmonary**. Pulmonary alveolar proteinosis is accumulation of surfactant from impaired alveolar-macrophage clearance, most often caused by autoantibodies to GM-CSF. Think of progressive dyspnea with hypoxemia, bilateral symmetric ground-glass opacity plus interlobular septal thickening ("crazy paving"), and a milky bronchoalveolar lavage that is PAS-positive. Serum anti-GM-CSF antibodies confirm autoimmune disease; transbronchial or surgical biopsy is rarely needed. Whole-lung lavage is standard therapy for clinically important disease, and inhaled GM-CSF is an alternative or adjunct. These patients are unusually susceptible to opportunistic infections, especially Nocardia, mycobacteria, and fungi.

3. **Gastroenterology**. Ischemic colitis typically causes sudden crampy left-sided abdominal pain followed by hematochezia in an older patient after transient hypoperfusion. It favors watershed regions such as the splenic flexure and rectosigmoid junction; CT may show segmental colonic wall thickening, and colonoscopy confirms pale, edematous, friable mucosa when there are no peritoneal signs. Most cases improve with bowel rest, intravenous fluid, and correction of the low-flow trigger. Peritonitis, gangrene, perforation, or ongoing instability requires surgery. Do not confuse it with acute mesenteric ischemia: severe pain out of proportion to examination calls for urgent CT angiography and revascularization, not routine colonoscopy.

4. **Infectious disease**. Fever in a patient without a functioning spleen is a medical emergency because overwhelming infection from encapsulated bacteria can become fatal within hours. Obtain cultures promptly and give empiric parenteral antibiotics without waiting for results; ceftriaxone is a usual backbone, with vancomycin added when resistant pneumococcus or meningitis is a concern. Prevention requires pneumococcal, meningococcal, and Haemophilus influenzae type b vaccination, plus annual influenza vaccination. Howell-Jolly bodies on the smear document splenic dysfunction. Also remember Capnocytophaga after a dog bite: asplenic patients are at particular risk for fulminant sepsis and warrant prompt bite-wound prophylaxis.

5. **Rheumatology**. Adult-onset Still disease is a diagnosis of exclusion marked by quotidian spiking fever, inflammatory arthritis, a transient salmon-colored rash that appears with fever, neutrophilic leukocytosis, and often striking hyperferritinemia; rheumatoid factor and ANA are usually negative. The dangerous complication is macrophage activation syndrome, suggested by persistent fever, organomegaly, cytopenias, hepatitis, coagulopathy, and a rapidly rising ferritin. A falling erythrocyte sedimentation rate can be falsely reassuring because macrophage activation consumes fibrinogen, which normally drives the sedimentation rate. Systemic disease is treated with glucocorticoids, with IL-1 or IL-6 blockade for severe or refractory disease.

6. **Endocrinology**. Myxedema coma is decompensated severe hypothyroidism: altered mental status, hypothermia, bradycardia, hypoventilation, hyponatremia, and hypoglycemia, often triggered by infection, cold exposure, sedatives, or surgery. Treat on clinical suspicion without waiting for thyroid tests. Give stress-dose glucocorticoid before or with intravenous thyroid hormone until concomitant adrenal insufficiency is excluded, support ventilation, correct sodium and glucose carefully, and treat the precipitant. Use passive rather than aggressive external rewarming, because peripheral vasodilation can worsen hypotension. A normal TSH does not exclude central hypothyroidism; interpret it with free T4 and the clinical setting.

7. **Hematology**. Acquired hemophilia A should be suspected when a patient with no personal or family bleeding history develops large ecchymoses, soft-tissue bleeding, or postpartum bleeding with an isolated prolonged aPTT. The aPTT fails to correct on an incubated mixing study because an autoantibody inhibits factor VIII; confirm with factor VIII activity and inhibitor titer. Unlike congenital hemophilia, hemarthroses are uncommon. Control major bleeding with a bypassing agent or recombinant porcine factor VIII, and eradicate the inhibitor with immunosuppression. Giving plasma alone does not overcome a high-titer inhibitor.

8. **Oncology**. Neutropenic enterocolitis (typhlitis) is fever, neutropenia, and right-lower-quadrant abdominal pain with bowel-wall thickening, usually involving the cecum after cytotoxic chemotherapy. CT is the diagnostic study; avoid colonoscopy and barium enema because an inflamed neutropenic bowel can perforate. Initial treatment is bowel rest, intravenous fluid, and broad-spectrum antibiotics covering gram-negative organisms including Pseudomonas plus anaerobes. Add antifungal therapy when fever persists or invasive fungal infection is suspected. Surgery is reserved for perforation, uncontrolled bleeding, clinical deterioration, or another surgical abdomen.

9. **Nephrology**. Drug-induced acute interstitial nephritis is usually recognized by acute kidney injury with pyuria, white-cell casts, and modest proteinuria after a culprit such as a beta-lactam, proton-pump inhibitor, NSAID, rifamycin, or immune checkpoint inhibitor. The classic fever-rash-eosinophilia triad occurs in only a minority, and urine eosinophils are neither sensitive nor specific. Stop the offending drug first. Kidney biopsy is the diagnostic standard when the diagnosis is uncertain or renal function does not improve; glucocorticoids are often used for persistent or severe biopsy-supported disease, but the evidence is observational. NSAID-associated disease may produce nephrotic-range proteinuria and often lacks allergic features.

10. **Neurology**. Posterior reversible encephalopathy syndrome presents with headache, seizures, encephalopathy, and visual symptoms in the setting of severe hypertension, eclampsia, kidney failure, autoimmune disease, or cytotoxic and immunosuppressive drugs. MRI shows vasogenic edema, classically in the parieto-occipital white matter; diffusion restriction suggests infarction rather than uncomplicated vasogenic edema. Treat the cause, control blood pressure carefully, remove the offending drug when possible, and treat seizures. The name is imperfect: lesions may extend beyond the posterior brain, and delayed treatment can leave infarction, hemorrhage, or permanent deficits.
