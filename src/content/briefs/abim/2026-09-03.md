1. **Cardiology**. Cardiac tamponade is a clinical diagnosis that echo confirms, not the reverse. The exam finding to trust is pulsus paradoxus: an inspiratory drop in systolic pressure of more than 10 mmHg. ECG shows low voltage and, less often, electrical alternans; echo shows right atrial and right ventricular diastolic collapse with a plethoric IVC. Treatment is urgent pericardiocentesis. Two traps: do not diurese or start positive-pressure ventilation (both cut preload and can precipitate arrest), and if the effusion is from a type A aortic dissection or ventricular rupture, needle drainage is relatively contraindicated; go to surgery.

2. **Pulmonary**. Acute PE is treated by risk tier. Hemodynamically stable with sPESI of 0 (age ≤80, no cancer, no chronic cardiopulmonary disease, HR <110, SBP ≥100 mmHg, SaO2 ≥90%) can go home on a DOAC. Intermediate-high risk (RV dysfunction on imaging plus a positive troponin) gets anticoagulation and monitoring, not routine lysis: in PEITHO, tenecteplase prevented hemodynamic decompensation but roughly doubled major bleeding and caused hemorrhagic stroke in about 2%. Systemic thrombolysis is for high-risk PE: sustained hypotension (SBP <90 mmHg for 15 minutes or more) or vasopressor requirement.

3. **Gastroenterology**. Alcohol-associated hepatitis: severity is Maddrey discriminant function ≥32 (4.6 × [PT − control] + bilirubin) or MELD >20. Severe disease without active infection, GI bleeding, or renal failure gets prednisolone 40 mg daily for 28 days. Reassess with the Lille score at day 7: a score of 0.45 or above means nonresponse, so stop the steroid. Pentoxifylline does not work (STOPAH), and steroids improve 28-day survival at best, not 90-day, so the durable intervention is abstinence.

4. **Infectious disease**. Ixodes ticks in the Northeast and upper Midwest transmit Lyme, anaplasmosis, and babesiosis, and coinfection is common. Anaplasmosis: fever, leukopenia, thrombocytopenia, transaminitis, morulae inside neutrophils; doxycycline. Ehrlichiosis is the south-central look-alike (Lone Star tick, morulae in monocytes), same drug. Babesiosis: hemolytic anemia with intraerythrocytic ring forms or the rare Maltese cross on smear, confirmed by PCR; treat with atovaquone plus azithromycin (clindamycin plus quinine is the alternative). Doxycycline does nothing for Babesia. Severe disease in asplenic, elderly, or immunocompromised patients: exchange transfusion when parasitemia is 10% or higher, or with severe hemolysis or organ dysfunction.

5. **Rheumatology**. CPPD (pseudogout): acute knee or wrist monoarthritis in an older patient, with rhomboid, weakly positively birefringent crystals on polarized microscopy and chondrocalcinosis on film (knee menisci, triangular fibrocartilage of the wrist, symphysis pubis). Treat the flare like gout: NSAIDs, colchicine, or intra-articular glucocorticoid; low-dose colchicine for frequent recurrences. There is no urate-lowering equivalent. The board question is the secondary screen: in patients under about 60 or with florid polyarticular disease, check for hemochromatosis (iron studies), hyperparathyroidism (calcium), hypomagnesemia, and hypophosphatasia (low alkaline phosphatase).

6. **Endocrinology**. Hypopituitarism has an ordering rule: replace glucocorticoid before thyroid hormone, because levothyroxine accelerates cortisol clearance and can precipitate adrenal crisis in an unrecognized ACTH deficiency. In central hypothyroidism the TSH is low or inappropriately normal, so it cannot be used for diagnosis or titration; follow free T4 and aim for the mid to upper normal range. Mineralocorticoid replacement is not needed in secondary adrenal insufficiency because aldosterone is renin-driven, not ACTH-driven.

7. **Hematology**. Microcytosis with a normal or high RBC count, a normal RDW, and normal iron studies is thalassemia trait, not iron deficiency: a Mentzer index (MCV divided by RBC count in millions) under 13 points to thalassemia. Beta-thalassemia trait shows elevated HbA2 (above about 3.5%) on hemoglobin analysis; alpha-thalassemia trait has a normal electrophoresis and is a diagnosis of exclusion or gene testing. Do not give iron. The trap: coexisting iron deficiency lowers HbA2 and can hide beta-thalassemia trait, so repeat the electrophoresis after repletion.

8. **Oncology**. Chemotherapy toxicities, one line each. Anthracyclines: cumulative dose-dependent, largely irreversible cardiomyopathy; dexrazoxane is the cardioprotectant. Trastuzumab: LVEF decline that is not dose-dependent and usually reversible on withdrawal; monitor LVEF every 3 months. Bleomycin: pulmonary fibrosis, risk rises with cumulative dose and renal impairment. Cisplatin: nephrotoxicity, renal magnesium wasting, ototoxicity, peripheral neuropathy. Cyclophosphamide and ifosfamide: hemorrhagic cystitis prevented with mesna and hydration; ifosfamide also causes encephalopathy. Vincristine: neuropathy and ileus, and fatal if given intrathecally. Fluoropyrimidines: coronary vasospasm, hand-foot syndrome, and life-threatening toxicity in DPD deficiency.

9. **Nephrology**. Rhabdomyolysis: CK more than 5 times the upper limit of normal, and AKI risk climbs when CK exceeds about 5,000 U/L. The classic lab clue is a urine dipstick positive for blood with no red cells on microscopy (myoglobinuria). Treatment is early, aggressive isotonic crystalloid targeting high urine output; bicarbonate and mannitol have no proven benefit. Expect hyperkalemia, hyperphosphatemia, and early hypocalcemia, but do not correct asymptomatic hypocalcemia, because calcium deposited in injured muscle is released during recovery and produces rebound hypercalcemia. Watch the limbs for compartment syndrome.

10. **Neurology**. Thunderclap headache: noncontrast head CT within 6 hours of onset, on a modern scanner read by a radiologist, is essentially 100% sensitive for subarachnoid hemorrhage and a negative scan in that window ends the workup (Perry, BMJ 2011). Beyond 6 hours sensitivity falls, so a negative CT needs an LP for xanthochromia and red cells that do not clear. Once SAH is confirmed: CT angiography to find the aneurysm, secure it early (coiling beat clipping in ISAT for suitable aneurysms), and oral nimodipine 60 mg every 4 hours for 21 days, which improves outcomes by reducing delayed cerebral ischemia rather than angiographic vasospasm.
