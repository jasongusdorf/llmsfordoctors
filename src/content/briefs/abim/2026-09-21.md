1. **Cardiology**. In acute aortic syndrome, control impulse first with an intravenous beta-blocker before adding a vasodilator; vasodilation alone can provoke reflex tachycardia and increase aortic shear. Ascending aortic involvement generally requires emergency surgery, whereas uncomplicated descending dissection is initially managed medically. Persistent pain, malperfusion, rupture, expansion, or uncontrolled hypertension makes a descending dissection complicated and prompts urgent endovascular or surgical evaluation.

2. **Pulmonary medicine**. Obesity hypoventilation syndrome requires obesity, sleep-disordered breathing, and awake alveolar hypoventilation after excluding other causes such as obstructive lung disease, neuromuscular weakness, hypothyroidism, and sedative drugs. Obstructive sleep apnea alone does not cause persistent daytime hypercapnia. Positive-airway-pressure therapy and sustained weight loss treat the underlying disorder; oxygen alone may worsen carbon-dioxide retention and does not correct hypoventilation.

3. **Gastroenterology**. Microscopic colitis causes chronic watery, nonbloody diarrhea, often with urgency and nocturnal stools, despite a colon that appears normal or nearly normal endoscopically. Diagnosis therefore requires biopsies from multiple colonic segments; a visually normal colonoscopy does not exclude it. Review associated drugs such as NSAIDs, proton-pump inhibitors, and selective serotonin-reuptake inhibitors, and use oral budesonide for symptomatic active disease.

4. **Infectious disease**. Acute bacterial septic arthritis requires prompt arthrocentesis, blood cultures, empiric antibiotics after cultures when the patient is stable, and drainage of the infected joint. Neither a low synovial leukocyte count nor the presence of urate or calcium-pyrophosphate crystals reliably excludes infection. Gonococcal arthritis may have negative synovial cultures, so obtain appropriate mucosal nucleic-acid testing when the epidemiology fits.

5. **Rheumatology**. Scleroderma renal crisis presents with abrupt hypertension and acute kidney injury, often with microangiopathic hemolysis, in systemic sclerosis. Start an ACE inhibitor immediately and titrate aggressively; a rising creatinine after initiation is not by itself a reason to stop it. High-dose glucocorticoids increase risk, especially in early diffuse disease. Dialysis may be temporary because renal recovery can occur months later, so transplantation decisions should not be rushed.

6. **Endocrinology**. Thyroid storm is a clinical diagnosis of severe thyrotoxicosis with systemic decompensation—typically fever, marked tachycardia, central-nervous-system dysfunction, heart failure, or gastrointestinal and hepatic manifestations—and treatment should not wait for hormone levels. Give a beta-blocker when hemodynamically tolerated, a thionamide, iodine only after the thionamide, and glucocorticoid, while treating the precipitant. Avoid aspirin because it can increase free thyroid hormone.

7. **Hematology**. Disseminated intravascular coagulation consumes platelets and coagulation factors while generating fibrin, producing thrombocytopenia, prolonged clotting times, elevated fibrin-degradation products, and often low fibrinogen. The decisive treatment is control of the trigger, such as sepsis, trauma, malignancy, or obstetric catastrophe. Give platelets or plasma for clinically important bleeding or an urgent procedure, not merely to normalize abnormal laboratory values in a nonbleeding patient.

8. **Oncology**. Tumor lysis syndrome releases potassium, phosphate, and nucleic acids, causing hyperkalemia, hyperphosphatemia, secondary hypocalcemia, hyperuricemia, acute kidney injury, arrhythmia, and seizures. High-risk patients need vigorous hydration, close laboratory monitoring, and urate-lowering prophylaxis before cytotoxic therapy. Allopurinol prevents formation of new uric acid but does not remove existing uric acid; rasburicase lowers it rapidly but is contraindicated in glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase deficiency because it can cause hemolysis and methemoglobinemia.

9. **Nephrology**. Drug-induced acute interstitial nephritis commonly presents with otherwise unexplained acute kidney injury and sterile pyuria; fever, rash, and eosinophilia form the classic triad but are often absent. Urine eosinophils neither confirm nor exclude the diagnosis. Stop the suspected drug promptly, and use kidney biopsy when diagnostic uncertainty would change management; glucocorticoids are often considered when kidney function fails to improve after drug withdrawal.

10. **Neurology**. Guillain-Barré syndrome causes progressive, usually symmetric weakness with reduced reflexes and may produce respiratory failure and severe autonomic instability. Cerebrospinal fluid often shows high protein with few cells, but it can be normal early; a substantial pleocytosis should prompt another diagnosis. Treat patients who cannot walk independently or who are progressing with intravenous immunoglobulin or plasma exchange—these are similarly effective, combining them adds no benefit, and glucocorticoids are ineffective.
