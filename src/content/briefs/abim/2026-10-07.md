1. **Cardiology**. In cocaine-associated chest pain, treat the sympathomimetic state first: benzodiazepines for agitation, tachycardia, and hypertension, plus aspirin and nitroglycerin for ischemic symptoms. Guidelines advise against giving beta-blockers to a patient with signs of acute cocaine intoxication because of concern for unopposed alpha-adrenergic vasoconstriction, a point still debated. Persistent hypertension can be treated with phentolamine or a non-dihydropyridine calcium-channel blocker. Cocaine also causes aortic dissection and true myocardial infarction, so an ECG with ST elevation still warrants emergent reperfusion.

2. **Pulmonary medicine**. Löfgren syndrome is an acute presentation of sarcoidosis: bilateral hilar lymphadenopathy, erythema nodosum, fever, and arthritis or periarticular inflammation, classically at both ankles. When the picture is classic, the diagnosis is made clinically and tissue biopsy is usually unnecessary. Prognosis is excellent, with spontaneous remission in most patients; NSAIDs relieve the joint symptoms, and systemic glucocorticoids are reserved for refractory or organ-threatening disease.

3. **Gastroenterology**. Hepatic encephalopathy is a clinical diagnosis in cirrhosis. An elevated ammonia level does not establish it or guide therapy, but a normal ammonia in a confused patient should prompt a search for another cause. Always look for a precipitant: infection including spontaneous bacterial peritonitis, gastrointestinal bleeding, constipation, hypokalemia, volume depletion, or sedatives. Treat with lactulose titrated to two or three soft stools daily, and add rifaximin to lactulose for secondary prevention after a recurrence.

4. **Infectious disease**. In cryptococcal meningitis, measure opening pressure at the first lumbar puncture. Elevated intracranial pressure is a major driver of early death and is managed with repeated therapeutic lumbar punctures, not acetazolamide, mannitol, or glucocorticoids. Induction therapy is amphotericin B plus flucytosine. In a patient with HIV not yet on antiretroviral therapy, defer starting it for several weeks: the COAT trial showed higher mortality with early initiation, attributed to immune reconstitution inflammatory syndrome.

5. **Rheumatology**. Drug-induced lupus most often follows hydralazine, procainamide, isoniazid, minocycline, or a TNF inhibitor. It typically causes arthralgia, myalgia, fever, and serositis, while kidney and central-nervous-system involvement are rare. Anti-histone antibodies are common but not specific, complement is usually normal, and anti-double-stranded DNA is uncommon except with TNF-inhibitor-induced disease. Symptoms resolve over weeks to months after the drug is stopped, although antibodies can persist longer.

6. **Endocrinology**. Endogenous hyperinsulinemic hypoglycemia, from an insulinoma or from a sulfonylurea or meglitinide, shows elevated insulin, C-peptide, and proinsulin with a suppressed beta-hydroxybutyrate when blood is drawn during spontaneous or provoked hypoglycemia. Exogenous insulin produces high insulin with suppressed C-peptide. Because the biochemistry of an insulinoma and an insulin secretagogue is identical, a plasma screen for oral hypoglycemic agents is mandatory before localizing a tumor.

7. **Hematology**. Time inherited-thrombophilia testing carefully, if it is done at all. Acute thrombosis consumes antithrombin, protein C, and protein S; heparin lowers antithrombin activity; warfarin lowers protein C and S; and direct oral anticoagulants interfere with clot-based assays such as lupus anticoagulant. Testing in these settings produces false-positive deficiencies. Genetic tests for factor V Leiden and the prothrombin gene variant are unaffected. After a provoked venous thromboembolism, results rarely change the duration of anticoagulation.

8. **Oncology**. Carcinoid syndrome from a midgut neuroendocrine tumor (flushing, secretory diarrhea, wheezing) usually means hepatic metastases, because the liver otherwise inactivates the serotonin and other vasoactive amines released into the portal circulation. Bronchial and ovarian primaries drain systemically and are the exception. Screen with 24-hour urinary 5-hydroxyindoleacetic acid while avoiding serotonin-rich foods. Look for carcinoid heart disease, which affects right-sided valves (tricuspid regurgitation, pulmonic stenosis), and give a somatostatin analogue before surgery or procedures to prevent carcinoid crisis.

9. **Nephrology**. In metabolic alkalosis, urine chloride is more informative than urine sodium, because bicarbonaturia obligates sodium loss even in a volume-depleted patient. Low urine chloride indicates chloride-responsive alkalosis from vomiting, nasogastric suction, or remote diuretic use, which corrects with saline. High urine chloride indicates a chloride-resistant cause: with hypertension, mineralocorticoid excess; without hypertension, current diuretic use, Bartter or Gitelman syndrome, or severe hypokalemia.

10. **Neurology**. In restless legs syndrome, check iron studies in every patient, because brain iron deficiency drives the disorder even without anemia. The 2024 American Academy of Sleep Medicine guideline recommends iron supplementation when ferritin is 75 ng/mL or below or transferrin saturation is below 20%, thresholds higher than those used for anemia. The same guideline favors alpha-2-delta ligands such as gabapentin or pregabalin and makes a conditional recommendation against standard dopamine-agonist use, because augmentation (earlier, more intense, more widespread symptoms) worsens over time.
