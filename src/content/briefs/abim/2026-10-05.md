1. **Cardiology**. In hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, dynamic left-ventricular outflow obstruction becomes louder when ventricular volume falls, such as with standing or the Valsalva maneuver, and softer when preload or afterload rises, such as with squatting or sustained handgrip. Exertional syncope, a family history of premature sudden death, documented ventricular arrhythmia, marked hypertrophy, an apical aneurysm, and impaired systolic function are important sudden-death risk markers. An implantable cardioverter-defibrillator prevents sudden death in selected high-risk patients; septal reduction treats refractory obstructive symptoms but is not a substitute for risk-based defibrillator assessment.

2. **Pulmonary medicine**. Allergic bronchopulmonary aspergillosis should be suspected in a patient with asthma or cystic fibrosis who has recurrent exacerbations, fleeting pulmonary opacities, eosinophilia, markedly elevated total IgE, and Aspergillus sensitization. Central bronchiectasis and mucus plugging support the diagnosis. Treatment targets both the allergic inflammation and fungal burden: systemic glucocorticoids are the usual initial anti-inflammatory therapy, while an azole may be added to reduce exacerbations or glucocorticoid exposure; invasive antifungal therapy is not indicated unless invasive disease is actually present.

3. **Gastroenterology**. In suspected celiac disease, obtain tissue-transglutaminase IgA together with a total IgA level while the patient is still eating gluten. Selective IgA deficiency can make an IgA-based assay falsely negative, so use an IgG-based celiac test in that setting. A positive serology in an adult is generally confirmed with multiple duodenal biopsies before a gluten-free diet is started; symptom improvement on diet alone does not establish the diagnosis.

4. **Infectious disease**. Test for *Clostridioides difficile* only in a patient with otherwise unexplained new diarrhea, and do not use a test of cure: nucleic-acid tests can remain positive after recovery. A positive molecular assay without compatible symptoms may represent colonization rather than infection. Stop the inciting antibiotic when feasible, avoid unnecessary antimotility drugs in fulminant disease, and obtain urgent surgical consultation when toxic megacolon, perforation, shock, or progressive organ failure develops.

5. **Rheumatology**. New headache, scalp tenderness, jaw claudication, visual symptoms, or polymyalgia symptoms in an older adult should raise concern for giant-cell arteritis. Start high-dose glucocorticoid immediately when suspicion is substantial—especially with visual symptoms—and do not wait for temporal-artery biopsy or vascular imaging. Biopsy can remain informative after treatment begins; a negative unilateral biopsy does not fully exclude the disease because arterial inflammation can be segmental.

6. **Endocrinology**. Suspected adrenal crisis is treated immediately with intravenous hydrocortisone and rapid isotonic crystalloid; add dextrose when hypoglycemia is present. Draw cortisol and adrenocorticotropic hormone before treatment if this can be done without delay, but never postpone glucocorticoid administration for testing. Hypotension, abdominal symptoms, hyponatremia, hyperkalemia, and hypoglycemia support primary adrenal failure, whereas hyperkalemia is usually absent in isolated secondary adrenal insufficiency because mineralocorticoid secretion is preserved.

7. **Hematology**. Thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura is a medical emergency characterized by microangiopathic hemolytic anemia and thrombocytopenia, often with neurologic, renal, or cardiac injury; the classic five-part presentation is not required. Send an ADAMTS13 activity level before plasma therapy if possible, but do not wait for the result when clinical probability is high. Begin urgent plasma exchange plus glucocorticoid, and add caplacizumab when immune TTP is strongly suspected; platelet transfusion is generally avoided unless bleeding is life-threatening.

8. **Oncology**. Superior vena cava syndrome causes facial or upper-extremity swelling, venous distention, dyspnea, cough, and symptoms that may worsen when supine. Most stable patients should undergo tissue diagnosis before tumor-directed therapy because treatment depends on histology. Airway compromise, cerebral edema, or hemodynamic instability makes it an emergency requiring immediate supportive and specialist management; endovascular stenting often provides the fastest symptomatic relief, while chemotherapy or radiation treats the underlying malignancy.

9. **Nephrology**. In severe hyperkalemia with electrocardiographic changes, intravenous calcium stabilizes the cardiac membrane but does not lower serum potassium. Insulin with glucose and an inhaled beta agonist shift potassium into cells temporarily; remove potassium with a diuretic when appropriate, a gastrointestinal potassium binder, or dialysis. Recheck potassium because intracellular shifting wears off, and use urgent dialysis when hyperkalemia is refractory or accompanied by kidney failure that prevents adequate potassium elimination.

10. **Neurology**. Botulism causes acute bilateral cranial-nerve palsies followed by symmetric descending flaccid paralysis with preserved sensation; pupils may be fixed or poorly reactive, and respiratory failure can develop before limb weakness is profound. Alert public-health authorities and give botulinum antitoxin as soon as the diagnosis is suspected rather than waiting for confirmatory testing. Antitoxin prevents further nerve binding but does not reverse established paralysis, so serial respiratory assessment and early ventilatory support are essential.
