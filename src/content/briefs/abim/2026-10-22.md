1. **Cardiology**. Acute aortic dissection is the chest-pain diagnosis where reflex treatment kills. Thrombolysis or full anticoagulation for a presumed MI can be catastrophic, and a type A dissection that extends into the right coronary ostium can produce an inferior STEMI. Clues are abrupt tearing pain radiating to the back, a pulse or blood-pressure differential between arms, new aortic regurgitation, or a focal neurologic deficit. CT angiography is the test in a stable patient; bedside TEE suits an unstable one. Give an IV beta-blocker before any vasodilator, so a reflex tachycardia does not raise aortic wall stress, and lower systolic pressure toward 120 mm Hg if tolerated. Type A (ascending aorta) goes to emergency surgery. Uncomplicated type B is managed medically, and complicated type B (malperfusion, rupture, refractory pain or hypertension) gets endovascular repair.

2. **Pulmonary medicine**. GINA has not recommended SABA-only treatment for adults and adolescents with asthma since 2019, because a short-acting bronchodilator alone leaves airway inflammation untreated and raises the risk of severe exacerbations. The preferred reliever is as-needed low-dose ICS-formoterol: alone in mild asthma (SYGMA 1 and 2, Novel START), and as single maintenance-and-reliever therapy (SMART) at higher steps. SMART works only with formoterol, whose onset is fast; salmeterol cannot be used this way. The 2020 NAEPP update also endorses SMART for moderate persistent asthma. An as-needed albuterol-budesonide inhaler is FDA approved for adults (MANDALA). A LABA without an inhaled corticosteroid is never appropriate in asthma.

3. **Gastroenterology**. The full Charcot triad of fever, right-upper-quadrant pain, and jaundice is present in only about half of patients with acute cholangitis, so a cholestatic pattern with fever and a dilated bile duct is enough to act. Reynolds pentad adds hypotension and confusion and signals severe disease. Draw blood cultures, start broad antibiotics, and arrange biliary drainage, usually by ERCP, which is the definitive treatment. Severe cholangitis with organ dysfunction needs urgent drainage once resuscitated; ASGE 2021 suggests ERCP within 48 hours for the rest. Percutaneous transhepatic drainage is the fallback when ERCP fails or anatomy prevents it. After source control, a short antibiotic course of about 4 to 7 days is enough (Tokyo Guidelines 2018).

4. **Infectious disease**. Erythema migrans is a clinical diagnosis: an expanding red lesion, usually 5 cm or larger, in someone with possible *Ixodes* exposure in an endemic area. Do not send serology for it, because antibodies are often absent in the first weeks and a negative result should not delay treatment. The 2020 IDSA, AAN, and ACR guideline recommends doxycycline for 10 days, or amoxicillin or cefuroxime for 14 days. Single-dose doxycycline prophylaxis after a tick bite is reasonable only when all three apply: the tick is an identified *Ixodes* species, the area is highly endemic, and the tick was attached for 36 hours or more; give it within 72 hours of removal. An IgM immunoblot by itself should not be used to diagnose Lyme disease of more than about a month's duration, because false positives are common.

5. **Rheumatology**. Antiphospholipid syndrome requires persistent laboratory positivity, meaning lupus anticoagulant, anticardiolipin, or anti-β2-glycoprotein I confirmed on two occasions at least 12 weeks apart, because transient antibodies are common after infection. Lupus anticoagulant testing is unreliable on heparin, warfarin, or a DOAC; the solid-phase antibody assays are not affected. Warfarin with a target INR of 2 to 3 is standard after a first venous thrombosis. Avoid DOACs in triple-positive patients and in those with arterial events. TRAPS (2018) stopped early when rivaroxaban produced more thromboses than warfarin in triple-positive APS, mostly arterial events, and EULAR 2019 advises against DOACs in that group.

6. **Endocrinology**. Before calling hyperprolactinemia a prolactinoma, exclude pregnancy, primary hypothyroidism, kidney disease, and drugs, especially antipsychotics such as risperidone and dopamine antagonists such as metoclopramide. Consider macroprolactin in an asymptomatic patient. A large nonfunctioning pituitary mass usually raises prolactin only modestly, through stalk compression. A very large macroadenoma with an unexpectedly modest prolactin may be the hook effect, so ask the lab to repeat the assay on a diluted sample. Cabergoline is first-line for prolactinomas, including macroadenomas with visual field loss, since tumors usually shrink on medical therapy; surgery is for resistance or intolerance. The valvulopathy seen with cabergoline mainly occurred at the much higher doses used in Parkinson disease.

7. **Hematology**. MGUS means a serum M-protein under 3 g/dL, fewer than 10% clonal marrow plasma cells, and no myeloma-defining events such as CRAB features. The Mayo model uses three risk factors: non-IgG isotype, M-protein of 1.5 g/dL or more, and an abnormal serum free light-chain ratio. In the 2005 Mayo cohort, 20-year progression was about 5% with none of them and about 58% with all three. A low-risk patient does not need a bone marrow biopsy or skeletal imaging; repeat the electrophoresis at 6 months and then every 2 to 3 years if stable (IMWG 2010). IgM MGUS progresses toward Waldenström macroglobulinemia or lymphoma rather than myeloma.

8. **Oncology**. Most cancers of unknown primary carry a poor prognosis, so the tested skill is spotting the favorable subsets that are treated like a known cancer. A woman with adenocarcinoma confined to axillary nodes is treated as node-positive breast cancer. A woman with serous papillary peritoneal carcinomatosis is treated as ovarian cancer. A young man with a midline mediastinal or retroperitoneal mass needs hCG and AFP and is treated as an extragonadal germ cell tumor. Squamous carcinoma limited to cervical nodes is treated as head and neck cancer, and a solitary metastasis may be treated with local therapy. Immunohistochemistry on the biopsy, not a whole-body search, usually decides which subset applies.

9. **Nephrology**. The three renal tubular acidoses separate on potassium and urine pH. Type 1 (distal) cannot acidify the urine, so urine pH stays above 5.5 despite acidemia; it brings hypokalemia, hypercalciuria, hypocitraturia, and calcium phosphate stones or nephrocalcinosis, with Sjögren syndrome and amphotericin as classic causes. Type 2 (proximal) wastes bicarbonate, often as part of Fanconi syndrome: glycosuria with normal serum glucose, phosphaturia, and aminoaciduria. Causes include myeloma light chains, tenofovir, and ifosfamide, and it takes large alkali doses to correct. Type 4 is the hyperkalemic form from low aldosterone or aldosterone resistance: diabetic kidney disease with hyporeninemic hypoaldosteronism, or ACE inhibitors, ARBs, spironolactone, trimethoprim, calcineurin inhibitors, and heparin.

10. **Neurology**. Levodopa is the most effective symptomatic drug for Parkinson disease. Delaying it to "save" it is not supported, since motor fluctuations track disease duration more than drug exposure. Dopamine agonists cause impulse-control disorders, sleep attacks, edema, and hallucinations, particularly in older patients. For psychosis, first reduce anticholinergics, amantadine, and agonists. If an antipsychotic is still needed, use quetiapine, clozapine with ANC monitoring, or pimavanserin; never haloperidol. In hospital, avoid metoclopramide and prochlorperazine. Ondansetron is a safer antiemetic but is contraindicated with apomorphine. Do not interrupt levodopa abruptly, because withdrawal can cause a neuroleptic-malignant-like syndrome.
