1. **Cardiology**. Not all AF anticoagulation is DOAC territory. Mechanical valves (RE-ALIGN: dabigatran had more thromboembolism *and* bleeding) and moderate-to-severe rheumatic mitral stenosis (INVICTUS: rivaroxaban had higher mortality than VKA) require warfarin. Everything else, including bioprosthetic valves and repaired mitral valves beyond the early postoperative window, can take a DOAC.

2. **Pulmonary**. Emphysema that is basilar-predominant, or in a young patient/never-smoker, or paired with unexplained liver disease, should trigger alpha-1 antitrypsin testing: serum level *plus* phenotype/genotype (PI*ZZ is the classic severe deficiency). Usual COPD is apical. Guidelines support testing all symptomatic COPD at least once.

3. **Gastroenterology**. Clarithromycin triple therapy is now the wrong first answer for H. pylori in the US, resistance is too high. Bismuth quadruple therapy for 14 days is preferred, especially with any prior macrolide exposure. Always confirm eradication ≥4 weeks after therapy with urea breath or stool antigen, holding the PPI 1–2 weeks first or you get a false negative.

4. **Infectious disease**. Latent TB: use an IGRA rather than TST in BCG-vaccinated patients. Rifamycin-based short courses are now preferred over 9 months of isoniazid, weekly isoniazid + rifapentine for 12 doses (3HP) or daily rifampin for 4 months, because completion rates and hepatotoxicity are both better. Rule out active disease with symptoms and a CXR first.

5. **Rheumatology**. Sjögren disease is the connective tissue disease with a real lymphoma signal (MALT, roughly 5–10% lifetime). The features that should make you look: persistent unilateral parotid enlargement, palpable purpura, cryoglobulins, and a low C4. Anti-Ro/SSA is the key antibody; a negative ANA does not exclude it, and labial salivary gland biopsy settles seronegative cases.

6. **Endocrinology**. In very-high-risk osteoporosis (recent fracture, T-score well below −2.5, multiple fractures), start with an anabolic, teriparatide, abaloparatide, or romosozumab, then follow with an antiresorptive, because the gains are lost within a year if you stop and give nothing. Romosozumab carries a boxed warning for MI, stroke, and CV death: avoid it after a recent cardiovascular event.

7. **Hematology**. Warm vs cold autoimmune hemolysis changes the treatment entirely. Warm (IgG, spherocytes) responds to glucocorticoids, then rituximab or splenectomy. Cold agglutinin disease (IgM, C3-positive DAT, acrocyanosis, worse in the cold) does *not* respond to steroids, keep the patient warm, warm transfused blood, treat any underlying lymphoproliferative disorder; rituximab-based therapy is standard.

8. **Dermatology**. Bullous pemphigoid: elderly, *tense* bullae, intensely pruritic, mucosa usually spared, subepidermal split, anti-BP180/BP230. Pemphigus vulgaris: *flaccid* bullae and erosions, mucosal involvement is often the first sign, Nikolsky positive, intraepidermal acantholysis, anti-desmoglein 1/3, and it is the one that is life-threatening and needs systemic therapy including rituximab.

9. **Neurology**. Guillain-Barré is treated with IVIG *or* plasma exchange, not both, and never glucocorticoids, which are ineffective and possibly harmful (this is the opposite of CIDP, where steroids work). The tested management point is respiratory monitoring: serial FVC and negative inspiratory force, with intubation considered around FVC <20 mL/kg or NIF weaker than −30 cm H2O. Do not wait for hypoxemia or a rising PaCO2.

10. **Psychiatry**. Antidepressant monotherapy is the wrong answer in bipolar depression, it risks a manic switch and cycle acceleration. Use quetiapine, lurasidone, cariprazine, or lamotrigine (titrate lamotrigine slowly for SJS risk). On lithium, remember the interactions that precipitate toxicity: NSAIDs, thiazides, ACE inhibitors/ARBs, and volume depletion; monitor level, creatinine, calcium, and TSH.
