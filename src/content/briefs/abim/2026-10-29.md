1. **Cardiology**. Most muscle symptoms blamed on statins are not caused by the statin. In the SAMSON n-of-1 trial (NEJM 2020), about 90% of the symptom burden reported on atorvastatin also appeared on placebo, and StatinWISE (BMJ 2021) likewise found no difference between statin and placebo months. So before calling a patient statin-intolerant, check the CK and TSH, stop the statin until symptoms resolve, then rechallenge with a different statin, a lower dose, or intermittent dosing; most patients tolerate one of these. A CK that stays high with progressive proximal weakness after the statin is stopped is a different disease (anti-HMGCR myopathy) and needs a different workup. For true intolerance, bempedoic acid is the outcome-proven option: in CLEAR Outcomes (NEJM 2023) it lowered LDL by about 21% relative to placebo and cut major adverse cardiovascular events (HR 0.87). It does not cause myalgia because it is inactive in skeletal muscle, but it raises uric acid (more gout), increases cholelithiasis, and carries a tendon-rupture warning.

2. **Pulmonary medicine**. Separate aspiration pneumonitis from aspiration pneumonia. Pneumonitis is chemical injury from gastric contents, usually after witnessed aspiration in a patient with depressed consciousness (overdose, seizure, anaesthesia). Hypoxaemia and infiltrates come on within hours and often improve within a day or two, and prophylactic antibiotics and corticosteroids are not recommended; give antibiotics only if the picture fails to resolve or a pneumonia develops. Aspiration pneumonia is an infection in a patient with dysphagia or poor dentition and a more indolent course. Infiltrates go to the dependent segments: the posterior upper lobes and superior lower lobes if the patient was supine, the basal segments if upright, the right more often than the left. The 2019 ATS/IDSA community-acquired pneumonia guideline advises against routinely adding anaerobic coverage for suspected aspiration pneumonia unless there is a lung abscess or empyema.

3. **Gastroenterology**. Ischaemic (hypoxic) hepatitis produces the steepest aminotransferase rise in medicine: often into the thousands within one to three days of a low-flow event, then a fast fall, often by half within about 72 hours, once perfusion recovers. LDH climbs early and very high, so an ALT-to-LDH ratio below about 1.5 favours ischaemic or toxic injury over viral hepatitis. Bilirubin rises late and modestly. Low blood pressure is often never recorded, especially when the setting is right-sided congestion (heart failure, tricuspid regurgitation, pulmonary hypertension) plus a second hit such as hypoxaemia or sepsis, so do not rule it out because the chart shows no hypotension. Acetaminophen also produces a very high LDH and must be excluded. There is no liver-specific therapy: treat the cardiac or circulatory cause. Prognosis depends on that underlying illness, not on the peak ALT.

4. **Infectious disease**. Lemierre syndrome is septic thrombophlebitis of the internal jugular vein, usually caused by *Fusobacterium necrophorum*, in an adolescent or young adult. The sequence is the clue: a sore throat that seems to be getting better, then within about a week rigors, unilateral neck pain or swelling along the sternocleidomastoid, and septic emboli, most often to the lungs as multiple nodular or cavitating lesions, sometimes to joints. Get anaerobic blood cultures and a contrast CT of the neck to show the jugular thrombus. Treat with prolonged intravenous therapy active against anaerobes and beta-lactamase producers: a beta-lactam/beta-lactamase inhibitor, a carbapenem, or metronidazole with ceftriaxone. Penicillin alone is not reliable because some strains produce beta-lactamase. Drain any abscesses. Anticoagulation is contested: there are no randomised trials, and it is usually reserved for clot extension or failure to improve.

5. **Rheumatology**. Diffuse idiopathic skeletal hyperostosis (DISH) is the mimic of ankylosing spondylitis in an older, often obese or diabetic man with a stiff back. The Resnick criteria are flowing ossification along the anterolateral aspect of at least four contiguous vertebral bodies, typically on the right side of the thoracic spine, with disc heights relatively preserved and no sacroiliac erosion, sclerosis, or fusion and no facet ankylosis. Inflammatory markers are normal, there is no HLA-B27 link, and the stiffness is not inflammatory, so TNF inhibitors have no role. Large anterior cervical osteophytes can cause dysphagia. In both DISH and ankylosing spondylitis the fused spine fractures after trivial falls, and plain films miss these fractures. Get a CT, or an MRI if there are neurologic signs, after even minor trauma.

6. **Endocrinology**. Bisphosphonates stay in bone, so a drug holiday is an option, unlike with denosumab. Per the 2016 ASBMR task force, reassess after about 5 years of oral therapy or 3 years of intravenous zoledronic acid. A patient who remains at high risk (femoral neck T-score still at or below −2.5, or a fracture before or during therapy) continues, up to about 10 years oral or 6 years intravenous. Others can stop for 2 to 3 years, with fracture-risk reassessment during the holiday. Long use raises the risk of atypical femoral fracture, which is rare in absolute terms. Prodromal thigh or groin pain in a patient on a bisphosphonate calls for femoral imaging. These fractures are transverse, start at the lateral cortex of the shaft, and are often bilateral, so image both femurs. The risk is higher in Asian women and with glucocorticoid use.

7. **Hematology**. Duffy-null-associated neutrophil count (formerly "benign ethnic neutropenia") explains many chronically low ANCs in people of African or Middle Eastern ancestry; about two-thirds of Black Americans are Duffy-null. The *ACKR1* Duffy-null genotype, which also protects against *Plasmodium vivax*, shifts the circulating neutrophil count down, often to roughly 1,000 to 1,500/µL, while marrow reserve and response to infection are normal. These patients have no excess infection risk. The clues are a stable, lifelong count, no recurrent infections, otherwise normal blood counts, and a normal examination; red-cell Duffy phenotyping, Fy(a−b−), confirms it. Recognising it avoids needless bone-marrow biopsies, withheld or delayed chemotherapy, and exclusion from clinical trials. An ANC below 500, falling counts, other cytopenias, or infections are not explained by Duffy-null status and need a full workup.

8. **Oncology**. For a symptomatic malignant pleural effusion, the first step is a large-volume therapeutic thoracentesis. It shows whether dyspnoea improves and whether the lung re-expands. Asymptomatic effusions do not need drainage. If the effusion recurs and the lung is expandable, the 2018 ATS/STS/STR guideline accepts either an indwelling pleural catheter or talc pleurodesis as first line. TIME2 (JAMA 2012) found no difference in dyspnoea, and AMPLE (JAMA 2017) found fewer hospital days with the catheter. If the lung is non-expandable (trapped by a visceral peel or endobronchial obstruction), pleurodesis cannot work because the pleural surfaces cannot meet, so place an indwelling catheter. Avoid serial thoracenteses as a long-term plan.

9. **Nephrology**. Nephrogenic systemic fibrosis, a progressive and untreatable fibrosis of skin, joints, and sometimes internal organs, was linked to the older group I linear gadolinium agents (gadodiamide, gadopentetate, gadoversetamide) in patients with an eGFR below 30, on dialysis, or with AKI. With the group II agents now standard, including macrocyclics such as gadobutrol, gadoterate, and gadoteridol and the linear agent gadobenate, the risk is so low that the 2020 ACR–National Kidney Foundation consensus says a needed contrast MRI should not be withheld or delayed because of kidney function. Under that consensus, eGFR screening before group II agents is optional, and starting dialysis to clear gadolinium is not recommended for patients not already on it. Group I agents remain contraindicated in high-risk kidney disease.

10. **Neurology**. In carpal tunnel syndrome, numbness affects the thumb, index, middle, and radial half of the ring finger, is worse at night, and is relieved by shaking the hand. The palm over the thenar eminence is spared because the palmar cutaneous branch of the median nerve leaves above the wrist. Numbness of the thenar palm points to a lesion more proximal, at the forearm or C6. Weakness of thumb abduction and thenar atrophy are late signs of axonal loss. Tinel and Phalen signs are of modest accuracy, and nerve conduction studies confirm the diagnosis and grade it. Look for an association: pregnancy, hypothyroidism, diabetes, rheumatoid arthritis, acromegaly, and transthyretin amyloidosis, in which bilateral carpal tunnel syndrome can precede the cardiomyopathy by years. Treat with a neutral-position night splint or a corticosteroid injection. Surgical release is for severe or axonal disease and for failed conservative care.
