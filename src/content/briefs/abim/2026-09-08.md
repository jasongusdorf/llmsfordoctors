1. **Cardiology**. Pre-excited atrial fibrillation is an irregular wide-complex tachycardia in a patient with an accessory pathway. If unstable, cardiovert. If stable, use IV procainamide or ibutilide. Do not give AV-nodal blockers — adenosine, beta-blockers, diltiazem/verapamil, or digoxin — because blocking the AV node can drive more conduction down the accessory pathway and precipitate ventricular fibrillation. That prohibition is specific to pre-excited AF; adenosine remains appropriate for a regular narrow-complex AV re-entrant tachycardia.

2. **Pulmonary**. Chronic thromboembolic pulmonary hypertension is the pulmonary hypertension that may be surgically curable. A normal ventilation-perfusion scan effectively excludes it; CT pulmonary angiography alone can miss chronic distal disease, so a V/Q scan is the screening test even when the original pulmonary embolus was diagnosed by CT. Refer every confirmed case to an expert CTEPH team for operability assessment. Pulmonary endarterectomy is first-line for surgically accessible disease; balloon pulmonary angioplasty and riociguat are options for inoperable or persistent disease. Lifelong anticoagulation is required.

3. **Gastroenterology**. Autoimmune hepatitis is a hepatocellular process: elevated aminotransferases and IgG, usually with ANA and/or anti-smooth-muscle antibody, and interface hepatitis with plasma cells on biopsy. Autoantibodies support the diagnosis but are neither perfectly sensitive nor specific, so exclude viral hepatitis and drug-induced liver injury and confirm with biopsy. Induction is glucocorticoid with or without azathioprine; check TPMT activity before azathioprine. Budesonide may reduce systemic steroid toxicity but must not be used in cirrhosis, where portosystemic shunting defeats its first-pass metabolism.

4. **Infectious disease**. Strongyloides can autoinfect for decades, then become fulminant when corticosteroids suppress host control. Before prolonged steroids, transplantation, or other major immunosuppression, screen patients with residence or substantial exposure in an endemic region; serology is more sensitive than a single stool examination. Hyperinfection causes pulmonary and gastrointestinal disease and can carry enteric gram-negative organisms into the bloodstream; eosinophilia may disappear rather than rise. Ivermectin is first-line, and in hyperinfection it is continued daily until stool or sputum remains negative for two weeks.

5. **Rheumatology**. Relapsing polychondritis attacks cartilage but spares the noncartilaginous ear lobe: a red, exquisitely tender pinna with a normal lobule is the board clue. Recurrent inflammation can produce cauliflower ears, saddle-nose deformity, inflammatory eye disease, audiovestibular symptoms, and nonerosive arthritis. Hoarseness, stridor, or wheeze is an emergency signal for laryngotracheobronchial chondritis and dynamic airway collapse; obtain inspiratory and expiratory airway imaging and pulmonary flow-volume loops. Mild auricular disease may respond to NSAIDs or colchicine, but airway or other organ-threatening disease requires systemic glucocorticoids plus immunosuppression.

6. **Endocrinology**. Primary aldosteronism is more common than the old “hypertension plus hypokalemia” stereotype; most affected patients are normokalemic. The 2025 Endocrine Society guideline now suggests screening all patients with hypertension using aldosterone and renin, with potassium measured to interpret aldosterone accurately. A suppressed renin with inappropriately high aldosterone is the signature. After biochemical confirmation, adrenal CT excludes a large mass, but CT cannot reliably determine unilateral versus bilateral secretion; a surgical candidate generally needs adrenal-vein sampling. Unilateral disease gets adrenalectomy, while bilateral disease gets a mineralocorticoid-receptor antagonist.

7. **Hematology**. Suspected heparin-induced thrombocytopenia starts with the 4Ts score, not a PF4 assay. A low score effectively excludes HIT and should prevent unnecessary testing and alternative anticoagulation. With intermediate or high probability, stop every source of heparin — including flushes — send an immunoassay with functional confirmation when indicated, and start a non-heparin anticoagulant while awaiting results. HIT is thrombotic despite the low platelet count, so isolated HIT still requires anticoagulation. Do not start warfarin until platelet recovery because early protein C depletion can cause venous limb gangrene; if warfarin was already given, reverse it with vitamin K.

8. **Oncology**. New focal back pain in a patient with cancer is metastatic spinal cord compression until proved otherwise, especially when pain is worse supine, with cough or Valsalva, or progresses to weakness, sensory loss, or sphincter dysfunction. Give dexamethasone promptly when neurologic signs are present and obtain urgent MRI of the entire spine, because clinically silent noncontiguous lesions are common. Do not wait for a plain radiograph. Definitive therapy is urgent radiation or surgical decompression plus stabilization; surgery is favored for mechanical instability, radioresistant tumor, uncertain diagnosis requiring tissue, or a fit patient with a single compressive site.

9. **Nephrology**. Cholesterol atheroembolic kidney disease usually appears days to weeks after angiography, vascular surgery, thrombolysis, or anticoagulation — later than contrast-associated acute kidney injury. The discriminating combination is subacute kidney failure plus livedo reticularis or painful blue toes with intact pulses; eosinophilia and low complement may accompany it. Urinalysis is usually bland or only mildly active. Kidney or skin biopsy shows cholesterol clefts when confirmation is necessary. There is no embolus to dissolve: treatment is supportive vascular-risk reduction and statin therapy, and further arterial instrumentation should be avoided when possible.

10. **Neurology**. Myasthenic crisis is respiratory or bulbar failure from myasthenia gravis, often triggered by infection, surgery, or a medication. Follow forced vital capacity and negative inspiratory force serially; pulse oximetry and arterial blood gas abnormalities appear late. Intubate electively for deteriorating mechanics, weak cough, or inability to handle secretions rather than waiting for hypoxemia. Treat with either IVIG or plasma exchange plus ventilatory support, and address the trigger. Magnesium, aminoglycosides, fluoroquinolones, macrolides, beta-blockers, and neuromuscular blockers can worsen weakness; IV magnesium is particularly dangerous because it impairs acetylcholine release.
