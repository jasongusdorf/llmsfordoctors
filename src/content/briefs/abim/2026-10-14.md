1. **Cardiology**. Digoxin toxicity shows up as nausea, anorexia, confusion, and visual change (blurring or yellow-green halos), with arrhythmias that pair increased automaticity and depressed conduction: frequent ventricular ectopy, atrial tachycardia with AV block, accelerated junctional rhythm, and the near-pathognomonic bidirectional ventricular tachycardia. Chronic toxicity is usually precipitated by declining kidney function, hypokalemia, hypomagnesemia, or an interacting drug such as amiodarone, verapamil, or clarithromycin. In acute overdose, hyperkalemia reflects Na-K-ATPase poisoning and is a marker of severity. Treat life-threatening toxicity with digoxin-specific antibody fragments; IV calcium for the hyperkalemia, once taught as dangerous, is now contested rather than forbidden. After Fab is given, total serum digoxin rises and no longer reflects free drug, so do not chase the level.

2. **Pulmonary medicine**. Test every patient with COPD once for alpha-1 antitrypsin deficiency (serum level plus phenotype or genotype), not only the young or the never-smoker; it is badly underdiagnosed. Clues are emphysema at a young age, a basilar panlobular pattern, unexplained liver disease, necrotizing panniculitis, and c-ANCA vasculitis. The PiZZ genotype causes lung disease through loss of protease protection and liver disease through retention of misfolded protein in hepatocytes. That split matters for treatment: IV augmentation therapy is used for severe deficiency with emphysema but does nothing for the liver. Smoking cessation is the most important intervention, and first-degree relatives should be offered testing.

3. **Gastroenterology**. Consider Wilson disease in any young patient with acute liver failure, especially with Coombs-negative hemolytic anemia. The biochemical tells are a strikingly low alkaline phosphatase and an AST that exceeds ALT; an alkaline phosphatase-to-total bilirubin ratio below 4 together with an AST-to-ALT ratio above 2.2 strongly favors Wilson disease in this setting. Do not rely on ceruloplasmin here: it can be normal in Wilson disease because it is an acute-phase reactant, and it can be low in acute liver failure of any cause. Fulminant Wilson disease is generally an indication for urgent transplant referral, because chelation alone rarely rescues it.

4. **Infectious disease**. Screen for latent tuberculosis with an interferon-gamma release assay or tuberculin skin test before starting a TNF inhibitor or similar biologic, and exclude active disease with symptoms and a chest radiograph before treating. Current CDC-preferred latent TB regimens are short and rifamycin-based: 12 weekly doses of isoniazid plus rifapentine (3HP), 4 months of daily rifampin (4R), or 3 months of daily isoniazid plus rifampin (3HR); 6 to 9 months of isoniazid is now an alternative. The catch with rifamycins is drug interactions: they induce metabolism of warfarin, direct oral anticoagulants, hormonal contraceptives, many antiretrovirals, and transplant immunosuppressants, so reconcile the medication list first.

5. **Rheumatology**. Adult-onset Still disease is a diagnosis of exclusion built on a recognisable pattern: a quotidian spiking fever, an evanescent salmon-pink rash that appears with the fever, arthralgia or arthritis, sore throat, and neutrophilic leukocytosis, with negative ANA and rheumatoid factor. Ferritin is often markedly elevated, and a low glycosylated-ferritin fraction adds specificity. First exclude infection, lymphoma, and other vasculitides. The dangerous complication is macrophage activation syndrome, a secondary hemophagocytic syndrome, so watch for falling counts, falling ESR, and rising triglycerides. Treatment starts with glucocorticoids, with IL-1 or IL-6 blockade for refractory or steroid-dependent disease.

6. **Endocrinology**. In asymptomatic primary hyperparathyroidism, parathyroidectomy is recommended (2022 international workshop) for any of: serum calcium more than 1 mg/dL above the upper limit of normal; osteoporosis by T-score of -2.5 or lower or a vertebral fracture; eGFR below 60 mL/min; kidney stones, nephrocalcinosis, or marked hypercalciuria; or age under 50. Surgery is also reasonable in anyone who chooses it. Before operating, exclude familial hypocalciuric hypercalcemia with a calcium-to-creatinine clearance ratio; a value below 0.01 points to FHH, which does not need surgery. Ensure vitamin D sufficiency, since deficiency can raise PTH and confuse the picture.

7. **Hematology**. Paroxysmal nocturnal hemoglobinuria is an acquired clonal stem-cell disorder in which loss of GPI-anchored complement regulators (CD55, CD59) leaves red cells exposed to complement. Suspect it with Coombs-negative intravascular hemolysis (high LDH, low haptoglobin, hemoglobinuria), thrombosis in unusual sites such as the hepatic, portal, mesenteric, or cerebral veins, or cytopenias, particularly alongside aplastic anemia. Diagnose by peripheral-blood flow cytometry, not the obsolete Ham test. Thrombosis is the leading cause of death. Complement C5 inhibitors (eculizumab, ravulizumab) control intravascular hemolysis but greatly increase the risk of *Neisseria* infection, so meningococcal vaccination is required before starting.

8. **Oncology**. USPSTF (2021) recommends annual low-dose CT lung cancer screening for adults aged 50 to 80 with at least a 20 pack-year history who currently smoke or quit within the past 15 years. The American Cancer Society (2023) dropped the years-since-quitting criterion, so former smokers who quit long ago are eligible under its guideline but not under USPSTF; guidelines differ here. Screening should be paired with smoking-cessation support and shared decision-making about false positives and overdiagnosis, and it stops once a person develops a health problem that substantially limits life expectancy or the ability or willingness to undergo curative lung surgery.

9. **Nephrology**. Hyperglycemia lowers measured serum sodium by pulling water out of cells, so the hyponatremia is hypertonic, not a free-water problem. The classic correction adds 1.6 mEq/L to the sodium for every 100 mg/dL of glucose above 100; some data support about 2.4 mEq/L, especially at higher glucose levels, so the factor is debated. The practical point in DKA and the hyperosmolar state is that a "normal" measured sodium in a very hyperglycemic patient implies a high corrected sodium and a substantial free-water deficit, and the measured sodium should rise as glucose falls. A measured sodium that fails to rise during treatment is a warning sign for overly rapid fall in osmolality.

10. **Neurology**. Idiopathic intracranial hypertension classically affects women of reproductive age with obesity or recent weight gain and causes headache, pulsatile tinnitus, transient visual obscurations, and papilledema, sometimes with a sixth-nerve palsy. Diagnosis requires normal brain imaging and normal CSF composition with an elevated opening pressure (at least 25 cm H2O in adults, measured lateral decubitus). MRI with MR venography is mandatory, because cerebral venous sinus thrombosis mimics it exactly. The disabling outcome is vision loss, not the headache, so formal visual fields guide urgency. Treat with weight loss and acetazolamide; rapidly progressive visual loss needs optic nerve sheath fenestration, CSF shunting, or venous sinus stenting.
