1. **Cardiology**. The ankle-brachial index for each leg is the higher of that leg's dorsalis pedis and posterior tibial pressures divided by the higher of the two brachial pressures. A resting ABI of 0.90 or less diagnoses peripheral artery disease, 0.91 to 0.99 is borderline, and 1.00 to 1.40 is normal. Above 1.40 the vessels are noncompressible, common in diabetes and advanced CKD, so the number is uninterpretable; get a toe-brachial index, where 0.70 or less is abnormal. Classic claudication with a normal resting ABI calls for an exercise ABI. Every patient with symptomatic PAD gets a high-intensity statin and a single antiplatelet agent (aspirin or clopidogrel), and supervised exercise therapy is first-line for claudication. Cilostazol improves walking distance but is contraindicated in heart failure of any severity. In patients at low bleeding risk, adding rivaroxaban 2.5 mg twice daily to aspirin reduced major cardiovascular and limb events (COMPASS, and VOYAGER PAD after revascularization). Revascularization for claudication is reserved for lifestyle-limiting symptoms despite medical therapy and exercise.

2. **Pulmonary medicine**. High-altitude illness comes in three forms. Acute mountain sickness is headache with nausea, fatigue, or dizziness within hours of ascent, usually above about 2,500 m. High-altitude cerebral edema is mountain sickness plus ataxia or altered mental status. High-altitude pulmonary edema is a noncardiogenic edema from exaggerated hypoxic pulmonary vasoconstriction; it typically starts two to four days after arrival with exertional dyspnea that progresses to dyspnea at rest and cough, and it can occur without preceding mountain sickness. Prevention is gradual ascent: above 3,000 m, raise sleeping elevation by no more than about 500 m a day, with a rest day every three to four days. Acetazolamide 125 mg twice daily, started the day before ascent, is the standard prophylaxis for moderate- or high-risk trips, with dexamethasone as the alternative. Descent is the definitive treatment for all three. Dexamethasone treats cerebral edema but not pulmonary edema; for pulmonary edema give oxygen and descend, and use nifedipine when neither is available.

3. **Gastroenterology**. Gastroparesis is delayed gastric emptying without mechanical obstruction, so do an upper endoscopy first to exclude obstruction. The confirmatory test is a 4-hour solid-meal gastric emptying scintigraphy; more than 10% retention at 4 hours is delayed, and shorter protocols misclassify patients. Stop opioids and GLP-1 receptor agonists before testing because both slow emptying, and test with glucose controlled, since acute hyperglycemia slows it too. Treatment starts with small, low-fat, low-fiber meals and glycemic control. Metoclopramide is the only FDA-approved drug and carries a boxed warning for tardive dyskinesia; avoid use beyond 12 weeks. Erythromycin works acutely but loses effect within weeks (tachyphylaxis) and prolongs the QT interval. In a young patient with cyclic vomiting relieved by hot showers, ask about cannabis before labelling it gastroparesis.

4. **Infectious disease**. Tetanus prophylaxis in wound care turns on two questions: is the wound clean and minor, and how many tetanus toxoid doses has the patient had? For a clean, minor wound, give Tdap or Td if the last dose was 10 or more years ago or the patient has had fewer than three doses or an unknown history; tetanus immune globulin is not needed. For any other wound (contaminated with dirt, feces, or saliva, or a puncture, avulsion, crush injury, burn, or frostbite), give Tdap or Td if the last dose was 5 or more years ago, and add tetanus immune globulin 250 units IM if the patient has had fewer than three doses or the history is unknown. Use Tdap if the patient has never had it as an adult. Clinical tetanus presents with trismus, risus sardonicus, and painful generalized spasms with a clear sensorium. Treat with human tetanus immune globulin, wound debridement, metronidazole, and benzodiazepines, and start the full vaccine series, because the disease does not confer immunity.

5. **Rheumatology**. Familial Mediterranean fever is an autosomal recessive autoinflammatory disease caused by *MEFV* mutations. It causes self-limited attacks of fever lasting 12 to 72 hours with serositis: peritonitis that mimics an acute abdomen, pleuritis, or an acute monoarthritis of a large joint. An erysipelas-like erythema over the ankle or dorsum of the foot is characteristic. Patients are well between attacks, and CRP is high during them. Onset is usually in childhood or adolescence, in people of Eastern Mediterranean descent: Armenian, Turkish, Arab, and Jewish (especially Sephardi and Mizrahi) populations. Many have had a negative appendectomy or laparotomy. Daily lifelong colchicine prevents attacks and, more importantly, AA amyloidosis, the main cause of death, which presents as nephrotic-range proteinuria; check the urine for protein periodically. Patients resistant to colchicine get IL-1 blockade; canakinumab is FDA-approved for this, and anakinra is also used.

6. **Endocrinology**. Sheehan syndrome is postpartum pituitary infarction after severe hemorrhage and hypotension; the pituitary enlarges in pregnancy and outgrows its blood supply. It can present acutely with hypotension, hypoglycemia, and hyponatremia, but more often it is recognized late. The earliest clue is failure to lactate (prolactin deficiency), followed by failure of menses to return, then fatigue, cold intolerance, and loss of axillary and pubic hair, sometimes diagnosed years after the delivery. The hyponatremia comes from cortisol deficiency and mimics SIADH. Central hypothyroidism makes TSH low or inappropriately normal, so diagnose and titrate levothyroxine by free T4, not TSH. Confirm adrenal insufficiency and start hydrocortisone before levothyroxine. Fludrocortisone is usually not needed, because aldosterone is regulated by the renin-angiotensin system and stays intact in secondary adrenal insufficiency. Late MRI often shows an empty sella.

7. **Hematology**. Chronic lymphocytic leukemia requires at least 5,000 clonal B cells per microliter in the blood, sustained for at least three months. Flow cytometry shows CD5 and CD23 positivity with dim surface immunoglobulin and dim CD20, and the smear shows smudge cells. Fewer than 5,000 clonal cells without lymphadenopathy, organomegaly, or cytopenias is monoclonal B-cell lymphocytosis. A CD5-positive, CD23-negative clone suggests mantle cell lymphoma; check cyclin D1. The lymphocyte count alone is never the indication to treat. Early asymptomatic disease is observed, and therapy starts only for active disease under iwCLL criteria: progressive marrow failure (hemoglobin under 10 g/dL or platelets under 100,000/µL), massive or progressive splenomegaly or lymphadenopathy, a lymphocyte doubling time under six months, constitutional symptoms, or autoimmune cytopenias that do not respond to steroids. Watch for autoimmune hemolytic anemia and ITP, infections from hypogammaglobulinemia, and Richter transformation, which presents with rapid growth of one node, a rising LDH, and B symptoms and needs a biopsy. Check *TP53* status before every line of therapy.

8. **Oncology**. Androgen deprivation therapy for prostate cancer has predictable toxicities: hot flashes, loss of bone density with more fractures, sarcopenia, weight gain, insulin resistance, and new diabetes. Patients on long-term ADT need a baseline fracture risk assessment with DXA, calcium and vitamin D, and a bisphosphonate or denosumab when fracture risk is high. GnRH agonists such as leuprolide cause a testosterone surge in the first weeks. In a patient with spinal metastases or impending urinary obstruction, that flare can precipitate cord compression, so add an androgen receptor blocker such as bicalutamide for the first weeks, or use a GnRH antagonist (degarelix or oral relugolix), which causes no surge. Abiraterone blocks CYP17 and causes mineralocorticoid excess (hypertension, hypokalemia, edema), so it is always given with prednisone.

9. **Nephrology**. Hypernatremia is a water problem. It almost always means water loss in someone who cannot drink: an older adult with dementia, an intubated patient, a nursing home resident. Urine osmolality sorts the cause. Maximally concentrated urine (above about 600 mOsm/kg) means extrarenal loss or inadequate intake with the kidney working normally. Urine more dilute than plasma in a hypernatremic patient means diabetes insipidus, now renamed arginine vasopressin deficiency (central) or resistance (nephrogenic). If urine osmolality rises substantially after desmopressin, the cause is central; little or no rise means nephrogenic. Intermediate values suggest an osmotic diuresis from glucose, urea (high-protein tube feeds, resolving AKI), or mannitol. Free water deficit = total body water × (serum Na/140 − 1). Restore volume with isotonic fluid first in a hypovolemic patient, then give water enterally or as D5W. Acute hypernatremia under 48 hours can be corrected quickly. For chronic hypernatremia, the traditional limit is about 10 to 12 mEq/L per day, but this is contested: observational adult data have not shown harm from faster correction.

10. **Neurology**. Creutzfeldt-Jakob disease is a rapidly progressive dementia over weeks to months, with myoclonus (often startle-provoked), ataxia, visual disturbance, and akinetic mutism at the end. About 85% of cases are sporadic, and it is fatal, usually within a year. The most useful tests are MRI with diffusion-weighted and FLAIR sequences, showing cortical ribboning and restricted diffusion in the caudate and putamen, and CSF RT-QuIC, which is highly specific. Periodic sharp-wave complexes on EEG are characteristic but often absent early. CSF 14-3-3 and total tau are nonspecific markers of rapid neuronal injury. The task on the exam and at the bedside is to exclude treatable mimics first: autoimmune encephalitis (anti-LGI1 with faciobrachial dystonic seizures and hyponatremia, anti-NMDA receptor), steroid-responsive encephalopathy with autoimmune thyroiditis, CNS lymphoma, Wernicke encephalopathy, nonconvulsive status epilepticus, and toxic-metabolic causes. Variant CJD affects younger patients, begins with psychiatric and painful sensory symptoms, and shows the pulvinar sign on MRI. Notify public health, and follow prion-specific handling of neurosurgical instruments, because standard sterilization does not inactivate prions.
