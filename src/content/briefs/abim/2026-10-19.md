1. **Cardiology**. Acute pericarditis is diagnosed from pleuritic, positional chest pain (better sitting forward), a friction rub, diffuse concave ST elevation with PR depression, or a new effusion. First-line treatment is a high-dose NSAID or aspirin plus colchicine, which roughly halves the recurrence rate (COPE, 2005; ICAP, 2013) and is continued for about three months. Glucocorticoids are second-line because early use is associated with more recurrences; reserve them for contraindications to NSAIDs or specific indications such as autoimmune disease or pregnancy. High-risk features that warrant admission and a search for a specific cause include fever, subacute onset, a large effusion or tamponade, immunosuppression, anticoagulation, trauma, and failure to respond to an NSAID within about a week. A raised troponin means myopericarditis. For recurrent, colchicine-resistant, steroid-dependent disease, interleukin-1 blockade (rilonacept, RHAPSODY 2021) is an option.

2. **Pulmonary medicine**. The Fleischner Society (2017) guidelines for an incidentally found solid pulmonary nodule apply to adults 35 and older who are not immunocompromised, have no known cancer, and are not in a lung cancer screening program, which uses Lung-RADS instead. For a single solid nodule smaller than 6 mm, a low-risk patient needs no routine follow-up and a high-risk patient may have an optional CT at 12 months. A 6 to 8 mm nodule gets CT at 6 to 12 months. A nodule larger than 8 mm calls for CT at about 3 months, PET-CT, or tissue sampling. Subsolid nodules follow a separate, longer schedule because they grow slowly.

3. **Gastroenterology**. Suspect primary biliary cholangitis in a middle-aged woman with fatigue, pruritus, and a cholestatic pattern (raised alkaline phosphatase). A positive antimitochondrial antibody with an elevated alkaline phosphatase establishes the diagnosis without biopsy. Treat with ursodeoxycholic acid at 13 to 15 mg/kg/day and assess biochemical response after about a year; an inadequate response predicts progression and prompts second-line therapy, where the PPAR agonists elafibranor and seladelpar received accelerated FDA approval in 2024. Look for associated Sjögren syndrome, autoimmune thyroid disease, osteoporosis, and fat-soluble vitamin deficiency. Cholestyramine is the usual first agent for pruritus, separated in time from ursodeoxycholic acid because it binds it.

4. **Infectious disease**. Babesiosis is a tick-borne (*Ixodes scapularis*) intraerythrocytic parasitosis of the US Northeast and upper Midwest that can also be transmitted by blood transfusion. It causes fever with hemolytic anemia, thrombocytopenia, raised LDH, and transaminitis, and it is most severe in asplenic, older, or immunocompromised patients. The smear shows intraerythrocytic ring forms, occasionally the pathognomonic tetrad ("Maltese cross"); PCR is more sensitive. Treat with atovaquone plus azithromycin (IDSA 2020); severe disease gets intravenous azithromycin, and exchange transfusion is considered for high-grade parasitemia (above 10%) or severe hemolysis or organ dysfunction. The same tick transmits Lyme disease and anaplasmosis, so consider coinfection, especially if fever persists.

5. **Rheumatology**. Axial spondyloarthritis presents as inflammatory back pain beginning before age 45, lasting more than three months, worse with rest and better with exercise, often with night pain and morning stiffness. MRI can show active sacroiliitis years before radiographs change (non-radiographic disease). NSAIDs are first-line. If they fail, add a TNF inhibitor or an IL-17 inhibitor; JAK inhibitors are a further option. Methotrexate and sulfasalazine do not help axial disease, and systemic glucocorticoids are not recommended (ACR/SAA/SPARTAN, 2019). Comorbidity guides the biologic choice: with inflammatory bowel disease or recurrent anterior uveitis, prefer a monoclonal anti-TNF antibody such as infliximab or adalimumab over etanercept, and avoid IL-17 inhibitors in active IBD.

6. **Endocrinology**. In subclinical hypothyroidism (raised TSH, normal free T4), repeat the TSH with free T4 in two to three months before acting, because many elevations are transient, for example during recovery from nonthyroidal illness. Levothyroxine is generally recommended when TSH is persistently 10 mIU/L or higher. Below that, treatment is individualized. In older adults, the TRUST trial (2017) found that levothyroxine did not improve symptoms or quality of life in patients 65 and older with mild elevation, and the TSH reference range rises with age. Pregnancy, or planning pregnancy, is the main exception to watchful waiting, and positive anti-TPO antibodies raise the risk of progression to overt disease.

7. **Hematology**. Heparin-induced thrombocytopenia typically causes a platelet fall of more than 50% that begins 5 to 10 days after heparin is started (sooner with recent exposure); the nadir is usually moderate, and thrombosis, not bleeding, is the danger. A low 4Ts score effectively excludes it and needs no testing. With an intermediate or high score, stop all heparin, including flushes and low-molecular-weight heparin, and start a non-heparin anticoagulant (argatroban, bivalirudin, fondaparinux, or a DOAC) even without thrombosis, then send an immunoassay and confirm with a functional assay such as the serotonin release assay. Do not start warfarin until the platelet count has recovered, because it can cause venous limb gangrene; if it was already started, reverse it with vitamin K. Avoid routine platelet transfusion.

8. **Oncology**. For most cancer-associated venous thromboembolism, a direct oral anticoagulant is now preferred over low-molecular-weight heparin. Edoxaban (Hokusai VTE Cancer, 2018) and rivaroxaban (SELECT-D, 2018) reduced recurrence but increased bleeding, concentrated in luminal gastrointestinal cancers. Apixaban (Caravaggio, 2020) was noninferior to dalteparin without a significant increase in major bleeding. In unresected luminal gastrointestinal or genitourinary cancer, apixaban or low-molecular-weight heparin is the safer choice. Check for interactions with chemotherapy and for severe thrombocytopenia. Treat for at least six months and continue while the cancer is active or treatment is ongoing.

9. **Nephrology**. In autosomal dominant polycystic kidney disease, *PKD1* variants cause earlier kidney failure than *PKD2*, and hypertension appears early. Use an ACE inhibitor or ARB, but not both. In HALT-PKD (2014), a lower blood-pressure target in patients aged 15 to 49 with preserved kidney function slowed growth in kidney volume. Tolvaptan, a vasopressin V2 antagonist, slows eGFR decline in patients at risk of rapid progression (TEMPO 3:4, 2012; REPRISE, 2017), at the cost of polyuria and a risk of idiosyncratic liver injury that requires regular liver-test monitoring. Screen for intracranial aneurysm with MR angiography when there is a family history of aneurysm or subarachnoid hemorrhage, or a high-risk occupation. Liver cysts are the most common extrarenal manifestation.

10. **Neurology**. Neuroleptic malignant syndrome follows a dopamine antagonist, including antiemetics such as metoclopramide, or abrupt withdrawal of levodopa. It develops over days, with altered mental status, lead-pipe rigidity, hyperthermia, autonomic instability, and a markedly raised CK. Serotonin syndrome develops within hours of a serotonergic drug and is distinguished by clonus (especially inducible or ocular), hyperreflexia worse in the legs, and gastrointestinal symptoms. For NMS, stop the offending drug (or restart the dopaminergic agent), cool, give fluids to protect against rhabdomyolysis-related kidney injury, and use benzodiazepines; dantrolene and bromocriptine are used in severe cases on limited evidence. When an antipsychotic is needed again, usual advice is to wait at least two weeks and choose a lower-potency agent.
