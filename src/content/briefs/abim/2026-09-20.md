1. **Cardiology**. Acute limb ischemia presents with sudden pain, pallor, pulselessness, paresthesia, weakness, and coolness. Begin intravenous unfractionated heparin promptly unless contraindicated and obtain urgent vascular-surgery assessment. A threatened limb with sensory loss or weakness needs immediate revascularization; do not delay it for prolonged diagnostic testing. An irreversibly anesthetic, paralyzed limb may require primary amputation because reperfusion can release potassium, acid, and myoglobin systemically.

2. **Pulmonary medicine**. Hepatopulmonary syndrome is liver disease or portal hypertension plus intrapulmonary vascular dilatation and impaired oxygenation. Platypnea and orthodeoxia—dyspnea and hypoxemia that worsen upright—are classic clues. On agitated-saline echocardiography, delayed bubbles in the left heart favor an intrapulmonary shunt, whereas immediate appearance suggests an intracardiac shunt. Supplemental oxygen is supportive, but liver transplantation is the only established definitive therapy.

3. **Gastroenterology**. Budd-Chiari syndrome is hepatic venous outflow obstruction, typically causing abdominal pain, hepatomegaly, and ascites. Look for an underlying prothrombotic disorder, especially a myeloproliferative neoplasm; a JAK2 mutation may reveal one even when blood counts are distorted by portal hypertension. Anticoagulate unless contraindicated, treat the cause, and escalate persistent portal-hypertensive disease to angioplasty or stenting when anatomically suitable, transjugular intrahepatic portosystemic shunt, or transplantation.

4. **Infectious disease**. Severe malaria is treated with intravenous artesunate, not oral therapy, and should trigger immediate consultation with public-health authorities for access and guidance. Obtain thick and thin blood smears to identify and quantify parasitemia, but repeat smears when initial testing is negative and suspicion remains high because parasitemia can be initially undetectable. Continue parenteral treatment until the patient can transition to a complete oral regimen; watch for delayed hemolytic anemia after artesunate.

5. **Rheumatology**. Remitting seronegative symmetrical synovitis with pitting edema causes abrupt symmetric distal synovitis and striking pitting edema of the hands or feet in an older adult, with negative rheumatoid factor. It often responds rapidly to low-dose glucocorticoid. Because the syndrome can be paraneoplastic, constitutional symptoms, an atypical course, or poor treatment response should prompt an age-appropriate search for malignancy rather than indefinite escalation of immunosuppression.

6. **Endocrinology**. In multiple endocrine neoplasia type 2, exclude or treat pheochromocytoma before operating on medullary thyroid carcinoma or hyperparathyroidism. An unrecognized catecholamine-secreting tumor can cause a lethal hypertensive crisis during anesthesia. Once pheochromocytoma is confirmed, establish alpha blockade and volume repletion before surgery; add beta blockade only after adequate alpha blockade if tachycardia requires it.

7. **Hematology**. Waldenstrom macroglobulinemia can cause IgM-mediated hyperviscosity with headache, visual change, mucosal bleeding, confusion, or retinal venous engorgement. Symptomatic hyperviscosity requires urgent plasma exchange, which removes intravascular IgM rapidly; do not wait for chemotherapy to work. Avoid unnecessary red-cell transfusion before viscosity is controlled, and remember that rituximab can transiently raise IgM, so patients with a high burden may need plasma exchange before rituximab-containing therapy.

8. **Oncology**. Differentiation syndrome during treatment of acute promyelocytic leukemia or other susceptible myeloid leukemia presents with unexplained fever, weight gain, edema, dyspnea, pulmonary infiltrates, pleural or pericardial effusions, hypotension, or kidney injury. Give dexamethasone immediately on suspicion rather than waiting for confirmation. Continue differentiation therapy in mild cases, but temporarily hold it when the syndrome is severe; manage leukocytosis and provide intensive supportive care.

9. **Nephrology**. Ethylene glycol poisoning produces a high-anion-gap metabolic acidosis, acute kidney injury, hypocalcemia, and calcium oxalate crystals, but neither crystals nor an osmolar gap is required. The osmolar gap may fall as the parent alcohol is metabolized while the anion gap rises, so a normal late osmolar gap does not exclude poisoning. Start fomepizole on strong suspicion without waiting for a level; severe acidosis, kidney failure, or major clinical deterioration can require hemodialysis.

10. **Neurology**. Cerebral amyloid angiopathy causes lobar intracerebral hemorrhage, cortical superficial siderosis, convexity subarachnoid hemorrhage, and transient focal neurologic episodes in older adults. MRI susceptibility sequences often show strictly lobar microbleeds rather than the deep microbleeds of hypertensive arteriopathy. Antithrombotic decisions require individualized risk assessment because recurrent lobar hemorrhage risk is high; glucocorticoids are reserved for the inflammatory variant, not ordinary cerebral amyloid angiopathy.
