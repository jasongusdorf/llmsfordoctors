1. **Cardiology**. Postural orthostatic tachycardia syndrome is a sustained heart-rate rise of at least 30 beats/min within 10 minutes of standing or head-up tilt (at least 40 beats/min in adolescents) with chronic orthostatic symptoms and without orthostatic hypotension. That last clause is the discriminator: in orthostatic hypotension the blood pressure falls, whereas in POTS the pressure holds and the heart rate climbs; inappropriate sinus tachycardia is fast at rest as well. Exclude dehydration, anemia, hyperthyroidism, and offending drugs first. First-line treatment is nonpharmacologic: more fluid and salt, compression garments, and graded exercise that starts recumbent (rowing, recumbent cycling, swimming) before progressing upright.

2. **Pulmonary medicine**. A milky pleural effusion is chylothorax, pseudochylothorax, or empyema. Centrifuge it first: empyema fluid clears because the turbidity is cells, whereas chylous and pseudochylous fluid stay turbid. A pleural triglyceride above 110 mg/dL supports chylothorax, a value below 50 mg/dL makes it unlikely, and chylomicrons on lipoprotein electrophoresis settle the intermediate range. Chylothorax follows thoracic-duct injury from surgery or trauma or obstruction by malignancy, especially lymphoma. Pseudochylothorax is a long-standing effusion, classically rheumatoid or tuberculous, behind thickened pleura, with high cholesterol, cholesterol crystals, and no chylomicrons. A fasting patient's chylothorax may not look milky at all.

3. **Gastroenterology**. In nonvariceal upper gastrointestinal bleeding, a Glasgow-Blatchford score of 0 or 1 identifies patients who can be managed as outpatients (2021 American College of Gastroenterology guideline). For admitted patients, transfuse at a hemoglobin of 7 g/dL (a somewhat higher threshold in cardiovascular disease), consider intravenous erythromycin before endoscopy to clear the stomach, and scope within 24 hours; endoscopy within 6 hours did not lower mortality in a 2020 NEJM randomized trial of high-risk patients. After endoscopic therapy for active bleeding, a visible vessel, or an adherent clot, give high-dose proton-pump inhibitor for 3 days, then twice daily. A clean-based ulcer needs no endoscopic therapy, and every peptic ulcer warrants testing for Helicobacter pylori.

4. **Infectious disease**. Rocky Mountain spotted fever kills when treatment waits for confirmation. Start doxycycline on clinical suspicion (fever, headache, and myalgia after possible tick exposure in spring or summer, often with thrombocytopenia, hyponatremia, and elevated transaminases) in patients of every age, including young children, for whom the CDC recommends doxycycline over alternatives. The rash typically begins at the wrists and ankles, spreads centrally, and involves the palms and soles, but it is often absent in the first days and never appears in some patients; many patients do not recall a tick bite. Serology is usually negative during the first week and needs paired acute and convalescent samples, so a negative early test does not exclude disease.

5. **Rheumatology**. Eosinophilic granulomatosis with polyangiitis follows a sequence: adult-onset asthma and chronic rhinosinusitis with nasal polyps, then peripheral eosinophilia, then vasculitis with mononeuritis multiplex, purpura, migratory pulmonary infiltrates, or glomerulonephritis. ANCA, usually anti-MPO, is positive in only a minority of patients, so a negative ANCA does not exclude it. Cardiac involvement (eosinophilic myocarditis or cardiomyopathy) is the leading disease-related cause of death and is often clinically silent, so check troponin, an ECG, and an echocardiogram at diagnosis. Organ-threatening disease needs glucocorticoids plus cyclophosphamide or rituximab; relapsing or refractory nonsevere disease responds to anti-IL-5 therapy such as mepolizumab or benralizumab.

6. **Endocrinology**. Thyrotoxicosis in the first trimester is often gestational transient thyrotoxicosis: high hCG stimulates the TSH receptor, frequently alongside hyperemesis gravidarum, with no goiter or orbitopathy, negative TSH-receptor antibodies, and resolution as hCG falls; it needs supportive care, not antithyroid drugs. Graves disease has positive TSH-receptor antibodies. When an antithyroid drug is needed, propylthiouracil is preferred in early pregnancy because methimazole is teratogenic (aplasia cutis, choanal and esophageal atresia), and methimazole is preferred after that because of propylthiouracil hepatotoxicity. Both cross the placenta, so use the lowest dose that keeps free T4 at or just above the upper limit of normal. Radioactive iodine is contraindicated in pregnancy.

7. **Hematology**. Hemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis should be considered in a patient with persistent fever, splenomegaly, and cytopenias in at least two lineages whose ferritin is strikingly elevated, especially with hypertriglyceridemia, low fibrinogen, liver dysfunction, or a high soluble IL-2 receptor (sCD25). Hemophagocytosis on bone-marrow biopsy is neither necessary nor sufficient for the diagnosis. In adults, the HScore helps estimate probability, and the trigger matters as much as the label: infection (especially EBV), malignancy (especially lymphoma), and autoimmune disease, where it is called macrophage activation syndrome. Untreated, it is rapidly fatal, so hematology involvement and a search for occult lymphoma should be urgent.

8. **Oncology**. Carriers of a pathogenic BRCA1 or BRCA2 variant get annual breast MRI from age 25, with mammography added from age 30, and are offered risk-reducing salpingo-oophorectomy at 35 to 40 for BRCA1, with deferral to 40 to 45 reasonable for BRCA2 once childbearing is complete. Ovarian cancer screening with CA-125 and transvaginal ultrasound is not a substitute for surgery. BRCA2 also raises the risk of male breast, prostate, and pancreatic cancer. Germline testing is recommended for every patient with epithelial ovarian cancer, pancreatic adenocarcinoma, or metastatic prostate cancer regardless of family history, partly because a positive result opens PARP-inhibitor therapy.

9. **Nephrology**. Suspect pseudohyperkalemia when a high potassium has no ECG changes and no reason for retention. Potassium leaks out of cells in the tube with hemolysis, fist clenching or a prolonged tourniquet, marked thrombocytosis, or marked leukocytosis such as chronic lymphocytic leukemia. In thrombocytosis, platelets release potassium during clotting, so serum potassium exceeds plasma potassium and a plasma (heparinized) sample corrects it. In extreme leukocytosis, fragile cells can lyse even in plasma, especially after pneumatic-tube transport ("reverse" pseudohyperkalemia), so measure a hand-carried whole-blood sample on a blood-gas analyzer. Treating the artifact with insulin or potassium binders risks hypokalemia and hypoglycemia.

10. **Neurology**. Amyotrophic lateral sclerosis combines upper-motor-neuron signs (spasticity, hyperreflexia, Babinski sign) with lower-motor-neuron signs (weakness, atrophy, fasciculations) in the same regions, while sensation, eye movements, and sphincter function are usually spared; frontotemporal cognitive or behavioral impairment occurs in a substantial minority. Sensory loss or bladder dysfunction suggests a mimic such as cervical spondylotic myelopathy, and a pure lower-motor-neuron picture with conduction block suggests multifocal motor neuropathy, which responds to IVIG. Riluzole modestly prolongs survival; noninvasive ventilation for respiratory insufficiency improves survival and quality of life; and tofersen is approved for SOD1-associated disease.
