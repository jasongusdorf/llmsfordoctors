1. **Cardiology**. A bicuspid aortic valve is a valve disease and an aortic disease. Many patients develop dilation of the ascending aorta or root, and that aortopathy progresses independently of the valve, so aortic imaging continues for life even after the valve has been replaced. Under the 2020 ACC/AHA valve guideline, replacing the aorta is recommended at a diameter above 5.5 cm. It is reasonable at 5.0 to 5.5 cm when there is an added risk factor for dissection (family history of dissection, growth of more than 0.5 cm per year, or coarctation), and at 4.5 cm or more when the patient is already having aortic valve surgery. Look for coarctation in every patient with a bicuspid valve, and remember the association with Turner syndrome.

2. **Pulmonary medicine**. Acute and chronic eosinophilic pneumonia are different diseases. Acute eosinophilic pneumonia is fever and hypoxemic respiratory failure over days, with diffuse bilateral infiltrates and often pleural effusions, in a previously healthy young adult, classically within weeks of starting to smoke or vape. Blood eosinophils are often normal at presentation, so the diagnosis comes from bronchoalveolar lavage (at least 25% eosinophils). It responds to corticosteroids within days and rarely relapses. Chronic eosinophilic pneumonia develops over weeks to months, often in a middle-aged woman with asthma, with peripheral blood eosinophilia and peripheral consolidation, the "photographic negative of pulmonary edema." It also responds briskly to steroids but often relapses during the taper.

3. **Gastroenterology**. Hepatitis E is no longer only a travel disease. In the US and Europe, genotype 3 is a zoonosis acquired from undercooked pork, wild game, or shellfish, and it mainly causes illness in older men. In endemic regions, waterborne genotype 1 can cause fulminant hepatitis in pregnancy. In solid-organ transplant recipients and other immunosuppressed patients, genotype 3 can become chronic and progress to cirrhosis. Antibody tests are unreliable in that setting, so test for HEV RNA. Treatment starts with reducing immunosuppression; ribavirin is used if viremia persists. Extrahepatic features include Guillain-Barré syndrome and neuralgic amyotrophy. Test for HEV in unexplained acute hepatitis, including suspected drug-induced liver injury.

4. **Infectious disease**. Whipple disease, caused by *Tropheryma whipplei*, classically affects a middle-aged white man. Migratory, seronegative arthralgia or arthritis often precedes diarrhea, weight loss, and malabsorption by years. That arthritis is often misread as a seronegative inflammatory arthritis, and immunosuppression, including TNF inhibitors, can make the infection markedly worse. Other features are lymphadenopathy, culture-negative endocarditis, and CNS disease; oculomasticatory myorhythmia is pathognomonic. Duodenal biopsy shows PAS-positive foamy macrophages, and *T. whipplei* PCR confirms the diagnosis. Standard treatment is intravenous ceftriaxone (or meropenem) for 2 weeks, then oral trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole for 1 year. Doxycycline plus hydroxychloroquine is an oral alternative.

5. **Rheumatology**. Takayasu arteritis is a large-vessel vasculitis of the aorta and its major branches, usually in a woman under 40. It presents with constitutional symptoms followed by arm claudication, diminished or absent pulses, a systolic pressure difference of more than 10 mm Hg between the arms, subclavian or abdominal bruits, and renovascular hypertension. Hypertension can be missed when the pressure is taken in a stenosed arm. ESR and CRP can be normal while disease is active, so they do not rule it out. Diagnose with CT or MR angiography of the aorta and its branches. Treat with glucocorticoids plus a steroid-sparing agent, such as methotrexate or a TNF inhibitor. When possible, revascularize only after the disease is quiescent.

6. **Endocrinology**. Subclinical hyperthyroidism is a low TSH with normal free T4 and T3. Before acting, repeat the tests in a few months and exclude levothyroxine overreplacement, recovery from thyroiditis, nonthyroidal illness, and drugs that suppress TSH, such as glucocorticoids and dopamine. Grading drives management. Under the 2016 American Thyroid Association guideline, a persistent TSH below 0.1 mU/L should be treated in anyone aged 65 or older. It should also be treated at any age in patients with heart disease, cardiac risk factors, osteoporosis, or hyperthyroid symptoms, and in postmenopausal women not taking estrogen or a bisphosphonate. A TSH between 0.1 mU/L and the lower limit of normal is usually observed, with treatment considered in the same higher-risk groups. The main risks of a suppressed TSH are atrial fibrillation and bone loss.

7. **Hematology**. In atrial fibrillation, apixaban is reduced from 5 mg to 2.5 mg twice daily only when at least two of three criteria are met: age 80 or older, weight 60 kg or less, and serum creatinine 1.5 mg/dL or higher. One criterion alone, for example an 82-year-old with normal kidney function, does not justify the lower dose. Inappropriate dose reduction is common and is associated with more strokes without less bleeding. The atrial fibrillation rule does not apply to venous thromboembolism, which starts at 10 mg twice daily for 7 days, then 5 mg twice daily. After at least 6 months of treatment, 2.5 mg twice daily can be used for extended secondary prevention.

8. **Oncology**. A Pancoast (superior sulcus) tumor is a lung cancer, usually non-small cell, at the lung apex that invades the chest wall, brachial plexus, and sympathetic chain. It causes shoulder pain radiating down the ulnar side of the arm in a C8 to T1 distribution, weakness and wasting of the hand muscles, and an ipsilateral Horner syndrome. Respiratory symptoms are usually absent, and an apical mass is easy to miss on a chest film, so these patients are often treated for shoulder disease or cervical radiculopathy for months. Suspect it in a smoker with persistent shoulder pain and Horner syndrome. MRI defines plexus, vascular, and vertebral involvement. In resectable disease, standard treatment is induction chemoradiation followed by surgical resection (SWOG 9416).

9. **Nephrology**. Alport syndrome is a type IV collagen disorder. It is most often X-linked (*COL4A5*); autosomal forms arise from *COL4A3* or *COL4A4*. It causes persistent microscopic hematuria from childhood, then proteinuria and progressive kidney failure, with bilateral sensorineural hearing loss and ocular findings such as anterior lenticonus. Electron microscopy shows irregular thinning, thickening, and splitting ("basket-weave") of the glomerular basement membrane, although early disease and heterozygous carriers may show only thinning. Women with the X-linked form are not just carriers; many develop proteinuria and some reach kidney failure. Genetic testing confirms the diagnosis. Start an ACE inhibitor early because it delays kidney failure. A rare complication after transplant is anti-GBM disease directed against the normal collagen chain the patient lacked.

10. **Neurology**. Chronic subdural hematoma is a treatable cause of subacute cognitive decline, gait failure, headache, or mild hemiparesis in an older adult. Risk factors include brain atrophy, anticoagulant or antiplatelet therapy, and alcohol use; the causative fall is often trivial or forgotten. It can mimic dementia, stroke, or TIA. Noncontrast CT shows a crescentic collection, which may be isodense to brain during the subacute phase. Symptomatic or large collections are drained surgically, usually through burr holes, and recurrence is common. Do not give dexamethasone: in Dex-CSDH (NEJM 2020) it led to worse functional outcomes. Middle meningeal artery embolization added to surgery reduced recurrence requiring repeat surgery in randomized trials published in 2024, including EMBOLISE.
