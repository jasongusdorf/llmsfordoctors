1. **Cardiology**. Chronic aortic regurgitation is operated on by numbers, not symptoms alone. Symptomatic severe AR gets valve surgery, and so does asymptomatic severe AR once the LVEF falls to 55% or below. With a normal EF, progressive left ventricular dilation is the trigger — an LV end-systolic dimension greater than 50 mm (or indexed above roughly 25 mm/m² in a small patient) is a reasonable indication in the 2020 ACC/AHA valve guideline. Vasodilator therapy is for symptomatic patients who are not surgical candidates, or for concomitant hypertension; it is not a substitute for surgery in a dilating ventricle. Acute severe AR from dissection or endocarditis looks nothing like the chronic form: no wide pulse pressure, no bounding pulses, just a quiet short murmur, tachycardia, and pulmonary edema, with premature mitral valve closure on echo. That is a surgical emergency, and an intra-aortic balloon pump is contraindicated.

2. **Pulmonary**. Idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis: a definite UIP pattern on high-resolution CT — subpleural, basal-predominant reticulation with honeycombing, with or without traction bronchiectasis, and no features suggesting an alternative diagnosis — in the right clinical setting makes the diagnosis without a surgical lung biopsy. The therapeutic point the exam wants is negative: immunosuppression is harmful. PANTHER-IPF stopped the prednisone/azathioprine/N-acetylcysteine arm early for excess death and hospitalization. Antifibrotics (pirfenidone, nintedanib) slow the rate of FVC decline; they do not improve symptoms or reverse fibrosis. Refer for transplant evaluation at diagnosis, not when the patient deteriorates.

3. **Gastroenterology**. Acute variceal hemorrhage has four interventions that change mortality, and antibiotics are the one most often forgotten. Give a vasoactive drug (octreotide) for 2 to 5 days, ceftriaxone for up to 7 days, endoscopy with band ligation within 12 hours, and transfuse restrictively to a hemoglobin target of about 7 g/dL — the liberal 9 g/dL strategy caused more rebleeding and higher mortality in cirrhosis. In high-risk patients (Child-Pugh C, or Child-Pugh B with active bleeding at endoscopy), pre-emptive TIPS within 72 hours reduces rebleeding and death. Secondary prophylaxis is a nonselective beta-blocker plus serial band ligation — both, not either.

4. **Infectious disease**. Latent TB treatment is now rifamycin-based and short. The preferred CDC regimens are 3HP (weekly isoniazid plus rifapentine for 12 weeks), 4R (daily rifampin for 4 months), or 3HR (daily isoniazid plus rifampin for 3 months); 6 to 9 months of isoniazid alone is an alternative, not the default, because completion rates and hepatotoxicity are both worse. Two things must happen first: exclude active disease with symptoms and a chest radiograph, since treating active TB with a single drug breeds resistance, and review the medication list for rifamycin interactions — oral contraceptives, DOACs, azoles, methadone, and many antiretrovirals.

5. **Rheumatology**. Scleroderma renal crisis: abrupt severe hypertension with acute kidney injury, often with a microangiopathic smear and thrombocytopenia, in early diffuse cutaneous systemic sclerosis. Anti-RNA polymerase III antibodies mark the highest risk; anticentromere and limited disease point instead toward pulmonary arterial hypertension. Treatment is an ACE inhibitor, captopril classically, titrated up aggressively — and continued even as the creatinine rises, because the renal recovery follows blood pressure control. Glucocorticoids at prednisone-equivalent doses of about 15 mg/day or more are a recognized precipitant, so avoid high-dose steroids in diffuse disease. Prophylactic ACE inhibitors do not prevent renal crisis and may worsen outcomes when it occurs.

6. **Endocrinology**. Prolactinoma is the pituitary macroadenoma that is not primarily surgical. Even a large tumor with chiasmal compression and visual field loss is treated first with a dopamine agonist (cabergoline preferred over bromocriptine), which shrinks the tumor and restores vision in most patients; surgery is for dopamine-agonist failure, intolerance, or apoplexy. Before calling it a prolactinoma, exclude the common non-tumor causes: pregnancy, hypothyroidism (TRH drives prolactin), chronic kidney disease, and drugs — antipsychotics, metoclopramide, and verapamil. And watch for the hook effect: a very large sellar mass with only a modestly elevated prolactin should prompt a repeat assay on a diluted specimen, because assay saturation can make an enormous prolactin look nearly normal.

7. **Hematology**. Anticoagulant reversal for life-threatening bleeding, by drug. Dabigatran: idarucizumab. Apixaban or rivaroxaban: andexanet alfa, or 4-factor prothrombin complex concentrate where andexanet is unavailable — ANNEXA-I showed better hemostatic efficacy than usual care in intracranial hemorrhage but more thrombotic events, so the decision is not automatic. Warfarin: 4-factor PCC plus intravenous vitamin K, and the vitamin K is not optional, because PCC clotting factors are cleared long before warfarin is. Fresh frozen plasma is the fallback when PCC is unavailable. Neither idarucizumab nor andexanet is a reason to delay the mechanical or surgical control of bleeding.

8. **Oncology**. Immune checkpoint inhibitor toxicity: the general rule is hold the drug and give corticosteroids — prednisone roughly 0.5 to 1 mg/kg/day for grade 2, methylprednisolone 1 to 2 mg/kg/day with permanent discontinuation for grade 3 or 4 — and escalate to infliximab for colitis that has not responded within about 48 to 72 hours. Two exceptions carry most of the exam points. Endocrinopathies (hypothyroidism, hypophysitis, checkpoint-induced diabetes) are managed with hormone replacement and usually do not require permanently stopping the drug; steroids do not restore the gland. And for hepatitis, use mycophenolate rather than infliximab, which is hepatotoxic. Myocarditis is uncommon but disproportionately lethal: any new dyspnea, chest pain, or troponin elevation on therapy warrants an urgent workup, high-dose steroids, and permanent discontinuation.

9. **Nephrology**. Membranous nephropathy is the nephrotic glomerulopathy with the highest venous thromboembolism risk, including renal vein thrombosis, so new flank pain, hematuria, or a falling GFR in a nephrotic patient should raise it. Anti-PLA2R antibodies are present in most primary cases and track disease activity, and a positive titer with compatible findings can spare the biopsy in some settings. A negative PLA2R pushes you to look for a secondary cause: hepatitis B, lupus (class V), medications including NSAIDs, and malignancy — the THSD7A subtype in particular is enriched for underlying cancer, and age-appropriate cancer screening is warranted. For high-risk disease, MENTOR established rituximab as first-line immunosuppression over cyclosporine for sustained remission.

10. **Neurology**. Guillain-Barré syndrome: ascending areflexic weakness progressing over days to a nadir within 4 weeks, most often after Campylobacter jejuni. CSF shows albuminocytologic dissociation — high protein with few cells — but it can be normal in the first week, so a normal early tap does not exclude it, and a pleocytosis above roughly 50 cells should redirect you toward HIV seroconversion, Lyme, or malignant infiltration. Monitor with serial bedside spirometry rather than pulse oximetry or blood gases, which fail late; declining forced vital capacity and negative inspiratory force are the intubation triggers. IVIG and plasma exchange are equally effective, are not combined, and corticosteroids do not work — that last point separates GBS from CIDP, where steroids do.
