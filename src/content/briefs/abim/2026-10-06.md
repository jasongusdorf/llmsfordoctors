1. **Cardiology**. Takotsubo (stress) cardiomyopathy causes transient left-ventricular dysfunction, classically apical ballooning, often in a postmenopausal woman after emotional or physical stress, with chest pain, ST-segment changes, and troponin elevation that mimic an acute coronary syndrome. No ECG or echocardiographic pattern reliably excludes infarction, so coronary angiography is needed to rule out obstructive disease before the label is applied. The trap is the hypotensive patient: basal hypercontractility can create dynamic left-ventricular outflow obstruction, which inotropes and vasodilators worsen; give fluids and a beta-blocker if not contraindicated, and screen for left-ventricular thrombus. Function usually recovers over days to weeks, but recurrence is possible.

2. **Pulmonary medicine**. Carbon monoxide poisoning presents with headache, nausea, dizziness, or confusion, often in several members of one household during heating season. Standard pulse oximetry reads falsely normal because it cannot distinguish carboxyhemoglobin from oxyhemoglobin, and arterial PaO2 is also normal; diagnosis requires a carboxyhemoglobin level by co-oximetry, and a venous sample is adequate. Start high-flow 100% oxygen by nonrebreather immediately, since it shortens carbon monoxide elimination. Hyperbaric oxygen is considered for severe poisoning such as loss of consciousness, neurologic deficits, cardiac ischemia, or pregnancy, though its benefit for delayed neurocognitive sequelae remains debated. After smoke inhalation, consider concomitant cyanide toxicity when lactic acidosis is out of proportion.

3. **Gastroenterology**. Gilbert syndrome is reduced UGT1A1 glucuronidation producing mild, intermittent unconjugated hyperbilirubinemia that rises with fasting, intercurrent illness, stress, or exertion. Once you confirm the bilirubin is predominantly unconjugated, liver enzymes are normal, and there is no hemolysis (normal hemoglobin, reticulocyte count, haptoglobin, and LDH), the diagnosis is clinical and benign: no imaging, biopsy, or genetic testing is required. A conjugated hyperbilirubinemia or abnormal enzymes means another diagnosis. The one practical consequence is drug metabolism: reduced UGT1A1 activity increases toxicity from irinotecan.

4. **Infectious disease**. Suspect Legionella pneumonia when community-acquired pneumonia comes with high fever, diarrhea, confusion, hyponatremia, or elevated liver enzymes, particularly after exposure to hotel, cruise-ship, or building water systems, or when a beta-lactam regimen is failing. The urinary antigen detects only *Legionella pneumophila* serogroup 1, so a negative result does not exclude infection; send a lower respiratory specimen for PCR or culture on selective media when suspicion persists. Legionella is intracellular, so beta-lactams are ineffective; treat with a respiratory fluoroquinolone such as levofloxacin or with azithromycin.

5. **Rheumatology**. Reactive arthritis is an asymmetric, lower-extremity-predominant oligoarthritis, often with enthesitis such as Achilles or plantar-fascia pain and dactylitis, that follows within weeks of enteric infection (Salmonella, Shigella, Campylobacter, Yersinia) or genitourinary *Chlamydia trachomatis* infection. Conjunctivitis, urethritis, and mucocutaneous lesions may accompany it, and the joint fluid is sterile. Test for chlamydia and treat active infection and partners, but antibiotics do not shorten established post-enteric arthritis; NSAIDs are first-line, with intra-articular glucocorticoids or a conventional DMARD for persistent disease. HLA-B27 is associated with more severe or chronic disease but is not required for diagnosis.

6. **Endocrinology**. Subacute granulomatous (de Quervain) thyroiditis causes a painful, tender thyroid, often after a viral upper respiratory illness, with fever and a markedly elevated ESR or CRP. Its thyrotoxic phase comes from release of preformed hormone, so radioiodine uptake is low, which separates it from Graves disease, and thionamides are useless. Treat symptoms with a beta-blocker plus an NSAID, reserving glucocorticoids for severe pain or NSAID failure. A transient hypothyroid phase may follow, and most patients return to euthyroidism. Among low-uptake thyrotoxicosis, a high thyroglobulin points to thyroiditis, whereas a low thyroglobulin suggests exogenous thyroid hormone.

7. **Hematology**. Acute intermittent porphyria should be considered in a young woman with recurrent severe abdominal pain out of proportion to a benign examination, especially with tachycardia, hypertension, hyponatremia, motor-predominant neuropathy, psychiatric symptoms, or dark urine. Attacks are provoked by porphyrinogenic drugs, alcohol, fasting, and luteal-phase hormones. Unlike porphyria cutanea tarda, there is no photosensitive skin disease. Screen with a spot urine porphobilinogen during symptoms; a normal result during symptoms makes the common acute porphyrias very unlikely. Treat by stopping triggers and giving intravenous hemin, and consider givosiran for recurrent attacks. Weakness can progress to respiratory failure, so monitor ventilatory function.

8. **Oncology**. In brain metastases, dexamethasone is given for symptomatic vasogenic edema and tapered as quickly as symptoms allow; asymptomatic edema does not require glucocorticoids, which carry substantial cumulative toxicity. Do not start prophylactic antiseizure medication in a patient who has never had a seizure, as guidelines advise against it; perioperative use is debated. When seizures do occur, a non-enzyme-inducing agent such as levetiracetam is preferred because enzyme inducers interact with chemotherapy and dexamethasone. Definitive treatment is individualized with surgery, stereotactic radiosurgery, or systemic therapy with intracranial activity.

9. **Nephrology**. Minimal change disease causes abrupt, often severe nephrotic syndrome with bland urine sediment. Light microscopy is normal and immunofluorescence is negative; diagnosis rests on diffuse podocyte foot-process effacement on electron microscopy. In adults, look for secondary causes, especially NSAIDs, lithium, and Hodgkin lymphoma. It is glucocorticoid-responsive, though adults respond more slowly than children and relapse is common; calcineurin inhibitors or rituximab are used for frequently relapsing or steroid-dependent disease. Acute kidney injury can occur, particularly in older adults with heavy proteinuria.

10. **Neurology**. Transient global amnesia is abrupt anterograde amnesia with repetitive questioning in a middle-aged or older adult who remains alert, knows their own identity, and has no focal deficits or seizure features. Episodes resolve within 24 hours, usually within several hours, leaving only a memory gap for the event, and are often preceded by exertion, emotional stress, cold-water immersion, or sexual activity. It is not a TIA and does not require stroke prevention therapy. Focal signs, impaired consciousness, persistence beyond 24 hours, head trauma, or recurrent brief episodes suggesting transient epileptic amnesia warrant further evaluation.
