1. **Cardiology**. Right-ventricular infarction should be suspected with an inferior myocardial infarction plus hypotension, elevated jugular venous pressure, and clear lungs. Confirm with right-sided ECG leads. The failing right ventricle is preload dependent: give cautious intravenous fluid when hypotension reflects underfilling and avoid nitrates, diuretics, and other preload-reducing drugs that can precipitate shock. Reperfuse urgently and treat bradyarrhythmias when they impair output.

2. **Pulmonary medicine**. Noninvasive positive-pressure ventilation reduces intubation and mortality in an acute COPD exacerbation with respiratory acidosis when the patient can protect the airway and cooperate. It is inappropriate when there is respiratory arrest, inability to protect the airway, severe hemodynamic instability, copious secretions, or rapidly worsening mental status. Do not let a failing trial delay endotracheal intubation.

3. **Gastroenterology**. Acute cholangitis is biliary obstruction plus infection, commonly presenting with fever, jaundice, and right-upper-quadrant pain; hypotension and altered mental status signal severe disease. Start intravenous antibiotics and resuscitation promptly, but antibiotics alone are insufficient when obstruction persists. Severe cholangitis or failure to improve requires urgent biliary drainage, usually by endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography.

4. **Infectious disease**. In suspected bacterial meningitis, obtain blood cultures and begin empiric antibiotics promptly; do not delay treatment for lumbar puncture or neuroimaging. Give dexamethasone immediately before or with the first antibiotic dose when pneumococcal meningitis is a concern, then discontinue it if another cause is established. Perform head imaging before lumbar puncture only when clinical features suggest increased risk from the procedure, such as a focal neurologic deficit, papilledema, new seizure, major immunocompromise, or markedly impaired consciousness.

5. **Rheumatology**. Behçet disease causes recurrent oral aphthae with genital ulcers, ocular inflammation, skin lesions, and variable-vessel vasculitis. Venous thrombosis may occur despite inflammatory rather than classic hypercoagulable pathogenesis, and pulmonary-artery aneurysm can cause life-threatening hemoptysis. Immunosuppression is central for major vascular disease; anticoagulation is individualized and can be dangerous until a pulmonary-artery aneurysm has been excluded.

6. **Endocrinology**. Severe symptomatic hyponatremia with seizure, coma, or impending herniation requires prompt hypertonic saline to achieve a small initial rise in serum sodium and reverse cerebral edema. After symptoms improve, slow correction and monitor frequently: excessive correction can cause osmotic demyelination, especially in patients with very low sodium, alcoholism, malnutrition, liver disease, or hypokalemia. Desmopressin and electrolyte-free water can halt or reverse an unintended overcorrection.

7. **Hematology**. Both transfusion-associated circulatory overload and transfusion-related acute lung injury cause dyspnea and bilateral pulmonary opacities after transfusion. Hypertension, jugular venous distention, elevated natriuretic peptide, and improvement with diuresis favor circulatory overload; fever, hypotension, and noncardiogenic pulmonary edema favor acute lung injury. Stop the transfusion and notify the blood bank in either case, but diuretics help circulatory overload and are not primary therapy for acute lung injury.

8. **Oncology**. Immune-checkpoint inhibitors can cause inflammatory toxicity in nearly any organ, including colitis, hepatitis, pneumonitis, myocarditis, nephritis, and hypophysitis. New organ dysfunction during or after therapy should trigger evaluation for both immune toxicity and competing causes such as infection or progression. Hold treatment and start glucocorticoids for most clinically significant toxicities, but endocrine gland failure usually requires long-term hormone replacement and does not necessarily reverse with immunosuppression.

9. **Nephrology**. Rhabdomyolysis causes acute kidney injury through myoglobin-mediated tubular injury and often produces a urine dipstick positive for blood with few or no red cells on microscopy. Creatine kinase confirms muscle injury; hyperkalemia is the most immediately dangerous electrolyte abnormality. Give early isotonic crystalloid while monitoring volume status and electrolytes, but routine bicarbonate, mannitol, or loop diuretics have not shown clear benefit for preventing kidney injury.

10. **Neurology**. A first unprovoked seizure requires evaluation for provoking metabolic or toxic causes and structural brain disease, generally with brain imaging and electroencephalography. Long-term antiseizure therapy is clearly indicated after recurrent unprovoked seizures, while treatment after a single event depends on recurrence risk. Epileptiform EEG findings, a causative brain lesion, or a prior brain insult raises that risk; a normal EEG does not exclude epilepsy.
