1. **Cardiology**. Mitral valve prolapse is heard as a mid-systolic click followed by a late systolic murmur, and the click moves with left ventricular volume. Anything that makes the ventricle smaller (standing, Valsalva) brings the click earlier and lengthens the murmur; squatting, which enlarges the ventricle, delays the click and shortens it. On echocardiography, prolapse means systolic displacement of a leaflet more than 2 mm above the mitral annulus in the parasternal long-axis view. Endocarditis prophylaxis is not indicated for prolapse alone. Most patients do well, but a small "arrhythmic MVP" subset, often young women with bileaflet prolapse, mitral annular disjunction, inferior T-wave inversions, and frequent complex ventricular ectopy, carries a risk of sudden death and deserves cardiology follow-up.

2. **Pulmonary medicine**. Tuberculous pleurisy is a lymphocyte-predominant exudate, usually with few mesothelial cells, and it is largely a hypersensitivity reaction with few organisms in the fluid. That makes fluid AFB smears rarely positive and fluid cultures positive only in a minority. An elevated pleural adenosine deaminase supports the diagnosis and, when low in a lymphocytic effusion, makes TB unlikely; ADA can also rise in empyema, lymphoma, and rheumatoid effusions. Pleural biopsy with histology (granulomas) and culture has the highest yield. Even though the effusion often resolves on its own, treat it as active tuberculosis, because untreated patients frequently go on to develop pulmonary or extrapulmonary TB.

3. **Gastroenterology**. Small intestinal bacterial overgrowth follows stasis or loss of the normal defenses: surgically altered anatomy (blind loops, Roux-en-Y), strictures or jejunal diverticula, dysmotility (systemic sclerosis, diabetes, opioids), ileocecal valve resection, and hypochlorhydria. It causes bloating, diarrhea, and in severe cases steatorrhea and weight loss. The tell on labs is a low vitamin B12 with a normal or high folate, because the bacteria consume B12 and synthesize folate. Diagnose with a glucose or lactulose breath test; by North American consensus, a rise in hydrogen of at least 20 ppm over baseline within 90 minutes is positive. Treat with antibiotics (rifaximin is commonly used) and correct the underlying cause, or it recurs.

4. **Infectious disease**. Toxic shock syndrome is fever, hypotension, and multiorgan involvement driven by superantigen toxins. Staphylococcal TSS follows tampon use, nasal packing, or surgical wounds, presents with a diffuse "sunburn" erythroderma, and desquamates over the palms and soles one to two weeks later; blood cultures are usually negative. Streptococcal TSS usually comes from invasive group A strep, often necrotizing soft-tissue infection, and blood cultures are usually positive. Management is source control (remove the tampon or packing, debride early) plus a bactericidal agent and clindamycin or linezolid, which suppress toxin production. IVIG is sometimes added in severe streptococcal TSS.

5. **Rheumatology**. Hypertrophic osteoarthropathy is digital clubbing plus periostitis of the long bones, most often the distal tibia, fibula, radius, and ulna. Patients complain of deep, aching bone pain in the shins or forearms, sometimes with noninflammatory joint effusions. Plain films show periosteal new bone, and bone scintigraphy shows symmetric linear uptake along the cortices (the "double stripe" sign). In an adult it is usually secondary, and the leading cause is lung cancer, especially non-small cell; other causes include chronic suppurative lung disease, cyanotic heart disease, and endocarditis. New clubbing with painful shins in a smoker means chest imaging, and the pain often eases after the tumor is resected.

6. **Endocrinology**. Hypertriglyceridemia has two different goals. When triglycerides are very high, the danger is pancreatitis, which becomes a real risk once levels exceed about 1,000 mg/dL. Address secondary causes first (uncontrolled diabetes, alcohol, hypothyroidism, nephrotic syndrome, estrogen, retinoids, some antipsychotics, and protease inhibitors), then use a very-low-fat diet and a fibrate. At moderate elevations, the goal is cardiovascular risk. Adding a fibrate to a statin has not reduced events (ACCORD Lipid; PROMINENT with pemafibrate). In REDUCE-IT (NEJM 2019), icosapent ethyl 4 g daily cut major cardiovascular events by about 25% in statin-treated patients with triglycerides of 135 to 499 mg/dL and established cardiovascular disease or diabetes with risk factors, although its mineral-oil placebo remains debated. Mixed EPA and DHA products are not equivalent: STRENGTH was neutral.

7. **Hematology**. Asplenia, whether surgical or functional (sickle cell disease), carries a lifelong risk of overwhelming post-splenectomy infection, most often from *Streptococcus pneumoniae* and other encapsulated organisms. Vaccinate against pneumococcus, meningococcus (both MenACWY and MenB), and *Haemophilus influenzae* type b, plus yearly influenza. Give the vaccines at least two weeks before an elective splenectomy, or, if surgery was urgent, at least two weeks afterward. Teach patients that fever is an emergency: they should seek care immediately for empiric ceftriaxone (plus vancomycin where resistance is a concern), and many keep a standby antibiotic at home. Also remember *Capnocytophaga canimorsus* sepsis after a dog bite, and severe babesiosis and malaria. Howell-Jolly bodies on the smear confirm absent splenic function.

8. **Oncology**. HPV-associated oropharyngeal cancer is rising, often in middle-aged men who have never smoked, and it classically presents as a painless, often cystic, upper neck mass from a small primary in the tonsil or base of the tongue. A cystic neck mass in an adult is not a branchial cleft cyst until cancer is excluded. The 2017 AAO-HNS guideline advises evaluating an adult neck mass that persists two weeks or more without an infectious explanation, avoiding repeated empiric antibiotics, and using fine-needle aspiration rather than an open biopsy. p16 immunohistochemistry is the HPV surrogate. HPV-positive disease has a much better prognosis and has its own AJCC staging. Swapping cisplatin for cetuximab with radiation to reduce toxicity was inferior (RTOG 1016 and De-ESCALaTE, 2019).

9. **Nephrology**. Thyrotoxic periodic paralysis is sudden proximal weakness with marked hypokalemia, classically in a young Asian man, after a carbohydrate-heavy meal, alcohol, or rest after exercise. The hyperthyroidism may be subtle. The hypokalemia is a shift into cells, not a loss, so urinary potassium excretion is low and there is no acid-base disturbance. Give modest doses of potassium only, because potassium moves back out of cells as the attack resolves and rebound hyperkalemia is a known complication. A nonselective beta-blocker (propranolol) aborts and prevents attacks, and restoring the euthyroid state ends them. Familial hypokalemic periodic paralysis (*CACNA1S* or *SCN4A* channel mutations) looks similar but begins earlier in life, has a family history, and has a normal thyroid.

10. **Neurology**. Normal pressure hydrocephalus presents with gait disturbance first and most prominently: a broad-based, magnetic, short-stepped gait with turns broken into many steps. Cognitive slowing and urinary urgency or incontinence follow. Imaging shows ventricles enlarged out of proportion to atrophy (Evans index above 0.3), often with tight high-convexity sulci, and lumbar opening pressure is normal. A high-volume lumbar tap (typically 30 to 50 mL), with gait timed before and after, or extended lumbar drainage, identifies likely responders to ventriculoperitoneal shunting. A negative tap does not exclude benefit. Gait improves most after shunting and cognition least. Unlike Parkinson disease, arm swing is preserved and levodopa does little.
