1. **Cardiology**. Suspect coarctation of the aorta in a young adult with hypertension in the arms, weak or delayed femoral pulses (radiofemoral delay), and lower blood pressure in the legs than in the arms. A chest film may show rib notching, where enlarged intercostal collaterals erode the undersides of the ribs, and a "3 sign" at the narrowing. Coarctation travels with bicuspid aortic valve and Turner syndrome, and it is associated with intracranial berry aneurysms. CT or MR angiography confirms the diagnosis and plans the repair. Significant coarctation with hypertension is repaired, usually by transcatheter stenting in adults. Hypertension often persists or comes back after repair, so patients need lifelong blood pressure follow-up and periodic imaging of the repair site for recoarctation or aneurysm.

2. **Pulmonary medicine**. Stratify acute pulmonary embolism before choosing treatment. High-risk PE means sustained hypotension or shock and gets systemic thrombolysis unless it is contraindicated; surgical or catheter therapy is for patients who cannot receive lysis or in whom it fails. Normotensive patients are split by right-ventricular dysfunction on imaging and by troponin. When both are abnormal, the patient is intermediate-high risk and gets anticoagulation with close monitoring, plus rescue lysis if they decompensate. In PEITHO, routine tenecteplase in this group prevented hemodynamic decompensation but increased major bleeding and hemorrhagic stroke, and it did not clearly lower mortality. Low-risk patients (sPESI of 0, or no Hestia criteria) can usually be treated at home with a DOAC. The role of catheter-directed therapy in intermediate-risk PE is still being studied.

3. **Gastroenterology**. Autoimmune hepatitis produces a hepatocellular pattern with elevated IgG, usually in women. It can present as anything from asymptomatic transaminitis to acute liver failure. Type 1 carries ANA, anti-smooth-muscle antibody, or both. Type 2 carries anti-LKM-1 and mostly affects children and young adults. The autoantibodies are not diagnostic on their own. Exclude viral hepatitis, Wilson disease, and drug-induced liver injury (nitrofurantoin and minocycline can cause an autoimmune-like hepatitis), and get a biopsy showing interface hepatitis with plasma cells. Induction is a glucocorticoid plus azathioprine, with TPMT activity checked first. Budesonide can replace prednisone in patients without cirrhosis. Avoid it in cirrhosis, because portosystemic shunting bypasses its first-pass hepatic metabolism. Most patients need long-term maintenance therapy, and relapse after withdrawal is common.

4. **Infectious disease**. Geography plus one clue usually identifies the endemic mycosis. Histoplasmosis comes from the Ohio and Mississippi River valleys, caves, and bird or bat droppings. In advanced HIV it can disseminate with fever, pancytopenia, hepatosplenomegaly, and oral ulcers, and urine or serum Histoplasma antigen is the most useful rapid test (it cross-reacts with Blastomyces). Blastomycosis comes from the Great Lakes and the Ohio and Mississippi valleys. It pairs pneumonia with verrucous or ulcerated skin lesions and bone involvement, and it shows broad-based budding yeast. Coccidioidomycosis comes from the desert Southwest and California's Central Valley. It causes "valley fever" with erythema nodosum, arthralgias, and peripheral eosinophilia, and uncomplicated primary infection in a healthy host often resolves without antifungals. Mild to moderate disease is treated with an azole: itraconazole for histoplasmosis and blastomycosis, fluconazole for coccidioidomycosis. Severe or disseminated disease starts with liposomal amphotericin B. The exception is coccidioidal meningitis, which is treated with high-dose fluconazole, continued lifelong.

5. **Rheumatology**. The extraglandular complication to watch for in Sjögren disease is B-cell lymphoma. It is most often a mucosa-associated (MALT) marginal-zone lymphoma of the parotid, and its risk is many times that of the general population. Red flags are persistent or unilateral parotid enlargement, palpable purpura, cryoglobulinemia, low C4, and a monoclonal protein. Anti-Ro/SSA also matters in pregnancy. It crosses the placenta and can cause neonatal lupus and congenital complete heart block, so these pregnancies usually get serial fetal heart surveillance. In a mother who has already had an affected child, hydroxychloroquine lowers the risk of recurrence. Dryness is treated with sialogogues (pilocarpine or cevimeline), artificial tears, and topical cyclosporine. Arrange regular dental care, because loss of saliva drives rapid tooth decay.

6. **Endocrinology**. Paget disease of bone usually shows up as an isolated high alkaline phosphatase in an older adult, with normal calcium and phosphate. A normal GGT, or a raised bone-specific alkaline phosphatase, points to bone rather than liver as the source. Plain radiographs of the affected area confirm the diagnosis, and a bone scan maps its extent. Complications depend on which bone is involved. Skull disease causes an enlarging head and hearing loss, long bones bow and fracture, and nearby joints develop arthritis. Osteosarcoma is rare, but suspect it when a patient has new focal pain or a rapidly enlarging lesion. Treat symptomatic disease with a single zoledronic acid infusion after correcting vitamin D deficiency, to avoid hypocalcemia. Immobilizing a patient with extensive disease can raise their serum calcium.

7. **Hematology**. Hereditary spherocytosis and warm autoimmune hemolytic anemia both produce spherocytes, reticulocytosis, and indirect hyperbilirubinemia. The direct antiglobulin test tells them apart: it is negative in hereditary spherocytosis and usually positive in warm AIHA. Hereditary spherocytosis is a red-cell membrane disorder, often of ankyrin or spectrin, and is usually autosomal dominant. Ask about relatives with anemia, splenectomy, or early gallstones. A high MCHC is a clue. The eosin-5'-maleimide (EMA) binding flow cytometry test is the preferred confirmation; osmotic fragility testing is older and less specific. Expect pigment gallstones, splenomegaly, and aplastic crisis with parvovirus B19, when the reticulocyte count falls to near zero. Give folic acid in moderate or severe disease. Splenectomy, total or partial, is reserved for severe disease and is preceded by pneumococcal, meningococcal, and Haemophilus influenzae type b vaccination.

8. **Oncology**. In cutaneous melanoma, Breslow thickness and ulceration drive staging and the next step. Sentinel lymph node biopsy is recommended for tumors thicker than 1 mm. It is considered for T1b tumors: 0.8 to 1.0 mm thick, or thinner than 0.8 mm with ulceration. A positive sentinel node no longer means routine completion lymph node dissection, because MSLT-II found no melanoma-specific survival benefit over nodal ultrasound surveillance. After resection of stage III disease, adjuvant anti-PD-1 therapy (pembrolizumab or nivolumab) improves recurrence-free survival. For BRAF V600-mutant tumors, dabrafenib plus trametinib is an alternative (COMBI-AD). Test every advanced melanoma for BRAF V600.

9. **Nephrology**. Patients with recurrent calcium stones, and first-time formers at high risk, get a 24-hour urine collection on their usual diet, measuring volume, calcium, oxalate, citrate, uric acid, and sodium. Do not restrict dietary calcium. A normal intake, about 1,000 to 1,200 mg a day taken with meals, binds intestinal oxalate and lowers stone risk, and any calcium supplement should also be taken with food. Cut sodium and animal protein, and aim for a urine output of at least 2.5 L a day. Thiazides treat hypercalciuria. Potassium citrate treats hypocitraturia and alkalinizes the urine for uric acid stones. Allopurinol is for calcium oxalate stones with hyperuricosuria. For an acute distal ureteral stone of 10 mm or less with controlled pain, an alpha-blocker such as tamsulosin is an option to help the stone pass (medical expulsive therapy).

10. **Neurology**. Treat suspected Wernicke encephalopathy on clinical grounds. Only a minority of patients have the full triad of encephalopathy, ophthalmoplegia or nystagmus, and gait ataxia, so any one of these in an at-risk patient is enough to start thiamine. Risk goes beyond alcohol use disorder to bariatric surgery, hyperemesis gravidarum, prolonged vomiting, cancer, malnutrition, and refeeding. Give high-dose parenteral thiamine several times a day, because oral absorption is unreliable. Never withhold glucose from a hypoglycemic patient, but give thiamine with it or before it. Replete magnesium, a cofactor for thiamine-dependent enzymes; without it, the response to thiamine can be poor. MRI may show symmetric signal change in the medial thalami, mammillary bodies, and periaqueductal region, but a normal scan does not exclude the diagnosis. Untreated, it progresses to Korsakoff syndrome, with anterograde amnesia and confabulation.
