1. **Cardiology**. Acute aortic syndrome is treated with anti-impulse therapy while definitive management is arranged. Give an IV beta-blocker first to reduce heart rate and shear stress; if blood pressure remains high, add a titratable vasodilator. Starting a vasodilator before rate control can provoke reflex tachycardia and increase aortic-wall stress. Acute type A dissection requires emergency surgery. Uncomplicated type B dissection is initially managed medically, whereas rupture, malperfusion, refractory pain, uncontrolled hypertension, or progressive enlargement makes it complicated and generally calls for endovascular repair.

2. **Pulmonary**. Obesity hypoventilation syndrome requires obesity, sleep-disordered breathing, and awake daytime hypercapnia after excluding another cause of hypoventilation. A serum bicarbonate below 27 mmol/L can effectively rule it out when clinical suspicion is low or moderate; a higher bicarbonate is a reason to obtain an arterial blood gas, not proof of the diagnosis. Most patients also have obstructive sleep apnea. Positive-airway-pressure therapy and sustained weight loss treat the cause; oxygen alone can worsen carbon dioxide retention and does not correct hypoventilation.

3. **Gastroenterology**. Wilson disease belongs in the differential for otherwise unexplained liver disease, neuropsychiatric symptoms, or Coombs-negative hemolysis in a young patient. No single screening result is definitive: ceruloplasmin may be normal, and Kayser-Fleischer rings may be absent in isolated hepatic disease. Combine slit-lamp examination, serum ceruloplasmin, 24-hour urinary copper, and, when necessary, hepatic copper quantification or ATP7B testing. Fulminant Wilson disease often couples intravascular hemolysis with a disproportionately low alkaline phosphatase; urgent liver-transplant evaluation is lifesaving.

4. **Infectious disease**. A painful prosthetic joint with a draining sinus tract has a prosthetic joint infection until proved otherwise. Before antibiotics, obtain inflammatory markers, blood cultures when systemic illness is present, and synovial fluid for cell count, differential, and cultures unless the patient is unstable. Debridement with implant retention is most successful when the implant is well fixed, there is no sinus tract, and infection is early after implantation or symptoms have been present for less than about three weeks. Chronic infection usually requires component exchange; rifampin is used only in combination for susceptible staphylococcal infection because resistance emerges rapidly with monotherapy.

5. **Rheumatology**. Scleroderma renal crisis presents with abrupt kidney injury, usually accelerated hypertension, microangiopathic hemolytic anemia, thrombocytopenia, and a bland or mildly active urine sediment. Major clues are early diffuse cutaneous systemic sclerosis, rapidly progressive skin thickening, anti-RNA polymerase III antibodies, and recent high-dose glucocorticoids. Start an ACE inhibitor immediately and titrate aggressively, even if creatinine initially rises; do not stop it solely because dialysis becomes necessary, because kidney recovery can occur months later. Normotensive crisis exists and has a worse prognosis.

6. **Endocrinology**. Suspected pheochromocytoma is screened with plasma free or 24-hour urinary fractionated metanephrines, ideally after minimizing physiologic stress and interfering drugs. Biochemical confirmation comes before tumor localization except in an emergency. Before elective resection, establish alpha-adrenergic blockade and volume repletion; add a beta-blocker only afterward if tachycardia persists. Giving beta-blockade first leaves unopposed alpha vasoconstriction and can precipitate hypertensive crisis. Also consider hereditary testing, because a substantial minority are linked to germline syndromes.

7. **Hematology**. Disseminated intravascular coagulation consumes platelets and coagulation factors: expect thrombocytopenia, prolonged PT and often aPTT, high D-dimer, falling fibrinogen, and schistocytes. Treat the trigger; give platelets, cryoprecipitate, or plasma for clinically important bleeding or an urgent procedure, not merely to normalize laboratory values. TTP is the key mimic, but its coagulation tests are usually normal because platelet-rich microthrombi do not consume clotting factors. Suspected TTP needs immediate plasma exchange, whereas plasma exchange is not routine treatment for DIC.

8. **Oncology**. Tumor lysis syndrome produces hyperkalemia, hyperphosphatemia, hypocalcemia, hyperuricemia, and acute kidney injury after rapid tumor-cell breakdown; it can begin spontaneously before chemotherapy. Prevention is risk stratification, vigorous hydration, frequent laboratory monitoring, and urate-lowering therapy. Allopurinol prevents formation of new uric acid but does not remove uric acid already present; rasburicase rapidly degrades existing uric acid and is preferred for established or very high-risk disease. Rasburicase is contraindicated in G6PD deficiency because it can cause severe hemolysis and methemoglobinemia.

9. **Nephrology**. Anti-GBM disease causes rapidly progressive glomerulonephritis, often with diffuse alveolar hemorrhage; kidney biopsy shows crescentic glomerulonephritis with linear IgG staining along the basement membrane. Send anti-GBM antibodies, but when pulmonary-kidney syndrome strongly suggests the diagnosis, begin treatment without waiting for the result. Standard therapy is plasma exchange plus glucocorticoids and cyclophosphamide. An important exception is irreversible kidney failure at presentation with dialysis dependence, no pulmonary hemorrhage, and essentially all glomeruli crescentic, where toxic immunosuppression is unlikely to restore kidney function.

10. **Neurology**. Guillain-Barré syndrome is a rapidly progressive, usually symmetric weakness with reduced or absent reflexes; autonomic instability and respiratory failure are the dangerous complications. Cerebrospinal fluid classically shows high protein with few cells, but protein can still be normal during the first week, so a normal early lumbar puncture does not exclude it. Follow forced vital capacity and inspiratory force serially rather than waiting for hypoxemia. IVIG and plasma exchange are similarly effective; combining them adds no benefit, and glucocorticoids do not work.
