1. **Cardiology**. Acute pericarditis is diagnosed clinically when at least two of four features are present: characteristic pleuritic chest pain, a pericardial friction rub, new widespread ST-segment elevation or PR depression, and a new or worsening pericardial effusion. Treat uncomplicated disease with an NSAID or aspirin plus colchicine and restrict strenuous exercise until symptoms and inflammatory markers resolve. Admit patients with high-risk features such as fever, a large effusion or tamponade, immunosuppression, trauma, anticoagulation, or failure to improve with initial anti-inflammatory therapy.

2. **Pulmonary medicine**. In acute pulmonary embolism, systemic thrombolysis is reserved primarily for persistent hypotension, obstructive shock, or cardiac arrest attributable to PE; right-ventricular strain or a positive troponin alone does not justify routine lysis in a normotensive patient. A deteriorating patient with an absolute contraindication to thrombolysis needs urgent evaluation for catheter-based therapy or surgical embolectomy. Begin anticoagulation promptly when clinical suspicion is high and bleeding risk is acceptable rather than waiting through a prolonged diagnostic delay.

3. **Gastroenterology**. In acute severe ulcerative colitis, test for *Clostridioides difficile*, exclude cytomegalovirus when appropriate, provide venous-thromboembolism prophylaxis, and begin intravenous glucocorticoids. Assess the response early; failure to improve calls for rescue therapy with infliximab or cyclosporine and prompt colorectal-surgical involvement. Toxic megacolon, perforation, uncontrolled hemorrhage, or clinical deterioration requires urgent colectomy rather than escalating immunosuppression.

4. **Infectious disease**. High-grade atrioventricular block in a patient with possible Lyme exposure should raise concern for Lyme carditis, even when the classic erythema migrans rash was never noticed. Admit and monitor patients with severe conduction disease; intravenous ceftriaxone is used initially, with transition to oral therapy after meaningful conduction recovery. The block usually resolves with antibiotics, so a permanent pacemaker is rarely needed; temporary pacing is appropriate when symptomatic bradycardia cannot be managed medically.

5. **Rheumatology**. Finding monosodium urate crystals does not exclude septic arthritis: gout and joint infection can coexist. Any acutely hot, swollen joint with meaningful infection risk requires synovial-fluid cell count, Gram stain, and culture, even if crystals are seen. Urate-lowering therapy should generally be continued during an acute flare, and anti-inflammatory prophylaxis is used when it is initiated or intensified because rapid changes in serum urate can provoke attacks.

6. **Endocrinology**. In diabetic ketoacidosis, total-body potassium is depleted even when the initial serum potassium is normal or high. Start isotonic fluid, check potassium before insulin, and replace potassium as it falls; if the patient is frankly hypokalemic, defer insulin until potassium replacement has begun because insulin can precipitate a lethal arrhythmia. Resolution is judged by closure of the ketoacid-driven anion gap and clinical recovery, not by glucose normalization alone, so add dextrose when needed to permit insulin to continue clearing ketones.

7. **Hematology**. Iron-deficiency anemia in an adult man or a postmenopausal woman warrants evaluation for occult gastrointestinal blood loss, usually with both upper endoscopy and colonoscopy, rather than iron replacement alone. A normal mean corpuscular volume does not exclude early iron deficiency; low ferritin is the most specific routine finding, but inflammation can raise ferritin and mask deficiency. In that setting, low transferrin saturation and the broader clinical picture help establish the diagnosis.

8. **Oncology**. Malignancy-associated hypercalcemia is treated first with intravenous isotonic fluid when volume depleted. Calcitonin lowers calcium quickly but tachyphylaxis limits it to short-term bridging; an intravenous bisphosphonate or denosumab provides the slower, sustained effect. Glucocorticoids are especially useful when excess calcitriol drives the hypercalcemia, as in some lymphomas. Loop diuretics are not routine calcium-lowering therapy and should be reserved for managing volume overload after rehydration.

9. **Nephrology**. A rapidly rising creatinine with hematuria, dysmorphic red cells, red-cell casts, and proteinuria suggests rapidly progressive glomerulonephritis and warrants urgent serologic testing and kidney biopsy. The major immunofluorescence patterns organize the differential: linear staining suggests anti-GBM disease, granular immune-complex deposition suggests lupus or infection-related disease, and little or no staining suggests pauci-immune ANCA-associated vasculitis. When pulmonary hemorrhage accompanies the kidney injury, treatment often must begin before every test has returned.

10. **Neurology**. A sudden headache reaching maximal intensity immediately is subarachnoid hemorrhage until proved otherwise. Obtain urgent noncontrast head CT; when CT is nondiagnostic but suspicion remains, proceed with the locally appropriate confirmatory strategy, typically lumbar puncture or CT angiography. Once aneurysmal hemorrhage is identified, give nimodipine to reduce delayed cerebral ischemia and secure the aneurysm urgently with endovascular coiling or surgical clipping; antifibrinolytic therapy is not a substitute for definitive aneurysm treatment.
