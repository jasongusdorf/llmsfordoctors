1. **Cardiology**. Hypertensive emergency is defined by acute target-organ damage, not by the blood-pressure number alone. Treat with a titratable intravenous agent in a monitored setting. The 2017 ACC/AHA guideline advises lowering systolic pressure by no more than 25% in the first hour, then to 160/100–110 mm Hg over the next 2–6 hours, then toward normal over 24–48 hours, because abrupt reduction can cause cerebral, coronary, or renal ischemia. The exceptions are aortic dissection (systolic below 120 mm Hg within the first hour), severe preeclampsia or eclampsia, and pheochromocytoma crisis (systolic below 140 mm Hg within the first hour). Acute stroke has its own targets. Severe hypertension without organ damage needs oral therapy and follow-up, not rapid lowering.

2. **Pulmonary medicine**. In pulmonary arterial hypertension, especially idiopathic, heritable, or drug-induced disease, the diagnostic right-heart catheterization should include acute vasoreactivity testing, usually with inhaled nitric oxide. Only a small minority are responders, and only they are candidates for high-dose calcium-channel blockers, with reassessment for a sustained response. Empiric calcium-channel blockade without testing can cause hypotension and right-ventricular failure. Non-responders receive pulmonary-arterial-hypertension-specific therapy (endothelin-receptor antagonists, phosphodiesterase-5 inhibitors, prostacyclin-pathway drugs). Testing does not apply to pulmonary hypertension from left-heart or lung disease.

3. **Gastroenterology**. Refeeding syndrome follows reintroduction of nutrition after starvation, prolonged poor intake, alcohol use disorder, or anorexia nervosa. Carbohydrate stimulates insulin, which drives phosphate, potassium, and magnesium into cells; hypophosphatemia is the hallmark and can cause weakness, respiratory failure, heart failure, arrhythmia, rhabdomyolysis, and hemolysis. Give thiamine before or with feeding, because glucose can precipitate Wernicke encephalopathy. Start calories low, advance slowly, and check and replete electrolytes before feeding and frequently over the first several days.

4. **Infectious disease**. In HIV-associated *Pneumocystis* pneumonia, add corticosteroids when disease is moderate to severe, defined as room-air PaO2 below 70 mm Hg or an alveolar-arterial gradient of 35 mm Hg or more, and start them as early as possible, within 72 hours of anti-*Pneumocystis* therapy. Trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole is first line. Check G6PD before dapsone or primaquine-based alternatives. In patients without HIV, typically on chronic glucocorticoids and often presenting during a taper, disease is more fulminant and the benefit of adjunctive steroids is less certain.

5. **Rheumatology**. Inclusion body myositis is the commonest acquired myopathy after age 50 and the classic "polymyositis" that does not respond to treatment. Weakness is insidious and often asymmetric, with a distinctive predilection for the finger flexors and knee extensors, causing poor grip, falls, and frequently dysphagia. Creatine kinase is normal or only modestly elevated. Biopsy shows endomysial inflammation with rimmed vacuoles, and anti-cN1A antibodies support the diagnosis. It does not respond to glucocorticoids or other immunosuppression; management is supportive.

6. **Endocrinology**. Nonthyroidal illness syndrome is a common pattern in acutely ill patients: low T3 appears first, low T4 appears with more severe illness, and TSH is usually normal or low. Dopamine and glucocorticoids further suppress TSH. During recovery TSH can rise transiently, sometimes above the reference range, before normalizing, which can be misread as primary hypothyroidism. Avoid thyroid testing in hospitalized patients unless thyroid disease is strongly suspected, do not treat these abnormalities with thyroid hormone, and repeat testing after recovery.

7. **Hematology**. Hereditary hemorrhagic telangiectasia is an autosomal dominant vascular disorder, usually from *ENG* or *ACVRL1* variants, diagnosed clinically by recurrent epistaxis, mucocutaneous telangiectasias, visceral arteriovenous malformations, and an affected first-degree relative. Gastrointestinal bleeding causes iron-deficiency anemia. Pulmonary arteriovenous malformations allow paradoxical embolism, causing ischemic stroke and brain abscess, so screen with transthoracic contrast echocardiography and treat significant lesions with embolization. Hepatic malformations can cause high-output heart failure. *SMAD4* variants combine the disorder with juvenile polyposis.

8. **Oncology**. Paraneoplastic neurologic syndromes often precede the cancer diagnosis. Anti-Hu antibodies accompany sensory neuronopathy or encephalomyelitis, usually with small-cell lung cancer. Anti-Yo antibodies accompany subacute cerebellar degeneration, usually with ovarian or breast cancer. Anti-NMDA-receptor encephalitis in a young woman should prompt a search for ovarian teratoma. A negative antibody panel does not exclude the diagnosis. Search for the tumor with CT and, if unrevealing, FDG-PET, and repeat screening over time. Treating the tumor is central; syndromes with antibodies to intracellular antigens respond poorly to immunotherapy, whereas cell-surface antibody syndromes often respond.

9. **Nephrology**. Calciphylaxis (calcific uremic arteriolopathy) occurs mostly in patients on dialysis and presents with exquisitely painful livedo or retiform purpura that progresses to necrotic ulcers, often over fatty areas such as the abdomen and thighs. Risk factors include warfarin, obesity, diabetes, female sex, hyperphosphatemia, calcium-based binders, and hyperparathyroidism. Management is multimodal: wound care and pain control, intensified dialysis, stopping warfarin and calcium or vitamin D supplementation where possible, and sodium thiosulfate, which is off-label with limited evidence. Mortality is high, mostly from wound infection and sepsis.

10. **Neurology**. Bell palsy is acute unilateral lower-motor-neuron facial weakness that includes the forehead; forehead sparing implies a central lesion and warrants stroke evaluation. Oral corticosteroids started within 72 hours of onset improve recovery. Antivirals alone are not recommended, and adding them to steroids offers at most a small benefit. Protect the eye with lubrication and taping at night. Ear or palate vesicles suggest Ramsay Hunt syndrome, and bilateral facial palsy should prompt evaluation for Lyme disease, Guillain-Barré syndrome, and sarcoidosis.
