1. **Cardiology**. Severe aortic stenosis can present with exertional dyspnea, angina, syncope, heart failure, or an otherwise unexplained fall in exercise tolerance. Once severe disease causes symptoms or left-ventricular systolic dysfunction, valve replacement—not medical therapy—is the definitive treatment. Low-flow, low-gradient measurements can hide truly severe stenosis; dobutamine stress echocardiography or CT aortic-valve calcium scoring helps distinguish fixed severe obstruction from pseudo-severe disease.

2. **Pulmonary medicine**. Allergic bronchopulmonary aspergillosis is a hypersensitivity disorder in patients with asthma or cystic fibrosis. Clues include difficult-to-control wheezing, eosinophilia, markedly elevated total IgE, Aspergillus sensitization, fleeting pulmonary opacities, mucus plugging, and central bronchiectasis. Systemic glucocorticoids treat acute disease; an azole can reduce fungal burden and steroid exposure in recurrent or treatment-dependent disease. Aspergillus in sputum alone does not establish the diagnosis.

3. **Gastroenterology**. Chronic pancreatitis causes irreversible structural damage and may produce chronic epigastric pain, pancreatic calcifications, exocrine insufficiency, and diabetes. A low fecal elastase supports exocrine pancreatic insufficiency; serum amylase and lipase may be normal because little functioning acinar tissue remains. Treat maldigestion with pancreatic enzymes taken during meals and snacks, plus nutrition and fat-soluble-vitamin assessment. Persistent weight loss despite enzymes should trigger a search for poor adherence, inadequate dosing, small-bowel disease, or pancreatic cancer.

4. **Infectious disease**. Prior antibiotic exposure is the commonest reason infective endocarditis cultures are negative. When cultures remain sterile, recheck the exposure history and pursue fastidious organisms guided by epidemiology—especially Coxiella burnetii and Bartonella with serology, and Tropheryma whipplei with molecular testing when appropriate. Obtain multiple blood-culture sets before antibiotics whenever the patient is stable, but never delay empiric treatment in an unstable patient.

5. **Rheumatology**. Sjögren disease causes autoimmune destruction of lacrimal and salivary glands, but fatigue, inflammatory arthritis, neuropathy, interstitial lung disease, renal tubular acidosis, and vasculitis may dominate. Anti-Ro/SSA is the key antibody; a negative antibody does not exclude disease, and objective ocular, salivary, or minor-salivary-gland testing may be needed. Persistent unilateral parotid enlargement, palpable purpura, low complement, cryoglobulinemia, or monoclonal gammopathy should raise concern for B-cell lymphoma.

6. **Endocrinology**. Suspected adrenal crisis is treated immediately with intravenous hydrocortisone and rapid isotonic crystalloid; add dextrose when hypoglycemia is present. Draw cortisol and ACTH first only if doing so will not delay treatment. Primary adrenal failure often causes hyperkalemia and salt wasting because aldosterone is deficient, whereas secondary adrenal failure usually preserves aldosterone and potassium. Search for and treat the precipitant, commonly infection or interruption of chronic glucocorticoid therapy.

7. **Hematology**. Cold agglutinin disease is complement-mediated autoimmune hemolysis that worsens with cold exposure and can cause acrocyanosis. The direct antiglobulin test is typically positive for complement and negative or only weakly positive for IgG, unlike warm autoimmune hemolytic anemia. Keep the patient and transfused blood warm and treat clinically important disease with complement-directed or rituximab-based therapy. Glucocorticoids and splenectomy usually work poorly because hemolysis is predominantly complement driven and hepatic.

8. **Oncology**. Fever during profound neutropenia after chemotherapy is an emergency. Obtain blood cultures promptly and start an antipseudomonal beta-lactam without waiting for culture results or neutrophil recovery. Vancomycin is not routine; add it for a suspected catheter or skin infection, pneumonia, hemodynamic instability, or another specific indication. Persistent fever alone in an otherwise stable patient is not a reason to keep broadening antibacterial therapy, but prolonged neutropenia should prompt evaluation and empiric coverage for invasive fungal infection.

9. **Nephrology**. Type 4 renal tubular acidosis is the hyperkalemic form of normal-anion-gap metabolic acidosis. It reflects deficient aldosterone effect and is common with diabetic kidney disease, tubulointerstitial disease, renin-angiotensin blockade, potassium-sparing diuretics, trimethoprim, NSAIDs, and heparin. Unlike distal acidification failure, the urine can become appropriately acidic; impaired ammonium excretion is the central defect. Remove contributing drugs when possible and treat hyperkalemia and the underlying cause.

10. **Neurology**. Normal-pressure hydrocephalus classically combines gait impairment, cognitive decline, and urinary urgency or incontinence, with ventriculomegaly out of proportion to cortical atrophy. Gait dysfunction is usually the earliest and most shunt-responsive feature; isolated dementia without a gait disorder should suggest another diagnosis. Improvement after large-volume lumbar puncture or temporary CSF drainage supports benefit from ventricular shunting, but failure to improve does not perfectly exclude it.
