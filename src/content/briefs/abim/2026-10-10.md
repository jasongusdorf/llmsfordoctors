1. **Cardiology**. Lipoprotein(a) is largely genetically determined, changes little over a lifetime, and is not meaningfully lowered by diet, exercise, or statins, so a single measurement is usually enough. The 2026 ACC/AHA dyslipidemia guideline recommends measuring it at least once in every adult; a level of 125 nmol/L (50 mg/dL) or higher is a risk-enhancing factor that should push you toward more intensive LDL-cholesterol lowering and tighter control of other risk factors. Lp(a) itself is not yet a treatment target: in September 2026 the pelacarsen outcome trial, Lp(a)HORIZON, lowered Lp(a) but did not reduce major cardiovascular events.

2. **Pulmonary medicine**. Silicosis follows inhalation of crystalline silica in work such as sandblasting, quarrying, mining, foundry work, and cutting engineered-stone countertops, which has driven recent clusters of accelerated disease in young workers. Look for upper-lobe nodules, and sometimes "eggshell" calcification of hilar lymph nodes. Silica exposure substantially raises the risk of tuberculosis, so screen for latent infection and keep a low threshold to investigate active disease. Silicosis is also associated with lung cancer and autoimmune disease. There is no effective drug therapy; ending exposure is the intervention.

3. **Gastroenterology**. Screening for Barrett esophagus is not offered to everyone with reflux. The 2022 American College of Gastroenterology guideline suggests a single screening endoscopy for patients with chronic GERD symptoms plus three or more additional risk factors: male sex, age over 50, White race, tobacco smoking, obesity, and Barrett esophagus or esophageal adenocarcinoma in a first-degree relative. After a negative screening endoscopy, it suggests against repeat screening. A swallowable nonendoscopic capsule-sponge device combined with a biomarker is an acceptable alternative to endoscopy.

4. **Infectious disease**. Spinal epidural abscess classically causes back pain, fever, and neurologic deficit, but the full triad is uncommon, so focal spinal pain with fever or an elevated inflammatory marker in a patient who injects drugs, has diabetes, has had a recent spinal procedure, or has bacteremia should prompt urgent gadolinium-enhanced MRI, ideally of the whole spine because skip lesions occur. *Staphylococcus aureus*, including methicillin-resistant strains, is the most common pathogen, so empiric therapy should cover it. A new or progressing neurologic deficit calls for urgent surgical consultation for decompression; outcome tracks the deficit present at the time of treatment.

5. **Rheumatology**. Hydroxychloroquine retinopathy is irreversible and can keep progressing after the drug is stopped, so prevention depends on dosing and screening. The American Academy of Ophthalmology advises keeping the daily dose at or below 5.0 mg/kg of real body weight. Obtain a baseline examination early in therapy; in a patient without major risk factors, annual screening with automated visual fields and spectral-domain OCT can begin after 5 years. Start annual screening immediately when risk factors are present, such as kidney disease, concurrent tamoxifen, or pre-existing macular disease.

6. **Endocrinology**. Do not stop denosumab without a plan. Unlike a bisphosphonate, its effect is not retained in bone: after discontinuation, bone turnover rebounds, bone density gains are lost, and some patients sustain multiple vertebral fractures. There is no denosumab drug holiday. If therapy is to end, transition to a bisphosphonate, such as zoledronic acid or oral alendronate, timed to the end of the dosing interval, and avoid delaying the next injection while that plan is being made.

7. **Hematology**. In an anemic patient, ferritin is the single most useful test for iron deficiency, and the American Gastroenterological Association recommends a cutoff of 45 ng/mL rather than 15 ng/mL to avoid missing it; inflammation and chronic kidney disease raise ferritin and blunt this threshold. For oral replacement, each dose triggers a hepcidin rise that reduces absorption of the next dose for roughly a day, so once-daily or alternate-day dosing is at least as effective as multiple daily doses and causes fewer gastrointestinal side effects. Switch to intravenous iron for intolerance, malabsorption, ongoing losses that outpace oral repletion, or need for rapid correction.

8. **Oncology**. Before rituximab or any other anti-CD20 antibody, test every patient for hepatitis B surface antigen and total hepatitis B core antibody. Reactivation can cause fulminant hepatitis and liver failure. Anti-CD20 therapy is high risk not only for HBsAg-positive patients but also for those who are HBsAg-negative and core-antibody-positive, so both groups generally warrant antiviral prophylaxis with entecavir or tenofovir, started before chemotherapy and continued for months after it ends. A lamivudine-based regimen is no longer preferred because of resistance.

9. **Nephrology**. Distal (type 1) renal tubular acidosis is a normal-anion-gap metabolic acidosis in which the kidney cannot excrete acid, so urine pH stays inappropriately high, above 5.5, despite systemic acidemia. Hypokalemia, hypocitraturia, nephrocalcinosis, and calcium phosphate stones are typical. In adults, look for Sjögren syndrome or other autoimmune disease, hypercalciuria, and drugs such as amphotericin B or lithium. Treat with oral alkali, usually potassium citrate, which corrects the acidosis and hypokalemia and reduces stone formation.

10. **Neurology**. Essential tremor is an action tremor, postural and kinetic, that usually affects both hands and may involve the head or voice; it is often familial and commonly improves transiently with alcohol. Parkinsonian tremor is typically a unilateral or asymmetric rest tremor that lessens with movement and comes with bradykinesia and rigidity, and a rest tremor alone should not be called essential tremor. First-line drugs for disabling essential tremor are propranolol and primidone; levodopa does not help it.
