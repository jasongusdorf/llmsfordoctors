1. **Cardiology**. A new or presumed-new left bundle branch block is no longer treated as a STEMI equivalent by itself; the 2013 ACCF/AHA STEMI guideline dropped it. Look for infarction with the Sgarbossa criteria, which are also applied to ventricular paced rhythms: concordant ST elevation of at least 1 mm in a lead with a positive QRS (5 points), concordant ST depression of at least 1 mm in V1 to V3 (3 points), and discordant ST elevation of at least 5 mm (2 points). A score of 3 or more is highly specific but insensitive. The Smith modification swaps the fixed 5 mm rule for a proportion, discordant ST elevation at least 25% of the depth of the preceding S wave, and is more sensitive. A patient with LBBB who is hemodynamically unstable or has ongoing ischemic pain despite treatment still goes to the cath lab without waiting on the ECG.

2. **Pulmonary medicine**. An isolated low DLCO, with normal spirometry and normal lung volumes, points to the pulmonary circulation: pulmonary arterial hypertension or chronic thromboembolic disease. Anemia, early interstitial lung disease, and early emphysema do the same, so correct the DLCO for hemoglobin before interpreting it. In obstruction, a low DLCO favors emphysema, while asthma usually leaves it normal or high. A raised DLCO suggests extra blood in the lung for the gas to bind: alveolar hemorrhage, polycythemia, or a left-to-right shunt. Asthma and obesity can raise it as well.

3. **Gastroenterology**. Vomiting followed by hematemesis is usually a Mallory-Weiss tear, a mucosal laceration at the gastroesophageal junction that stops bleeding on its own in most patients. Vomiting followed by severe chest or upper abdominal pain is Boerhaave syndrome until proven otherwise. That is a transmural rupture, usually of the left posterolateral distal esophagus. Clues are subcutaneous or mediastinal emphysema and a left pleural effusion. Pleural fluid that is acidic, high in amylase from saliva, or contains food particles all point to it. Confirm with CT or a water-soluble contrast esophagram, not barium first. Management is nil by mouth, broad-spectrum antibiotics, and urgent thoracic surgery consultation; mortality rises steeply with each hour of delay.

4. **Infectious disease**. Rabies post-exposure prophylaxis for an unvaccinated, immunocompetent patient (ACIP 2010) starts with thorough wound washing. Then give human rabies immune globulin at 20 IU/kg, infiltrating as much as is anatomically feasible into and around the wound, and four vaccine doses on days 0, 3, 7, and 14. An immunocompromised patient gets a fifth dose on day 28. Never inject the immune globulin at the same site or with the same syringe as the vaccine. A previously vaccinated patient gets vaccine on days 0 and 3 only, with no immune globulin. A healthy dog, cat, or ferret that bites can be confined and observed for 10 days instead of starting prophylaxis. Bat bites can go unnoticed, so give prophylaxis after direct bat contact where a bite cannot be excluded, including a bat found in the room of someone who was asleep, unless the bat tests negative.

5. **Rheumatology**. Antisynthetase syndrome combines inflammatory myositis, interstitial lung disease, mechanic's hands (cracked, hyperkeratotic radial fingers), non-erosive inflammatory arthritis, Raynaud phenomenon, and fever. Anti-Jo-1 (histidyl-tRNA synthetase) is the commonest antibody. With non-Jo-1 antibodies such as anti-PL-7 and anti-PL-12, lung disease often dominates and the myositis can be mild or absent, so the first presentation may be to a pulmonologist. Lung involvement drives prognosis. A different pattern to know is anti-MDA5 dermatomyositis: little or no muscle weakness, skin ulceration and painful palmar papules, and rapidly progressive interstitial lung disease with high early mortality that needs aggressive combination immunosuppression.

6. **Endocrinology**. After parathyroidectomy for severe hyperparathyroidism, prolonged hypocalcemia has two explanations, and phosphate separates them. In hungry bone syndrome, demineralized bone takes up mineral avidly once PTH falls, so calcium, phosphate, and magnesium all drop; PTH is normal or high. Risk factors are a raised preoperative alkaline phosphatase, radiographic bone disease, a large adenoma, and renal (secondary or tertiary) hyperparathyroidism. Postsurgical hypoparathyroidism gives low calcium with a normal or high phosphate and a low or inappropriately normal PTH. Hungry bone needs aggressive IV and oral calcium, calcitriol, and magnesium repletion, and it can last weeks. Replace magnesium in any case of hypocalcemia, because hypomagnesemia impairs both PTH secretion and its action.

7. **Hematology**. Lead poisoning in adults is mainly occupational: battery recycling, smelting, renovating or demolishing surfaces with old paint, and firing ranges. A retained bullet, some imported traditional remedies, and some glazed ceramics are other sources. It causes abdominal colic, constipation, fatigue, cognitive change, motor-predominant neuropathy (wrist drop is classic), hypertension, and gout. The anemia may be microcytic or normocytic, with basophilic stippling on the smear. Lead inhibits heme synthesis, including ferrochelatase, so zinc protoporphyrin rises. Confirm with a venous blood lead level, because fingerstick samples are easily contaminated. Removing the exposure comes first. Chelation is reserved for severe toxicity, and encephalopathy is an emergency.

8. **Oncology**. The classic renal cell carcinoma triad of hematuria, flank pain, and a palpable mass is present in only a minority; most tumors are now found incidentally on imaging. Paraneoplastic clues include erythrocytosis from erythropoietin, hypercalcemia from PTH-related peptide (or from bone metastases), fever, and Stauffer syndrome. Stauffer syndrome is raised alkaline phosphatase and transaminases with a prolonged prothrombin time and no liver metastases; it reverses after nephrectomy, and its return suggests recurrence. A new left varicocele that does not decompress when the patient lies down suggests the tumor is obstructing the left renal vein, which drains the left gonadal vein. Risk factors are smoking, obesity, hypertension, acquired cystic kidney disease in dialysis patients, and von Hippel-Lindau disease.

9. **Nephrology**. In an anion-gap metabolic acidosis, the delta-delta looks for a second metabolic disorder. Compare the rise in anion gap (measured gap minus about 12) with the fall in bicarbonate (24 minus measured). A ratio between about 1 and 2 fits a pure anion-gap acidosis. Below 1 means bicarbonate has fallen more than the anion gap explains, so there is also a normal-anion-gap acidosis; DKA after saline resuscitation and concurrent diarrhea are common examples. Above 2 means bicarbonate is higher than expected, from a coexisting metabolic alkalosis such as vomiting or diuretics, or from compensation for chronic respiratory acidosis. First correct the anion gap for albumin by adding about 2.5 for every 1 g/dL below 4. Treat the cutoffs as approximate, because the normal anion gap varies between labs.

10. **Neurology**. HINTS is the bedside exam for acute vestibular syndrome: continuous vertigo for hours to days with nystagmus, nausea, and unsteadiness. It was more sensitive than early diffusion-weighted MRI for posterior circulation stroke in the first 48 hours (Kattah, Stroke 2009). Any one of three findings suggests a central cause. The first is a normal head impulse test; in vestibular neuritis it is abnormal, with a corrective saccade. The second is nystagmus that changes direction with gaze or is purely vertical or torsional. The third is vertical skew deviation on alternate cover testing. Being unable to stand or walk unaided is another central red flag. HINTS does not apply to brief, position-triggered vertigo, which is BPPV territory (Dix-Hallpike, then Epley), or to a patient without nystagmus. It is only reliable in trained hands.
