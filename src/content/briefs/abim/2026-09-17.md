1. **Cardiology**. In hypertrophic obstructive cardiomyopathy, the systolic murmur becomes louder when left-ventricular volume falls, as with standing or the strain phase of Valsalva, and softer when preload or afterload rises, as with squatting or sustained handgrip. Initial symptom control is usually with a nonvasodilating beta-blocker; avoid reflexively giving preload-reducing vasodilators to a symptomatic patient with dynamic outflow obstruction. New atrial fibrillation is poorly tolerated and warrants anticoagulation regardless of the usual stroke-risk score.

2. **Pulmonary medicine**. Tension pneumothorax is a clinical diagnosis: acute respiratory distress, unilateral absent breath sounds, hypotension, and obstructive shock in the right setting require immediate decompression. Do not wait for a chest radiograph in an unstable patient. Needle or finger decompression is a temporizing step and must be followed by tube thoracostomy; tracheal deviation is a late and unreliable finding.

3. **Gastroenterology**. Achalasia causes impaired lower-esophageal-sphincter relaxation and absent organized peristalsis, producing dysphagia to both solids and liquids from the outset. Endoscopy is required to exclude a mechanical lesion, and high-resolution manometry confirms the diagnosis; a barium esophagram may show a dilated esophagus with distal “bird-beak” narrowing. Rapid weight loss, short symptom duration, or older age should heighten concern for malignancy-related pseudoachalasia.

4. **Infectious disease**. Fever in an asplenic patient is an emergency because overwhelming infection from encapsulated bacteria can progress within hours. Obtain cultures promptly and give empiric parenteral antibiotics without waiting for results; ceftriaxone is a common backbone, with additional coverage guided by local resistance and clinical severity. Prevention requires pneumococcal, meningococcal, and *Haemophilus influenzae* type b vaccination, but vaccination does not make a febrile episode safe to observe at home.

5. **Rheumatology**. Calcium pyrophosphate deposition disease can mimic gout, rheumatoid arthritis, or osteoarthritis. Rhomboid-shaped, weakly positively birefringent crystals support the diagnosis, while chondrocalcinosis is suggestive but neither required nor specific. In a relatively young patient or one with unusually extensive disease, look for associated hemochromatosis, hyperparathyroidism, hypomagnesemia, and hypothyroidism; crystals still do not exclude concomitant septic arthritis.

6. **Endocrinology**. Central adrenal insufficiency causes cortisol deficiency with low or inappropriately normal adrenocorticotropic hormone, but aldosterone is usually preserved because it is regulated mainly by the renin-angiotensin system. Therefore hyperkalemia and salt wasting point more strongly toward primary adrenal failure. When both adrenal and thyroid axes are deficient, replace glucocorticoid before thyroid hormone because levothyroxine can precipitate adrenal crisis.

7. **Hematology**. Acquired hemophilia A presents with new spontaneous soft-tissue, mucosal, gastrointestinal, or postpartum bleeding in a patient without a personal or family bleeding history. The activated partial thromboplastin time is isolatedly prolonged and fails to correct on a mixing study because an autoantibody inhibits factor VIII. Control major bleeding with a bypassing agent or recombinant porcine factor VIII and begin inhibitor-eradication therapy; do not treat it like congenital hemophilia with routine human factor VIII alone.

8. **Oncology**. Immune checkpoint inhibitors can inflame almost any organ. Diarrhea suggests colitis, cough or dyspnea suggests pneumonitis, and fatigue or hypotension may reflect hypophysitis, adrenal insufficiency, or thyroid disease rather than cancer progression. Hold immunotherapy and give systemic glucocorticoids for most clinically significant toxicities after excluding important mimics such as infection; endocrine injury often requires permanent hormone replacement even after inflammation resolves.

9. **Nephrology**. Fanconi syndrome is generalized proximal-tubule dysfunction, causing urinary loss of glucose despite normal serum glucose, phosphate, bicarbonate, uric acid, amino acids, and low-molecular-weight proteins. The resulting pattern may include non-anion-gap metabolic acidosis, hypophosphatemia, hypokalemia, and osteomalacia. Acquired causes include monoclonal light chains and drugs such as tenofovir, ifosfamide, and outdated tetracycline; treatment targets the cause and replaces the solutes being lost.

10. **Neurology**. Lambert-Eaton myasthenic syndrome causes proximal weakness, reduced reflexes, and autonomic symptoms such as dry mouth; strength and reflexes may transiently improve with repeated activation. This presynaptic calcium-channel disorder is strongly associated with small-cell lung cancer, so diagnosis should trigger a malignancy search. Unlike myasthenia gravis, ocular and bulbar symptoms are usually less prominent; amifampridine is the principal symptomatic therapy, with cancer treatment and immunotherapy when appropriate.
