1. **Cardiology**. Peripartum cardiomyopathy is a diagnosis of exclusion defined by a new LVEF below 45% with no other identifiable cause, arising toward the end of pregnancy or in the months after delivery. The management trap is drug selection by trimester: ACE inhibitors, ARBs, ARNI, and mineralocorticoid antagonists are all contraindicated in pregnancy, so an undelivered patient gets hydralazine plus nitrates with a beta-blocker, and the full guideline-directed regimen starts after delivery — enalapril and captopril are compatible with breastfeeding. Anticoagulate when the ejection fraction is severely reduced, since the thrombotic risk of the peripartum state compounds a dilated ventricle. Bromocriptine is biologically plausible and used in some European centers but remains unproven and is not standard US care. Continue therapy for at least a year after the ejection fraction normalizes, and counsel that a subsequent pregnancy carries substantial risk of relapse — highest, and potentially prohibitive, when the ejection fraction has not recovered.

2. **Pulmonary**. Reading pulmonary function tests: obstruction is a reduced FEV1/FVC ratio, and the severity of obstruction is then graded by the FEV1 percent predicted, not by the ratio. Restriction cannot be diagnosed from spirometry alone — a low FVC with a normal ratio only suggests it, and you need a reduced total lung capacity on lung volumes to confirm. The DLCO is what separates the differential within each pattern. Obstruction with a low DLCO is emphysema; obstruction with a normal or high DLCO is asthma or chronic bronchitis. Restriction with a low DLCO is interstitial lung disease; restriction with a preserved DLCO points to the chest wall, neuromuscular weakness, or obesity, where the lung parenchyma is normal. An isolated low DLCO with normal spirometry and normal volumes is the classic pulmonary vascular pattern — pulmonary arterial hypertension or chronic thromboembolic disease — though early emphysema and early ILD can look the same.

3. **Gastroenterology**. PBC versus PSC. Primary biliary cholangitis: middle-aged woman, fatigue and pruritus, cholestatic alkaline phosphatase, antimitochondrial antibody positive in the large majority, normal bile ducts on imaging. Ursodeoxycholic acid is genuinely disease-modifying and improves transplant-free survival, with obeticholic acid or a fibrate as second-line for incomplete responders; screen for osteoporosis and fat-soluble vitamin deficiency. Primary sclerosing cholangitis: younger, more often male, AMA-negative, and tied to inflammatory bowel disease in most cases. Diagnosis is by MRCP showing multifocal strictures and beading, and the key negative is that ursodiol is not recommended — high-dose ursodiol caused harm in trial. PSC carries a real cholangiocarcinoma risk warranting ongoing surveillance, and PSC with colitis carries a high enough colorectal cancer risk to justify annual surveillance colonoscopy. Transplant is the only definitive therapy.

4. **Infectious disease**. Hepatitis C is now a screen-once, treat-everyone disease. USPSTF recommends one-time screening in all adults aged 18 to 79, and screening in each pregnancy. A reactive anti-HCV antibody means nothing on its own: confirm with HCV RNA, because the antibody stays positive after spontaneous or treated clearance. Pan-genotypic direct-acting antivirals — glecaprevir/pibrentasvir or sofosbuvir/velpatasvir — cure well over 90% in 8 to 12 weeks, and genotyping is no longer required for most patients. Treatment is indicated regardless of fibrosis stage; there is no longer a threshold of liver damage to reach. The point most often missed: sustained virologic response does not end surveillance in a patient who already has cirrhosis, who still needs hepatocellular carcinoma screening every 6 months indefinitely.

5. **Rheumatology**. Lupus nephritis: biopsy drives therapy, so any patient with SLE and meaningful proteinuria — roughly 0.5 g/day or more — needs a kidney biopsy, because the clinical picture does not reliably predict the histologic class. Hydroxychloroquine goes to everyone with lupus unless contraindicated. For active proliferative disease (class III or IV), KDIGO 2024 lists several acceptable first-line regimens, all with glucocorticoids: mycophenolate, or low-dose IV cyclophosphamide on the Euro-Lupus schedule, or belimumab added to either of those, or mycophenolate plus a calcineurin inhibitor such as voclosporin when kidney function is not severely impaired. The exam-relevant edges are the outliers: pure class V membranous lupus with nephrotic-range proteinuria warrants immunosuppression, while class VI is globally sclerotic burnt-out disease where immunosuppression only adds toxicity and the treatment is supportive kidney care.

6. **Endocrinology**. Hypotonic polyuria, now renamed: what used to be central diabetes insipidus is AVP deficiency and nephrogenic DI is AVP resistance. Establish the syndrome first with a high urine volume and an inappropriately dilute urine, then separate the three causes. On the classic water deprivation test, AVP deficiency shows a substantial rise in urine osmolality after desmopressin, AVP resistance shows little response to desmopressin, and primary polydipsia concentrates with deprivation alone — but the test misclassifies partial forms often enough that stimulated copeptin measurement, particularly hypertonic saline–stimulated copeptin, has been shown to be more accurate and is the modern approach where available. Reflexively check the reversible causes of AVP resistance before committing: lithium, hypercalcemia, and hypokalemia.

7. **Hematology**. Paroxysmal nocturnal hemoglobinuria is complement-mediated intravascular hemolysis from loss of the GPI-anchored complement regulators CD55 and CD59. Suspect it in Coombs-negative hemolysis with a high LDH and a low haptoglobin, in thrombosis at unusual sites — hepatic, portal, mesenteric, or cerebral veins — and in aplastic anemia or MDS, where subclinical clones are common. Diagnosis is flow cytometry showing GPI-anchor deficiency on red cells and granulocytes; the old Ham and sucrose lysis tests are obsolete. Thrombosis, not anemia, is the leading cause of death. Terminal complement inhibition with eculizumab or ravulizumab controls intravascular hemolysis and thrombotic risk but mandates meningococcal vaccination beforehand, with antibiotic cover until immunity develops, because blocking C5 confers a large Neisseria risk. Persistent anemia on a C5 inhibitor is usually C3-mediated extravascular hemolysis, now addressable with proximal inhibitors such as pegcetacoplan or iptacopan.

8. **Oncology**. Cervical cancer screening. Screening begins at 21 regardless of sexual history or HPV vaccination status, and ages 21 to 29 are screened with cytology alone every 3 years — HPV testing is not used in this age group because transient infection is near-universal. From 30 to 65 the accepted options are cytology every 3 years, primary high-risk HPV testing every 5 years, or cotesting every 5 years, with recent US guidance moving toward primary HPV testing every 5 years as the preferred strategy and endorsing patient self-collected samples; this piece has changed recently. Stop at 65 only if prior screening was adequate and negative. Two commonly missed rules: HPV vaccination does not change the screening schedule, and a woman who has had a total hysterectomy for benign disease with no history of CIN 2 or worse does not need any further screening.

9. **Nephrology**. Recurrent kidney stones: get a 24-hour urine collection measuring volume, calcium, oxalate, citrate, uric acid, sodium, and pH, because targeted therapy depends on it. The counterintuitive dietary point the exam loves is that dietary calcium should NOT be restricted — a normal-calcium, low-sodium, moderate-protein diet outperformed a low-calcium diet for preventing recurrence in hypercalciuric stone formers, because dietary calcium binds oxalate in the gut and reduces oxalate absorption. Fluid intake should be enough to produce more than about 2.5 L of urine daily. Thiazides for hypercalciuria; potassium citrate for hypocitraturia and for uric acid stones, where urinary alkalinization can actually dissolve the stone; allopurinol for hyperuricosuric calcium oxalate disease. Recognize the special stones: struvite staghorn calculi from urease-producing organisms such as Proteus require complete surgical clearance because antibiotics alone will not sterilize them, and hexagonal crystals mean cystinuria. Tamsulosin as medical expulsive therapy helps mainly for larger distal ureteral stones.

10. **Neurology**. Multiple sclerosis diagnosis was substantially revised: the 2024 revisions of the McDonald criteria, published in The Lancet Neurology in September 2025, are the first update since 2017. Three changes matter. The optic nerve is now accepted as a fifth anatomical region for demonstrating dissemination in space, alongside periventricular, cortical/juxtacortical, infratentorial, and spinal cord. CSF kappa free light chains are accepted as a biomarker on par with oligoclonal bands. And novel MRI markers — the central vein sign and paramagnetic rim lesions — can supply supporting specificity when the diagnosis is uncertain, which matters mostly for avoiding misdiagnosis in patients with nonspecific white matter disease. The net effect is a more unified framework that permits diagnosis without waiting for a second clinical attack in more situations. Treatment principles are unchanged: IV methylprednisolone speeds recovery from an acute relapse without altering long-term disability, and the durable benefit comes from starting disease-modifying therapy early. Before treating, exclude NMOSD by testing aquaporin-4 IgG, since several MS drugs make that disease worse.
