1. **Cardiology**. Torsades de pointes is polymorphic ventricular tachycardia in the setting of a prolonged QT interval. Stop QT-prolonging drugs, correct potassium and magnesium, and give intravenous magnesium even when the serum magnesium is normal. If recurrent episodes are pause-dependent despite magnesium, increase the heart rate with overdrive pacing or isoproterenol in acquired long-QT syndrome. An unstable or pulseless patient needs immediate unsynchronized defibrillation; amiodarone can further prolong QT and is the wrong reflex.

2. **Pulmonary**. In an acute COPD exacerbation, controlled oxygen is safer than indiscriminate high-flow oxygen in a patient at risk for hypercapnic respiratory failure. Titrate oxygen to a saturation of 88–92% while obtaining a blood gas; worsening acidosis with persistent hypercapnia despite initial medical treatment is an indication for noninvasive ventilation. Noninvasive ventilation reduces intubation and mortality, but inability to protect the airway, severe hemodynamic instability, or immediate need for intubation makes it inappropriate.

3. **Gastroenterology**. Diagnose spontaneous bacterial peritonitis when ascitic fluid contains at least 250 polymorphonuclear cells/mm³; do not wait for the culture. Treat promptly with an appropriate third-generation cephalosporin and give intravenous albumin when renal dysfunction or substantial hyperbilirubinemia raises the risk of hepatorenal syndrome. A very high ascitic protein, low glucose, multiple organisms, or failure of the neutrophil count to fall should raise concern for secondary peritonitis and prompt abdominal imaging and surgical evaluation.

4. **Infectious disease**. Suspected bacterial meningitis is a treat-first emergency: obtain blood cultures and perform lumbar puncture promptly, but do not delay antibiotics for CT or a difficult procedure. Empiric therapy for an adult is vancomycin plus a third-generation cephalosporin; add ampicillin when Listeria coverage is needed, especially in older or immunocompromised adults. Give dexamethasone before or with the first antibiotic dose when pneumococcal meningitis is possible; benefit is unlikely if it is started after antibiotics.

5. **Rheumatology**. New headache, jaw claudication, scalp tenderness, visual symptoms, or polymyalgia rheumatica in an adult over 50 should trigger concern for giant cell arteritis. Start glucocorticoids immediately when suspicion is high; treatment must not wait for temporal-artery biopsy or vascular imaging because visual loss can become permanent. Intravenous pulse glucocorticoid is used for threatened or established visual loss. Biopsy can remain diagnostic after treatment begins, and ultrasound or large-vessel imaging can support the diagnosis.

6. **Endocrinology**. In hypercalcemia, an elevated or even inappropriately normal parathyroid hormone level means the process is PTH-mediated; malignancy-associated hypercalcemia should suppress PTH. Primary hyperparathyroidism usually has higher urinary calcium, whereas familial hypocalciuric hypercalcemia causes lifelong mild hypercalcemia with low urinary calcium and does not improve with parathyroid surgery. Thiazides, lithium, kidney disease, and vitamin D status can distort the urinary-calcium distinction, so correct or account for them before labeling FHH.

7. **Hematology**. Suspected immune thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura is thrombocytopenia plus microangiopathic hemolytic anemia without another clear cause; neurologic, renal, or fever findings may be absent. Draw ADAMTS13 activity before therapy, but start plasma exchange and glucocorticoids immediately rather than waiting for the result; severe deficiency supports the diagnosis. Add caplacizumab early when immune TTP is strongly suspected and rituximab to suppress the autoantibody. Platelet transfusion is generally avoided unless bleeding is life-threatening.

8. **Oncology**. Superior vena cava syndrome from malignancy usually causes progressive facial or upper-extremity swelling, venous distention, cough, and dyspnea. In a stable patient without airway compromise or cerebral edema, obtain tissue diagnosis before radiation or chemotherapy because treatment depends on tumor histology. Endovascular stenting provides the fastest relief for severe symptoms and can be performed before a final cancer diagnosis. Elevate the head and treat the underlying tumor; anticoagulate when catheter-associated or bland thrombosis is present.

9. **Nephrology**. SIADH causes hypotonic hyponatremia with inappropriately concentrated urine and persistent urinary sodium excretion in a clinically euvolemic patient. The diagnosis requires exclusion of adrenal insufficiency, hypothyroidism, kidney failure, recent diuretic use, and other causes of impaired water excretion. Fluid restriction treats most chronic cases, but severe neurologic symptoms require hypertonic saline. Correct chronic hyponatremia cautiously and monitor frequently because overly rapid correction can cause osmotic demyelination; desmopressin can halt an unintended water diuresis.

10. **Neurology**. A convulsive seizure lasting 5 minutes, or recurrent seizures without recovery, is status epilepticus and should be treated immediately. Give an adequate dose of a benzodiazepine first, then promptly load a longer-acting antiseizure drug such as levetiracetam, fosphenytoin, or valproate; no one of these second-line agents is clearly superior. Check glucose and correct reversible triggers in parallel. Persistent seizures after a benzodiazepine and second-line drug require airway control, continuous EEG, and anesthetic therapy for refractory status.
