1. **Cardiology**. Constrictive pericarditis and restrictive cardiomyopathy can both cause right-sided heart failure with preserved ejection fraction. In constriction, ventricular interdependence produces discordant left- and right-ventricular systolic pressure changes with respiration, and the medial mitral annular tissue velocity is often preserved or greater than the lateral velocity. Restriction more often produces concordant respiratory pressure changes and reduced tissue velocities. Pericardial thickening supports constriction, but normal thickness does not exclude it; cardiac catheterization is useful when noninvasive testing is inconclusive.

2. **Pulmonary medicine**. A pleural effusion in a patient with heart failure may satisfy exudative criteria after diuresis because fluid removal concentrates pleural protein and lactate dehydrogenase. When the clinical picture still favors heart failure, a serum-to-pleural-fluid albumin gradient or pleural-fluid natriuretic peptide can identify this “pseudoexudate.” Do not label every post-diuresis exudate as infection or malignancy without reconciling it with the pretest probability.

3. **Gastroenterology**. In a patient with cirrhosis and ascites, an ascitic-fluid polymorphonuclear leukocyte count of at least 250 cells/mm³ establishes spontaneous bacterial peritonitis and warrants immediate empiric antibiotics; a positive culture is not required. Perform diagnostic paracentesis promptly for hospital admission, gastrointestinal bleeding, encephalopathy, kidney injury, fever, abdominal symptoms, or clinical deterioration. Intravenous albumin reduces kidney failure and mortality in higher-risk disease, while secondary antibiotic prophylaxis is indicated after recovery.

4. **Infectious disease**. Diabetic foot osteomyelitis is suggested by an ulcer that probes to bone, exposed bone, a persistently elevated inflammatory marker, or characteristic imaging, but none of these findings alone is definitive. Plain radiographs are the initial study; MRI is preferred when uncertainty remains. When microbiologic diagnosis will change therapy, culture bone rather than a superficial wound swab, because surface organisms often do not represent the pathogen in bone. Antibiotics alone may be reasonable for selected forefoot disease without urgent need for drainage, peripheral arterial disease, or exposed bone.

5. **Rheumatology**. Antiphospholipid syndrome requires a compatible clinical event, such as thrombosis or defined pregnancy morbidity, plus persistent antiphospholipid antibodies; a single positive test does not establish the diagnosis. Lupus anticoagulant prolongs phospholipid-dependent clotting assays yet predicts thrombosis in vivo, not bleeding. For thrombotic disease, a vitamin K antagonist remains standard long-term therapy; direct oral anticoagulants should be avoided in high-risk patients, especially those positive for all three antibody types or with arterial thrombosis.

6. **Endocrinology**. A very large pituitary mass with only modestly elevated prolactin may reflect the immunoassay “hook effect,” in which extreme antigen excess produces a falsely low result. Ask the laboratory to repeat prolactin on a diluted sample before diagnosing a nonfunctioning macroadenoma with stalk effect. Also exclude pregnancy, primary hypothyroidism, kidney disease, and dopamine-antagonist drugs. True prolactinoma is usually treated first with a dopamine agonist, even when the tumor is large.

7. **Hematology**. Warm autoimmune hemolytic anemia usually produces extravascular hemolysis with anemia, reticulocytosis, elevated lactate dehydrogenase and indirect bilirubin, low haptoglobin, and spherocytes. The direct antiglobulin test is typically positive for IgG, with or without complement. Glucocorticoids are first-line treatment; transfusion should not be withheld from a patient with severe symptomatic anemia merely because compatible crossmatching is difficult, since the blood bank can select the safest available units while evaluating for alloantibodies.

8. **Oncology**. Malignant pericardial effusion may present with dyspnea, tachycardia, elevated jugular venous pressure, or tamponade physiology, and the size of the effusion alone does not determine hemodynamic severity. An unstable patient requires urgent drainage. In a stable patient, pericardiocentesis can provide both relief and cytology, but recurrent malignant effusions often require a surgical window or another durable drainage strategy; systemic cancer therapy is adjunctive and does not replace emergency decompression.

9. **Nephrology**. Type 4 renal tubular acidosis is a hyperkalemic normal-anion-gap metabolic acidosis caused by aldosterone deficiency or resistance, commonly from diabetic kidney disease, renin-angiotensin-system blockers, potassium-sparing diuretics, trimethoprim, or heparin. Unlike distal acidification defects, the central clue is hyperkalemia, which itself suppresses renal ammonium excretion. Treatment begins with removing contributing drugs when possible and correcting hyperkalemia; selected patients may benefit from a loop or thiazide diuretic, alkali, or mineralocorticoid therapy.

10. **Neurology**. Normal-pressure hydrocephalus causes a progressive gait disorder, cognitive impairment, and urinary urgency or incontinence with ventriculomegaly out of proportion to cortical atrophy. Gait impairment is usually the earliest and most shunt-responsive feature; isolated dementia without a gait disorder should prompt another diagnosis. Improvement after removal of cerebrospinal fluid by a large-volume lumbar tap or temporary lumbar drainage supports potential benefit from ventriculoperitoneal shunting, but failure to improve does not absolutely exclude responsiveness.
