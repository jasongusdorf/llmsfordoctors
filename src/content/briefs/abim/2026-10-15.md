1. **Cardiology**. Rheumatic mitral stenosis decompensates when the heart rate rises, because tachycardia shortens diastolic filling time across the narrowed valve. That is why pregnancy, new atrial fibrillation, fever, and thyrotoxicosis so often unmask it, and why rate control is the first move. On exam, listen for a loud S1, an opening snap, and a low-pitched diastolic rumble; the shorter the S2-to-opening-snap interval, the more severe the stenosis. Severe stenosis in the 2020 ACC/AHA guideline is a valve area of 1.5 cm² or less. Symptomatic severe stenosis with favorable valve morphology, no left atrial thrombus, and no more than mild mitral regurgitation goes to percutaneous balloon mitral commissurotomy, after a transesophageal echo to exclude thrombus. Unfavorable anatomy goes to surgery.

2. **Pulmonary medicine**. Suspect methemoglobinemia when pulse oximetry sits near 85% and does not improve with supplemental oxygen, yet the arterial PaO2 is normal. That mismatch is the saturation gap, and the blood may look chocolate brown. Confirm with co-oximetry. Common culprits are topical benzocaine or other local anesthetic sprays, dapsone, nitrites, primaquine, and rasburicase. Symptomatic patients get IV methylene blue, usually 1 to 2 mg/kg. It is contraindicated in G6PD deficiency, where it does not work and can cause hemolysis, so the alternatives there are ascorbic acid, exchange transfusion, or hyperbaric oxygen. Methylene blue is also a monoamine oxidase inhibitor and can trigger serotonin syndrome in patients on serotonergic drugs. Dapsone has a long half-life, so watch for rebound after the first dose.

3. **Gastroenterology**. Acute liver failure means coagulopathy (INR at least 1.5) plus any degree of encephalopathy, in a patient without preexisting cirrhosis, with illness lasting less than 26 weeks. Wilson disease, autoimmune hepatitis, and vertically acquired hepatitis B are exceptions: they can qualify even when cirrhosis is already present. Contact a transplant center early, while the patient is still well enough to move, rather than waiting for deep coma. N-acetylcysteine is standard for acetaminophen toxicity. In a randomized trial (Lee et al., 2009) it also improved transplant-free survival in non-acetaminophen acute liver failure, but only in patients with early-grade (I to II) encephalopathy, so give it early in those cases as well. Check glucose frequently, because hypoglycemia is common.

4. **Infectious disease**. Necrotizing soft-tissue infection starts with pain out of proportion to a skin exam that may look nearly normal. Bullae, dusky or anesthetic skin, crepitus, and systemic toxicity come later. It is a surgical diagnosis: the next step is urgent exploration and debridement. Imaging should not delay the operating room, and a low LRINEC score does not rule out the infection. Start empiric broad-spectrum therapy: vancomycin or linezolid, plus piperacillin-tazobactam or a carbapenem, plus clindamycin. Clindamycin is there to suppress toxin production in streptococcal and clostridial infection, not for its spectrum. Expect repeat debridement within about a day.

5. **Rheumatology**. Raynaud phenomenon is primary when it starts at a young age, affects both hands symmetrically, causes no digital ulcers or pitting, and comes with normal nailfold capillaries and a negative ANA. Red flags for secondary Raynaud are onset later in adulthood, asymmetry, digital ulcers or pits, enlarged or dropped-out nailfold capillary loops, and positive ANA or scleroderma-specific antibodies. Raynaud is present in nearly all patients with systemic sclerosis and is often its first sign. Treatment starts with avoiding cold, stopping smoking, and stopping vasoconstrictors. A dihydropyridine calcium-channel blocker is first-line drug therapy, and PDE-5 inhibitors come next. Critical digital ischemia needs urgent IV prostanoid therapy and an evaluation for vascular or embolic causes.

6. **Endocrinology**. Hyperosmolar hyperglycemic state differs from DKA in degree and tempo. Glucose is usually above 600 mg/dL, effective osmolality is very high, and significant ketoacidosis is absent. The fluid deficit is larger, mortality is higher, and altered mental status tracks with osmolality. Fluids come first and alone lower glucose substantially. Start insulin only once potassium is at least 3.3 mEq/L, the same rule as in DKA. Bring osmolality down gradually and add dextrose as glucose falls, because a rapid osmolar shift risks cerebral edema. The 2024 ADA-led consensus report revised the DKA and HHS diagnostic criteria. Overlap DKA-HHS is common, so measure ketones and the anion gap rather than assuming which one you have.

7. **Hematology**. Essential thrombocythemia is a diagnosis of exclusion. First rule out reactive thrombocytosis (iron deficiency, inflammation, infection, prior splenectomy) and check BCR-ABL, because CML can present with an isolated high platelet count. Most patients carry a JAK2, CALR, or MPL driver mutation, and a minority are triple-negative. Thrombotic risk, not the platelet count, drives cytoreduction. Age over 60 or a prior thrombosis means high risk, which gets hydroxyurea plus aspirin; lower-risk patients are often managed with aspirin alone, especially if JAK2-positive. Extreme thrombocytosis, above about 1,000 × 10⁹/L, can cause acquired von Willebrand syndrome and bleeding, so check ristocetin cofactor activity before giving aspirin.

8. **Oncology**. Stage III (node-positive) colon cancer gets adjuvant fluoropyrimidine plus oxaliplatin. The IDEA pooled analysis (2018) showed that 3 months of CAPOX is noninferior to 6 months for low-risk stage III (T1 to T3 and N1), with much less cumulative oxaliplatin neuropathy. High-risk stage III (T4 or N2) still generally gets 6 months. Stage II is different: adjuvant therapy is considered only with high-risk features. Mismatch-repair-deficient (MSI-high) stage II tumors have a good prognosis and do not benefit from fluoropyrimidine monotherapy, which is one reason MMR status is checked on every tumor.

9. **Nephrology**. Metformin dosing follows eGFR, per the 2016 FDA labeling. It is contraindicated below 30 mL/min/1.73 m². Starting it at an eGFR of 30 to 45 is not recommended, and if eGFR falls below 45 on therapy, reassess the benefit against the risk. For iodinated contrast, hold metformin at or before the study in patients with an eGFR of 30 to 60, liver disease, alcohol use disorder, or heart failure, or when contrast is given intra-arterially. Recheck eGFR about 48 hours later before restarting. Metformin-associated lactic acidosis is rare and usually needs a second insult, such as hypoperfusion, sepsis, or hepatic failure. The more common long-term problem is vitamin B12 deficiency, so check B12 periodically, especially if neuropathy develops.

10. **Neurology**. Cervical artery dissection is a leading cause of stroke in young adults. Carotid dissection presents with ipsilateral neck, face, or head pain and a partial Horner syndrome (ptosis and miosis, usually without facial anhidrosis, because the facial sweat fibers travel with the external carotid). It may also cause pulsatile tinnitus or a lower cranial nerve palsy. Vertebral dissection presents with posterior neck or occipital pain and posterior-circulation ischemia. Triggers include minor trauma and neck manipulation, though many cases are spontaneous. Diagnose with CTA or MRA; fat-suppressed MRI can show the intramural hematoma. Treat with antithrombotic therapy. Antiplatelet therapy and anticoagulation are both acceptable, and neither CADISS nor TREAT-CAD settled which is better. Extracranial dissection is not by itself a contraindication to IV thrombolysis.
