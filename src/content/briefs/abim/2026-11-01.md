1. **Cardiology**. In a hypertensive emergency (severe hypertension with acute target-organ damage), do not drive the pressure to normal. The 2017 ACC/AHA guideline advises lowering systolic pressure by no more than 25% in the first hour, then to about 160/100–110 mm Hg over the next 2 to 6 hours, then toward normal over 24 to 48 hours, because autoregulation has shifted and abrupt normalisation can cause cerebral, coronary, or renal ischaemia. The exceptions go faster: aortic dissection (systolic below 120 mm Hg within the first hour), and severe preeclampsia/eclampsia or pheochromocytoma crisis (below 140 mm Hg in the first hour). Acute stroke has its own thresholds. Severe hypertension without organ damage is not an emergency: oral therapy and close follow-up, no IV drips, and never sublingual nifedipine.

2. **Pulmonary medicine**. Every pneumonia with more than a trivial effusion needs a diagnostic tap, because the fluid decides whether a chest tube is needed. Drain when the fluid is frank pus, when Gram stain or culture is positive, when pleural pH is below 7.20 or glucose is below 60 mg/dL, or when the effusion is loculated or occupies half the hemithorax or more. Measure pH in a blood gas analyzer, collected anaerobically: air in the syringe falsely raises it and residual lidocaine falsely lowers it. Do not bother checking pH on frank pus. For loculated pleural infection that drains poorly, MIST2 showed that intrapleural tPA plus DNase together, not either alone, improved drainage and reduced surgical referral.

3. **Gastroenterology**. Microscopic colitis causes chronic, watery, non-bloody diarrhea, classically in older women, with a colon that looks normal at colonoscopy. The diagnosis is histologic (lymphocytic or collagenous colitis), so biopsies must be taken from the right and left colon even when the mucosa looks normal. Review medications first: NSAIDs, proton-pump inhibitors, and SSRIs are the classic associations, along with smoking. Screen for coexisting celiac disease, which is associated. Budesonide is the induction therapy of choice (AGA 2016); relapse after stopping is common, and loperamide alone is reasonable only for mild disease.

4. **Infectious disease**. *Candida* in a blood culture is never a contaminant. IDSA 2016 management: start an echinocandin; remove central venous catheters when it is safe to do so; repeat blood cultures daily or every other day until clear; and treat for 2 weeks after the first negative culture and resolution of symptoms if there are no metastatic complications. Step down to fluconazole after about 5 to 7 days if the isolate is susceptible and the patient is stable with cleared cultures. IDSA advises a dilated eye exam, ideally within the first week in nonneutropenic patients, because endophthalmitis changes the drug and duration (routine screening is debated by some ophthalmologists). By contrast, *Candida* in sputum almost never means pneumonia, and asymptomatic candiduria is not treated except in neutropenia or before a urologic procedure.

5. **Rheumatology**. Adult-onset Still disease is the fever-of-unknown-origin answer when you see daily (quotidian) spiking fevers to 39 °C or higher, an evanescent salmon-coloured rash that appears with the fever, arthralgia or arthritis, sore throat, and a neutrophilic leukocytosis, with negative ANA and rheumatoid factor. Ferritin is often strikingly high, but that is nonspecific; a low glycosylated ferritin fraction (20% or less) is more specific. It remains a diagnosis of exclusion under the Yamaguchi criteria, so infection, lymphoma, and other causes must be ruled out first. Watch for macrophage activation syndrome. Treatment escalates from NSAIDs and glucocorticoids to IL-1 blockade (anakinra, canakinumab) or IL-6 blockade.

6. **Endocrinology**. Diagnose male hypogonadism only with symptoms plus a low morning, fasting total testosterone confirmed on a second occasion (Endocrine Society 2018); do not screen asymptomatic men or test in the afternoon or during acute illness. Then check LH and FSH: high means primary testicular failure, low or inappropriately normal means secondary, which warrants prolactin, iron studies, and pituitary evaluation as appropriate. Exogenous testosterone suppresses spermatogenesis, so it is the wrong drug for a man who wants fertility soon. Do not start with hematocrit above 50%, and stop if it rises above 54%. TRAVERSE (2023) found no increase in major cardiovascular events in older men at high cardiovascular risk, but more atrial fibrillation, acute kidney injury, and pulmonary embolism.

7. **Hematology**. Most patients on a DOAC need neither heparin bridging nor drug-level testing before surgery. In PAUSE (2019), apixaban and rivaroxaban were held 1 day before a low-bleeding-risk procedure and 2 days before a high-bleeding-risk procedure, and resumed 1 day after low-risk or 2 to 3 days after high-risk surgery, with low rates of major bleeding and arterial thromboembolism. Dabigatran needs longer when creatinine clearance is below 50 mL/min (2 and 4 days). The key distinction is warfarin in a patient with a mechanical mitral valve or other very high thrombotic risk, who still gets bridged; DOACs are contraindicated in mechanical valves to begin with.

8. **Oncology**. Neutropenic fever is a single oral temperature of 38.3 °C or higher, or 38.0 °C sustained over an hour, with an absolute neutrophil count below 500/µL or expected to fall below 500 within 48 hours. Start an antipseudomonal beta-lactam (cefepime, piperacillin-tazobactam, or a carbapenem) within an hour of presentation, before the workup is complete. Vancomycin is not routine: add it for hemodynamic instability, suspected catheter or skin and soft-tissue infection, pneumonia, or known MRSA colonisation. Patients with a MASCC score of 21 or higher are low risk and, if they can be followed reliably, can be treated as outpatients with oral ciprofloxacin plus amoxicillin-clavulanate.

9. **Nephrology**. Suspect rhabdomyolysis when the urine dipstick is strongly positive for blood but microscopy shows few or no red cells: the heme is myoglobin. CK above five times the upper limit confirms muscle injury. Look for hyperkalemia, hyperphosphatemia, high anion gap acidosis, and early hypocalcemia from calcium deposition in damaged muscle. Do not replace that calcium unless the patient is symptomatic or has hyperkalemic ECG changes, because it is released back during recovery and causes rebound hypercalcemia. The treatment is early, generous isotonic fluid; bicarbonate and mannitol have no proven benefit. Remember drug causes (statins, especially with gemfibrozil; colchicine), hypokalemia and hypophosphatemia, and compartment syndrome.

10. **Neurology**. In facial palsy, the forehead is the localiser. A peripheral (lower motor neuron) lesion such as Bell palsy weakens the whole half of the face, including the forehead. Forehead sparing points to a central lesion and needs stroke workup. For Bell palsy, start oral glucocorticoids within 72 hours (Sullivan 2007, NEJM, prednisolone improved recovery and acyclovir added nothing). Antivirals alone are not recommended, and adding them to steroids is optional at most. Routine labs and imaging are not needed for a typical case, but protect the eye. Bilateral facial palsy is not Bell palsy: think Lyme disease, Guillain-Barré syndrome, sarcoidosis, and HIV.
