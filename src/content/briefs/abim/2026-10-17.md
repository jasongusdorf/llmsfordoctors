1. **Cardiology**. Atrial fibrillation in a patient with Wolff-Parkinson-White syndrome looks like an irregular, very fast, wide-complex tachycardia whose QRS width and morphology vary from beat to beat, because conduction runs down the accessory pathway to different degrees. Do not give AV nodal blockers: the ACC/AHA/HRS guidelines list intravenous digoxin or amiodarone and intravenous or oral beta-blockers, diltiazem, and verapamil as potentially harmful, and adenosine is avoided for the same reason, since slowing the AV node pushes more conduction down the pathway and can precipitate ventricular fibrillation. Use synchronized cardioversion if the patient is unstable and intravenous procainamide or ibutilide if stable. Catheter ablation of the pathway is the definitive treatment.

2. **Pulmonary medicine**. Hypersensitivity pneumonitis is an immune reaction to an inhaled antigen (birds and feathers, mold in humidifiers or water-damaged homes, hot tubs, farm dust), and the most important treatment is identifying and removing the exposure; no antigen is found in a large share of cases. The 2020 ATS/JRS/ALAT guideline classifies disease as nonfibrotic or fibrotic, and fibrosis on CT carries a much worse prognosis. Supportive clues are centrilobular nodules, ground glass, and mosaic attenuation with air trapping on expiratory CT, plus a lymphocytosis on bronchoalveolar lavage. Glucocorticoids are used for nonfibrotic disease that persists after avoidance, and nintedanib slowed lung-function decline in progressive fibrosing ILD, including fibrotic hypersensitivity pneumonitis, in INBUILD (2019).

3. **Gastroenterology**. Eosinophilic esophagitis is the classic cause of solid-food dysphagia and food impaction in a young man with atopy. Endoscopy may show rings, linear furrows, white exudates, or a narrow-caliber esophagus, but can look normal, so take multiple biopsies (current guidance suggests at least six, from proximal and distal esophagus); the histologic threshold is at least 15 eosinophils per high-power field. A response to a proton-pump inhibitor no longer excludes the diagnosis; PPIs are now a first-line treatment, alongside swallowed topical glucocorticoids, elimination diets, and dupilumab. Dilation treats strictures but not the inflammation.

4. **Infectious disease**. Ocular or otic syphilis can occur at any stage, including early infection, and is treated as neurosyphilis: intravenous aqueous crystalline penicillin G, 18 to 24 million units per day, for 10 to 14 days (CDC 2021). Uveitis is the commonest ocular finding, and hearing loss or tinnitus suggests otosyphilis. Under the CDC guidance, a patient with isolated ocular symptoms, reactive serology, and confirmed eye findings does not need a lumbar puncture before treatment, because a normal CSF would not change management. Do not substitute benzathine penicillin, which does not reach treponemicidal levels in the CNS. A penicillin-allergic patient should be desensitized when possible, and every patient should be tested for HIV.

5. **Rheumatology**. IgG4-related disease is a fibroinflammatory condition that forms tumor-like masses: type 1 autoimmune pancreatitis (a diffusely enlarged "sausage" pancreas), IgG4-related sclerosing cholangitis, enlarged salivary and lacrimal glands, retroperitoneal fibrosis, and aortitis. An elevated serum IgG4 supports the diagnosis but is neither sensitive nor specific. Biopsy shows a dense lymphoplasmacytic infiltrate rich in IgG4-positive plasma cells, storiform fibrosis, and obliterative phlebitis, and its main job is to exclude the mimics, pancreatic cancer, cholangiocarcinoma, and lymphoma. Disease responds briskly to glucocorticoids but often relapses; B-cell depletion with rituximab or inebilizumab is used for relapse or steroid sparing.

6. **Endocrinology**. Before testing for Cushing syndrome, exclude exogenous glucocorticoids, including inhaled, topical, and injected forms. Then use one or more first-line tests: late-night salivary cortisol (at least two samples), the 1-mg overnight dexamethasone suppression test (a morning cortisol above 1.8 µg/dL is failure to suppress), or 24-hour urinary free cortisol (at least two collections). Oral estrogen raises cortisol-binding globulin and causes false-positive dexamethasone tests, and severe depression, alcohol use disorder, and severe obesity can produce a mild "pseudo-Cushing" picture. Only after hypercortisolism is established, measure ACTH: suppressed ACTH points to an adrenal source; normal or high ACTH means a pituitary or ectopic source.

7. **Hematology**. For major or life-threatening bleeding on warfarin, give four-factor prothrombin complex concentrate plus intravenous vitamin K (5 to 10 mg). PCC reverses the INR within minutes, faster and with far less volume than plasma, while vitamin K sustains the reversal after the PCC wears off. Without bleeding, an INR between 4.5 and 10 is usually managed by holding warfarin, without routine vitamin K; above 10, CHEST guidance suggests adding oral vitamin K. Direct oral anticoagulants have their own antidotes: idarucizumab for dabigatran, and andexanet alfa or four-factor PCC for the factor Xa inhibitors.

8. **Oncology**. Hypercalcemia of malignancy is most often humoral, driven by PTH-related peptide from squamous cell, renal, breast, or other solid tumors, with a suppressed PTH. Lytic bone metastases (myeloma, breast) and calcitriol production by lymphoma account for most other cases. Treat a symptomatic patient with intravenous isotonic saline first, add calcitonin for a fast but short-lived effect (tachyphylaxis within about two days), and give zoledronic acid, whose effect takes two to four days to arrive. Denosumab is used when bisphosphonates fail or kidney function precludes them, and glucocorticoids treat the calcitriol-mediated hypercalcemia of lymphoma. Loop diuretics are not routine and belong only after volume repletion.

9. **Nephrology**. Timing separates IgA nephropathy from post-streptococcal glomerulonephritis. IgA nephropathy causes gross hematuria during or within a few days of an upper respiratory infection ("synpharyngitic"), whereas post-streptococcal disease follows pharyngitis by one to three weeks and a skin infection by several weeks. Complement settles it: C3 is low in post-streptococcal glomerulonephritis and usually normalizes within about eight weeks, while C3 is normal in IgA nephropathy. A low C3 that persists beyond that window should prompt evaluation for C3 glomerulopathy or membranoproliferative glomerulonephritis. Post-streptococcal disease is managed supportively and usually resolves in children; adults fare less well.

10. **Neurology**. In convulsive status epilepticus, give a benzodiazepine within the first five minutes and do not underdose it. Intramuscular midazolam was at least as effective as intravenous lorazepam when given by paramedics in RAMPART (2012), so the lack of IV access should not delay treatment. If seizures continue, the second-line choices are intravenous levetiracetam, fosphenytoin, or valproate, which stopped seizures in about half of patients each, with no meaningful difference between them, in ESETT (2019). Refractory status needs intubation, a continuous anesthetic infusion, and EEG monitoring, because nonconvulsive seizures often persist after visible convulsions stop.
