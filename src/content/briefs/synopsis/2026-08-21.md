## TL;DR

**One real regulatory event, one peer-reviewed commentary, and five preprints, and the preprints all point the same direction: our measurement instruments are worse than we have been pretending.**

- **FDA opened a generative-AI device docket on 18 Aug** (FDA-2026-N-7874, comments due 19 Oct 2026). High confidence
- **NEJM AI ran a Perspective (17 Aug)** arguing that AI safety class is a property of the deployment, not the model, the same model can be S2, S3, or S4 depending on whether a clinician reviews before action. Framework, not data. High confidence
- **The deflationary cluster.** Swap in “none of the above” and four LLMs lose 14–47 points on nephrology board items. ## 1. Regulatory: FDA's Generative-AI Device Discussion Paper

**Date:** 18 August 2026 · **Source grade:** primary agency document · High confidence

The one item this week that will still matter in a year. CDRH's Digital Health Center of Excellence released *Considerations for the Regulation of Generative AI-Enabled Medical Devices* and opened docket **FDA-2026-N-7874**, comments due **19 October 2026**.

The animating idea, verbatim from the press release: the paper

> “discusses a potential approach to premarket evaluation built on the concept of competency assessment, inspired at a high level by how physicians are trained and evaluated, consisting of non-clinical device benchmarking and clinical confirmation.”

That is a genuine conceptual departure. Traditional SaMD review evaluates a locked algorithm against a fixed performance specification. A generative model has no fixed output distribution and an effectively unbounded task surface, so “sensitivity and specificity of the device” stops being a coherent question. The competency framing sidesteps this by asking, in effect: can it pass the boards, and does it perform in supervised practice?

Named leadership: DHCoE Director Rick Abramson, CDRH Director Michelle Tarver, with a quote from Acting Commissioner Kyle Diamantas.

**Read the disclaimers carefully before you over-index on this.** The DHCoE landing page states the paper is “intended for discussion purposes only and does not represent draft or final guidance… not intended to address whether the approaches discussed are within FDA's existing legal authorities.” That second clause is doing enormous work. FDA is floating a pathway while explicitly declining to assert it can build one. No device has been, or can currently be, authorized under this concept.

**Verification caveat, stated plainly:** fda.gov and regulations.gov return 403/404 to automated fetchers. The docket number and deadline were confirmed by cross-matching independent trade editorial (AuntMinnie, MDDI, 24x7) against the wire reprint of the FDA release, and one verifier retrieved the FDA pages directly with a browser user-agent (HTTP 200, verbatim match). No Federal Register notice carries this docket number, expected for a CDRH discussion paper issued outside rulemaking, but it means the deadline has no FR record. **If you intend to file a comment, confirm the date on Regulations.gov yourself.**

### Why an academic hospitalist might actually file

Comment dockets on conceptual frameworks are unusually high-leverage, because there is no entrenched text to argue against yet. Two things in the competency analogy are worth pressure-testing from someone who has actually been assessed this way: (1) physician competency assessment is *longitudinal and supervised*, with graduated autonomy and a named responsible attending, the paper's two-part structure has no obvious analogue for supervision or for withdrawal of privileges; (2) board examination performance is famously a weak predictor of clinical performance, which is precisely the finding the nephrology preprint below reproduces in silico.

## 2. Peer-Reviewed: NEJM AI on Safety Classification

**Date:** 17 August 2026 · **DOI:** 10.1056/AIp2600358 · High confidence (that it says this) · Framework, not evidence

Sheng, Song, Shah, Wu, Car, and Wong propose a four-tier classification of medical AI safety. The premise: existing frameworks under-serve patient safety because AI harms are “delayed, difficult to attribute, and irreversible.”

| Tier | Definition | Governance implication |
|----|----|----|
| **S1** | Narrow, directly testable uses | Conventional performance validation suffices |
| **S2** | Clinician-reviewable decision support | Human review is the safety mechanism |
| **S3** | Harm surfaces only on follow-up | Longitudinal surveillance required *before* wider rollout |
| **S4** | High-risk autonomous action | Restrict or prohibit |

The load-bearing claim, near-verbatim: “The same AI function may remain classified as S2 when used as clinician-reviewed decision support, become S3 when safety depends on referral completion and follow-up outcomes, or approach S4 if used to bypass clinical judgment.” DeepDR-LLM is the worked example.

**The useful implication for you:** this is a direct argument that model-level regulatory clearance is category-incoherent. The identical weights deployed as a suggestion in a note versus as an auto-executed order are different safety objects. That tension sits underneath the FDA paper above, and neither document resolves it.

**Grade it honestly:** this is a Perspective, opinion and framework, not empirical validation. Two details circulating alongside it did *not* survive verification and should be dropped: the gloss that the R1–R3 distinction derives from “decidability theory,” and a purported Tsinghua press release dated 20 Aug (no such release exists). ai.nejm.org blocks automated fetch; confirmation rests on Crossref metadata plus indexed primary text.

## 3. Benchmarks and Evaluation: The Week's Deflationary Cluster

### 3a. The “none of the above” collapse in nephrology

**Posted:** 18 August 2026 · medRxiv Preprint · High confidence in the numbers

Soejima and colleagues took 210 Japanese Society of Nephrology board-renewal questions (2014–2023), had two nephrologists independently screen them, and retained the 145 items where replacing the correct answer with “none of the other answers” made NOTA the sole correct response. Then they re-ran four models.

| Model            | Standard | NOTA-substituted | Drop (95% CI)           | P      |
|------------------|----------|------------------|-------------------------|--------|
| GPT-4o           | 66.21%   | 19.31%           | −46.90 pp (37.93–56.55) | \<.001 |
| Gemini 2.5 Pro   | 86.90%   | 55.86%           | −31.03 pp (22.76–39.31) | \<.001 |
| Gemini 2.0 Flash | 58.62%   | 31.03%           | −27.59 pp (17.93–36.55) | \<.001 |
| GPT-5            | 87.59%   | 73.10%           | −14.48 pp (8.28–21.38)  | \<.001 |

The authors' mechanism: models “may select the most familiar or semantically proximate distractor instead of fully eliminating all remaining choices to reach NOTA.” Their conclusion is appropriately restrained: “Newer models may be more robust, but multiple-choice accuracy remains an incomplete measure of clinical reasoning robustness.”

This converges with prior work, ACL 2025's “Wait, that's not an option,” MedFuzz, ReMedQA, and no contradicting source surfaced.

**Caveats:** preprint; n=145; single specialty; single-run design (no sampling variance); GPT-4o and Gemini 2.0 Flash are prior-generation, so “frontier models fail” overstates it. These were board-style items, not public leaderboards, do not repeat any framing that says leaderboard scores were directly invalidated.

**Why it matters clinically:** real inpatient medicine is structurally NOTA-heavy. The right answer is frequently “none of these, get more data,” and a model that cannot reach that conclusion under pressure has a failure mode that MCQA benchmarks are constitutionally blind to. That GPT-5 still lost 14 points is the number to remember.

### 3b. HealMed: multilingual medical benchmark

**Posted:** 20 August 2026 · arXiv 2608.19981 Preprint · Medium confidence

Roughly 43 authors; 1,000 examples in each of nine languages, drawn from nine datasets, across MCQA, NLI, and open-ended QA. Built by 23 physicians across nine countries over two years, each translation revised by two bilingual experts.

Headline: “The strongest proprietary models were the most stable across languages, whereas many open-source and medically specialized models showed larger and less consistent gaps. Medical specialization alone did not ensure multilingual robustness.” Declines were steepest in low-resource languages, with gap size varying markedly across languages.

**Read the authors' own confound:** they concede “expert revision could either raise or lower measured performance, indicating that translation quality materially affects cross-language evaluation results.” So an unknown share of the measured gap is translation artifact. Any causal reading, “specialization *causes* multilingual fragility”, overreaches. It is also an in-silico QA benchmark, not clinical validation, so it cannot support clinical conclusions regardless of eventual peer-review status.

## 4. Ambient Documentation: Two Preprints Worth Your Attention

### 4a. Language equity in a safety-net system

**Posted:** 17 August 2026 · medRxiv Preprint · High confidence in the reported result

Aldis, Wang, Sage, Metzmaker, and Galvin analyzed 54,160 outpatient encounters at Cambridge Health Alliance. Senior author Hannah Galvin is CHA's Chief Health Information Officer.

**Primary finding, verbatim:** “Non-English encounters were 21%–25% less likely than English encounters to achieve the same performance threshold.” And: “There was no significant difference in generative documentation performance between interpreter-mediated and bilingual provider encounters.”

| Bilingual provider vs interpreter-mediated | Adjusted OR | 95% CI    | p     |
|--------------------------------------------|-------------|-----------|-------|
| Spanish                                    | 0.91        | 0.79–1.06 | 0.230 |
| Portuguese                                 | 1.21        | 0.82–1.79 |,     |
| Haitian Creole                             | 1.00        | 0.83–1.21 |,     |
| Other                                      | 1.00        | 0.49–2.05 |,     |

**The qualifiers here are not optional, several change the interpretation:**

- The “performance threshold” is **≥80% Percent Effort Reduction**, the share of final-note words that were AI-generated and left unedited. That is a documentation-*effort* proxy. It is **not** note accuracy, safety, or clinical quality. A clinician who edits heavily because the note is wrong and one who edits heavily out of habit are indistinguishable in this metric.
- 21–25% is a range of **GEE odds reductions** clustered by patient. Phrase it as “lower odds,” not a risk difference.
- Data span **March 2024 – August 2025**. Model versions are up to two years stale.
- **The vendor is not named in the paper.** CHA is a publicly documented Abridge site, which makes an Abridge inference circumstantial, do not state it as fact.
- No control for visit complexity, provider editing behavior, acoustic environment, specialty, or case mix. And a null is not equivalence; the less-common-language subgroups are likely underpowered.

### 4b. There is no gold standard for scribe errors

**Posted:** 17 August 2026 · medRxiv Preprint · Medium confidence · Vendor-authored

Bergman, Liu, Austin, and Sangera ran a pre-registered, blinded, GRRAS-reported human-validation study: 10 external post-certification GPs adjudicated a stratified sample of 434 pipeline flags (565 adjudications, 131 double-rated).

| Agreement measure | Result |
|----|----|
| Automated judge vs clinician | **64%** (95% CI 60–68) |
| Clinician vs clinician (raw) | **59%** (95% CI 50–67); Gwet's AC1 **0.24** |
| Judge vs clinician consensus | 70% (59–79) |
| Genuine-error rate among flagged items | 68% (94% credible interval 48–83) |

The 94% CrI is the ArviZ Bayesian default, not a typo. The authors' own conclusion: “no gold standard exists”; the right test is “not accuracy but defensibility.”

**Independence failure, stated fully.** All four authors list a single affiliation: Clinical AI Research, **Heidi Health**. Competing interests: “All authors are employees of Heidi Health, which develops the ambient AI scribe and automated judges evaluated in this study.” Funded “in its entirety by Heidi Health,” with sponsor involvement in design, analysis, and manuscript decisions. Underlying data are proprietary and unavailable. The *external clinician raters are* genuinely independent, so read this as vendor-designed/funded/authored rather than as having no independent participants. This is **not** Ambience, it does not satisfy the watchlist.

**The framing tension is worth naming.** The same 64% figure is presented by the vendor as “clinician-equivalent” performance. The skeptical reading, “only 64%”, is equally faithful to the data. Both are true. What is unambiguous is the second-order implication: if clinicians agree with each other on scribe errors only 59% of the time with an AC1 of 0.24, then *every* published ambient-scribe accuracy metric, vendor-reported or not, is calibrated against a reference standard that barely exists. Authors explicitly disclaim generalization, “a within-pool triangulation and not a population error rate”, and concede that the latent-class conditional-independence assumption “is imperfect given shared model lineage.”

## 5. Safety, Bias, and Equity

### Stigmatizing language across 107 models

**Posted:** 14 August 2026 · medRxiv Preprint · Medium confidence

Yang, Gu, Hathaway, Bates, Lin, Zhou, and Yang, lead affiliation Division of Pharmacoepidemiology and Pharmacoeconomics, Brigham and Women's/Harvard, with Qingyu Chen (Yale), David W. Bates, and Leo Anthony Celi, audited 107 LLMs across 35 real-world clinical tasks (3,745 model-task pairs).

- Stigma rates ranged 0–33.33%; **84.06% of pairs contained stigma terms**.
- **19.76%** of pairs *amplified* stigma already present in the input note.
- Reasoning-optimized \> non-reasoning: 2.35% vs 1.70% (p\<0.0001).
- Open-source \> proprietary: 1.97% vs 1.60% (p\<0.01).
- Medical vs general models: no difference (2.00% vs 1.80%, p=0.26).

**Downgraded to medium for interpretive reasons, not sourcing:** (a) preprint; (b) “stigma” here is keyword/NLP detection of stigma terms via a psychiatrist-validated system, term presence is not demonstrated harm or biased recommendation; (c) the reasoning and open-source effects are **0.65 and 0.37 percentage points in absolute terms**, statistically robust across a large grid but of unproven clinical magnitude; (d) measurement is on *reasoning traces*, not deployed documentation, so “documentation bias” overstates it, say “model-generated text.”

**One claim was refuted and should not be repeated.** The abstract's assertion that destigmatizing prompt engineering cut stigma by up to 91.91% without performance loss was **refuted 0–3** in adversarial verification. If you cite this paper, either omit that number or flag it explicitly as an unverified author claim.

## 8. What This Means for a Hospitalist

**On ambient scribes, which is where this touches your day directly.** Two independent lines of evidence this week converge on the same uncomfortable place: we cannot currently measure whether these notes are good. The Heidi study shows the automated error-detection layer is no better than clinician-clinician agreement, which is itself poor (AC1 0.24). The CHA study shows that the field's favorite operational metric, effort reduction, is not a quality metric at all. The practical consequence for anyone evaluating a scribe deployment: *ask what the reference standard is*. If the answer is another model, or a single unadjudicated reviewer, the reported accuracy number is close to uninterpretable.

**On the language finding specifically.** Even accepting all the caveats, if your service cares for a substantial non-English-speaking population, the possibility that ambient documentation performs systematically worse for those encounters deserves local measurement rather than assumption. The equity failure mode here is quiet: nobody sees a bad note for a Haitian Creole–speaking patient and attributes it to the tool. Worth noting the null between interpreter-mediated and bilingual-provider encounters, the gap appears to be about the language itself, not about the interpreter workflow, which points at the ASR layer more than at the clinical-encounter structure. That is a testable hypothesis and nobody has tested it.

**On board-style benchmarks.** The NOTA result should permanently change how you read any “model scores X% on medical licensing exam” claim, including ones you may be asked to referee. GPT-5 dropped 14 points when the correct answer stopped being in the option list. Inpatient reasoning is disproportionately about recognizing that the available options are all wrong. This is the single most transferable finding of the week.

**On the FDA docket.** There is a real, time-limited opportunity here for someone with your background, closing 19 October. The competency-assessment analogy is being proposed by regulators to an audience that mostly has not been through competency assessment. Physicians who have, and who also understand model evaluation, are an unusually small population, and the docket is open.

**On evidence expectations generally.** The Nature Medicine systematic review below is the frame: 4,609 papers, 19 prospective randomized trials. This week produced six preprints and one Perspective, which is not an anomaly. It is the base rate.

## 11. Open Questions

1.  **Does FDA's competency-assessment concept displace locked-algorithm performance testing, or sit alongside it under existing 510(k)/De Novo authorities?** FDA explicitly declines to say whether the approaches fall within its current legal authorities, the load-bearing unanswered question for anyone modeling this pathway. (Note: the interpretive gloss that competency assessment *replaces* traditional testing failed verification; FDA never says this.)
2.  **Is there any independent, non-vendor evaluation of Ambience Healthcare's inpatient/chart-aware products anywhere in the literature**, not merely absent this week, but absent generally? This week's negative is week-scoped. The systematic question is more interesting and remains unanswered.
3.  **Does the NOTA robustness gap replicate outside single-specialty Japanese board items?** Specifically on English-language US internal medicine content, on open-ended rather than multiple-choice formats, and with multiple sampling runs. The 14–47 pp spread across generations also raises whether newer models are genuinely more robust or merely better at recognizing NOTA as a distractor pattern, a distinction with real clinical consequences.
4.  **Does the language-based ambient-documentation gap persist with current-generation models?** The CHA data end in August 2025. And nobody has yet decomposed the gap into ASR performance vs note-generation quality vs clinician editing behavior.
5.  **Has the Heidi finding been replicated by a non-vendor group?** If automated documentation-error judges genuinely perform no better than clinician-clinician agreement, it undermines the measurement layer beneath essentially every ambient-scribe quality claim currently in circulation, including vendor-reported accuracy metrics used in procurement.

## 12. Methodological Notes on This Report

- **Evidence grade is the headline caveat.** Six of the eight in-window substantive items are non-peer-reviewed preprints. Exactly one peer-reviewed journal item falls in the window (the NEJM AI Perspective), and it is commentary, not data. Nothing this week is trial-grade.
- **Fetch limitations.** fda.gov, regulations.gov, and ai.nejm.org all return 403/404 to automated fetchers. The FDA docket and NEJM AI content were verified via Crossref, browser-user-agent retrieval, search-index primary text, and independent reprints rather than routine page loads.
- **Measurement validity.** Two of this week's ambient-AI findings rest on proxies that are not clinical quality: ≥80% Percent Effort Reduction, and stigma-keyword counts in reasoning traces. Neither measures note accuracy, patient harm, or biased recommendation.
- **Dropped sub-details.** Three attributions failed verification and are excluded: NEJM AI's R1–R3 distinction originating in “decidability theory”; a purported Tsinghua press release of 20 Aug 2026; and the gloss that FDA's competency assessment replaces traditional locked-algorithm testing.
- **Time-sensitivity.** FDA comments close 19 October 2026. All preprints may change materially or fail peer review, re-check versions before citing.
