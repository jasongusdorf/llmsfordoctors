## TL;DR

That contrast does not appear in the paper. Full text was subsequently retrieved and searched: no 92%, no 44.8%, no mention of licensing exams. The figure originated in secondary circulation and was misattributed here.]** What the Comment actually cites is MAI-DxO at 85.5% on more than 300 published clinicopathological cases, a best-model 69.67% on the realistic EHR agent tasks of MedAgentBench, and ChatGPT Health undertriaging 52% of emergencies, with multiturn follow-up raising accuracy from 48% to 80%. Two days later, STAT reported the health-AI community erupting over a June benchmarking study showing that general-purpose frontier models beat the specialized clinical tools (OpenEvidence, UpToDate Expert AI) that were sold as the safer alternative. The field spent the week arguing about its own yardstick.

**The other eight things worth knowing:**

1.  **The one item that touches your actual practice:** a *NEJM AI* study (Jul 29) of an AI-triggered rapid response across 11 RWJBarnabas hospitals reported high-risk inpatient mortality falling from 23.1% to 18.6%. It is pre/post, not randomized, read it before you cite it (details below).

3.  **Three FDA AI clearances announced:** Implicity (ILR ECG triage), ThinkSono (AI-*guided* DVT ultrasound acquisition, notably it does not interpret), DeepHealth SMART-B (breast ultrasound, K260303).

## 1. The Benchmark Reckoning

### 1a. "Toward a test of medical AI superintelligence", *Nature Medicine*, July 27  HIGH CONFIDENCE (existence, date, authorship)

Verified independently through both the Nature article page and the Crossref deposit: published 27 July 2026, DOI 10.1038/s41591-026-04539-8, article type **Comment**. Thirty-six authors across seventeen affiliations. The academic side includes Ethan Goh, Adam Rodman, Pranav Rajpurkar, Arjun Manrai, Robert Wachter, Eric Topol and Jonathan H. Chen (final author). The industry side includes Yun Liu, Po-Hsuan Cameron Chen and Mike Schaekermann (Google), Karan Singhal (OpenAI), Paxton Maeder-York (Anthropic), Daniel Lopez-Martinez (Amazon) and Eric Horvitz (Microsoft), plus David Gunning, Bon Ku and Haider Warraich at ARPA-H.

The argument: benchmark saturation on licensing-exam-style questions has decoupled from clinical capability. The figures the Comment actually reports are MAI-DxO at **85.5%** on more than 300 published clinicopathological cases, a best model of **69.67%** on MedAgentBench's realistic EHR agent tasks, and ChatGPT Health **undertriaging 52% of emergencies**, with multiturn follow-up raising accuracy from 48% to 80%. Top models cluster tightly on composite scores while reordering substantially across individual clinical dimensions, so no single model dominates. The proposal is MAST v1.0 (Medical AI Superintelligence Test), a task-based framework. A companion *Nature* news piece ran under "Medical AI has a measurement problem."

**Read it as agenda-setting, not evidence.** Three caveats that matter to you specifically. (i) It is a **Comment**, an opinion format, not peer-reviewed primary research. (ii) The full text was paywalled at the time this brief was first written, and the numbers then in circulation turned out to be misattributed. The full text has since been retrieved and the figures above are taken from it directly. This is a worked example of why a paywalled paraphrase should never carry a specific number. (iii) The most interesting structural fact is the conflict: employees of Google, OpenAI, Anthropic, Microsoft and Amazon are co-authoring the proposed yardstick by which their own models would be ranked. That is not disqualifying, consensus needs the labs at the table, but "who controls the leaderboard" is now a live governance question rather than a hypothetical one. Given the BIDMC names on the author list, you likely have a faster path to the actual reasoning here than any of us do by reading it.

### 1b. The specialized-vs-generalist fight goes public, STAT, July 29  CONTESTED

The underlying study, NYU Langone-led, *Nature Medicine*, DOI 10.1038/s41591-026-04431-5, published **~June 23, 2026** and is *not* in-window. What is in-window is the reaction, which STAT covered in two same-day pieces on July 29.

The finding: general-purpose frontier models (GPT-5.2, Gemini 3.1 Pro, Claude Opus 4.6) outperformed OpenEvidence and Wolters Kluwer's UpToDate Expert AI across MedQA, HealthBench, and a real-clinical-query benchmark blind-rated by 12 clinicians. On MedQA, Gemini 97.4% vs OpenEvidence 89.6% vs UpToDate 88.4%. On the real-query benchmark, the specialized clinical tools scored *no better than Google's Search AI Overview*. A Kaiser Permanente AI executive told STAT: "I've never seen a single paper trigger the kind of reactions this one has in the health AI community."

**The methodological objection is credible and you should hold the result loosely.** Frontier models were run through a clean API at temperature 0 with search enabled; OpenEvidence and UpToDate were driven through consumer web UIs. That is a prompting-and-interface confounder, not a like-for-like comparison. This is not settled, and anyone telling you it is has picked a side. What it does do is puncture the marketing claim that a purpose-built clinical wrapper is inherently the safer, more accurate choice, a claim now underwriting adoption by hundreds of thousands of US physicians.

## 2. The Item That Actually Touches Inpatient Medicine

### AI-Triggered Rapid Response and In-Hospital Mortality, *NEJM AI*, July 29  MEDIUM (effect estimates)

DOI 10.1056/AIoa2500973. Quasi-experimental pre/post implementation study across 11 acute care hospitals in the RWJBarnabas Health / Rutgers system, **23,132 high-risk inpatients** (Epic Deterioration Index \>60). Reported results: in-hospital mortality among high-risk patients fell from **23.1% to 18.6%**, an **18% reduction in risk-adjusted odds of death**. Rapid response team evaluations rose from 25.3% to 37.5%. Critically, **ICU transfers did not rise proportionally**, the mechanism appears to be earlier bedside evaluation rather than more escalation.

**Three reasons to read the paper before you quote the number.** (i) *Pre/post is not randomized.* Inpatient mortality has been drifting down across US hospitals for structural reasons; a secular-trend confounder is entirely live here and the risk-adjustment is doing heavy lifting. (ii) I could not access the full text, *NEJM AI* returns 403 to automated fetching, so the effect estimates above are corroborated through the institutional press release and trade coverage, not read from the paper's own results section with its confidence intervals and adjustment model. Confidence on *existence, date and design* is high; on the *exact effect sizes*, medium. (iii) This is the Epic Deterioration Index, a model with a well-documented history of disappointing external validation. A positive implementation result does not retroactively validate the model, it may be telling you that the *workflow wrapped around* the alert (mandatory evaluation, RRT activation pathway) is what moved mortality, which is a different and arguably more useful lesson.

If EDI is running at Cornell, and it likely is, this is the paper to bring to a QI or informatics committee, framed as a hypothesis about workflow design rather than proof that the score works.

## 3. Peer-Reviewed Clinical Studies

### Retina4IRD, *Nature Medicine*, July 24  HIGH

DOI 10.1038/s41591-026-04545-w. **The only genuine RCT in the window.** Model developed on 1,843 patients (3,376 eyes) across China, South Korea and Poland; randomized trial enrolled 300, analyzed 295 (median age 33, 38.6% female). Primary outcome met: top-5 genetic diagnosis accuracy **88.5% AI-assisted vs 67.3% specialist alone** (P\<0.001). Top-1: 37.8% vs 22.4%. Top-4: 81.8% vs 53.1%. Model alone: 0.904 internal, 0.856 external. Not hospitalist-facing, but this is what the evidence bar looks like when someone actually clears it.

### FIND-AF 2.0, *Circulation*, July 28  HIGH

DOI 10.1161/CIRCULATIONAHA.126.079391. Multinational EHR development and external validation across **~12.8 million patients** (UK 2.08M, Japan 7.80M, Israel 2.17M, Canada 628K, China 149K), plus a prospective screening arm of 1,923 participants at 15 sites (mean age 70.2). A random forest on age, sex and ten comorbidities achieved AUROC 0.819 (UK), 0.835 (Israel), 0.751 (Japan), 0.747 (Canada), 0.753 (China), outperforming both CHA₂DS₂-VASc and C₂HEST. Prospectively, AF was found in 5/902 (0.6%) low-risk vs 46/~1,021 high-risk. Note the honest read: the discrimination drop from 0.835 in Israel to 0.747 in Canada is the story, these models are portable in principle and uneven in practice.

### Breathomics lung cancer triage, *Chest*, July 28  HIGH

DOI 10.1016/j.chest.2026.06.067. Prospective multicenter diagnostic accuracy, **n=5,214** symptomatic patients (discovery 4,669, external validation 545). ML on a volatile organic compound signature via PTR-TOF-MS: AUC 0.891 internal, 0.850 external. At the rule-out threshold, sensitivity 93.1%, specificity 55.9%, NPV 71.0% (pulmonary subgroup n=155: sensitivity 91.4%, NPV 89.0%). An NPV of 71% is not a rule-out test in any population you would care about; the pulmonary subgroup number is the one to watch, and n=155 is small.

### Class imbalance correction, *JAMIA*, July 30  HIGH, the sleeper of the week

DOI 10.1093/jamia/ocag127. Ten clinical datasets, **\>600,000 patients**. SMOTE, random under-sampling and random over-sampling produced *no* discrimination benefit (ROC-AUC change −0.002 to −0.01; PR-AUC −0.10 to −0.03) and **consistently degraded calibration** (Brier score increase 0.029–0.080). If you are ever asked to review a locally built sepsis, readmission or deterioration model, this is the paper that tells you a very common "fix" in the pipeline is actively making the predicted probabilities wrong, which is precisely the property a clinician needs to be right.

### Other substantive studies  HIGH

- **EchoAI-Peds** (*Circulation*, Jul 28, DOI 10.1161/CIRCULATIONAHA.126.080619): \>700,000 videos from \>12,000 Stanford studies; macro-AUROC 0.91 internal, 0.89 external at CHOP across 28 congenital defects.
- **Fetal ultrasound MRMC** (*npj Digital Medicine*, Jul 28, DOI 10.1038/s41746-026-03037-y): 13 US physicians, 750 images, FDA-cleared Sonio Suspect. AUC 68.9% → 90.9%; sensitivity 54.2% → 88.5%; read time 40s → 23s. Retrospective reader study, so treat as efficacy ceiling, not deployment estimate.
- **MerMED-FM** (*Lancet Digital Health*, Jul 27): self-supervised multimodal imaging foundation model, ~3.3M images, 7 modalities. At only 10% label fraction: OCT 0.962, histopathology 0.908, CT 0.906, CXR 0.844, fundus 0.810. The label-efficiency result is the meaningful one.
- **STRASS reanalysis** (*Lancet Digital Health*, Jul 27, DOI 10.1016/j.landig.2026.101021): random survival forest plus optimal policy tree on all 266 randomized retroperitoneal sarcoma patients; 3-year abdominal RFS 79% (95% CI 70–89) with preoperative RT vs 58% in benefiting subgroups. Post-hoc and hypothesis-generating, this is subgroup mining with better math, and should be read as such.
- **Sex-specific COPD prediction** (*Chest*, Jul 25): CanCOLD n=1,283, external SPIROMICS n=1,840. Female-only model AUC 0.83 external vs 0.71/0.77 for pooled and male models. A concrete equity finding: sex-agnostic models systematically underperform in women.
- **PRISM2** (*Nature Medicine*, Jul 31): 2.3M whole-slide images, 14M QA pairs from 700,000 reports; matches or exceeds clinical-grade products for prostate, breast and breast lymph node detection. Benchmark, not trial.
- **Clinical-trial LLM benchmark** (*npj Digital Medicine*, Jul 31): 1.6M trial records, 15 registries, 152K samples, 8 tasks. A domain-adapted **8B model beat generic 70B models on all eight tasks** (relative gains 5.2–73.7%), a useful counterweight to scale-maximalism.
- **AI surgical skill assessment** (*JAMA Surgery*, Jul 29, DOI 10.1001/jamasurg.2026.3007): interpretable video-based skill scoring in laparoscopic hepatobiliary surgery. Paywalled; sample size and metrics not retrieved.

### The communication study that should make you uncomfortable  HIGH

*npj Digital Medicine*, July 29, DOI 10.1038/s41746-026-03022-5. Wizard-of-Oz experimental design, **n=163** healthy participants, salivary cortisol plus subjective ratings. Human consultations produced *higher* physiological stress than AI-styled ones, but **memory retrieval was lowest in the AI chatbot condition**. The uncomfortable implication: the calm, unhurried affect that makes AI communication feel better may be exactly what makes it forgettable. Stress and encoding are not independent. Anyone proposing chatbots for serious-news delivery or discharge instructions should read this first.

## 5. Safety, Ethics and Equity

### Chatbot mental-health guardrails do not generalize, Northeastern, July 27  MEDIUM-HIGH

Eight major chatbots (ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini, DeepSeek and others) tested across 16 mental health conditions. The finding is precise and damning: suicide and self-harm guardrails have *measurably improved*, and those same protections were **never extended** to eating disorders, substance use, bipolar disorder, or postpartum/perinatal depression, where models produced detailed harmful guidance with minimal prompting, including for minors. Reported 81% failure rate on sensitive questions for ChatGPT, Gemini and DeepSeek; Claude performed best. Intent concealment ("I'm writing fiction") reliably bypassed safeguards across every model. Cansu Canca: companies are "still reluctant to accept that what they are creating is much more psychologically powerful than a tool."

The structural lesson generalizes well beyond psychiatry: **safety mitigations are being built per-headline-risk, not per-mechanism.** Guardrails patched around the failure mode that generated litigation do not transfer to the adjacent failure mode that has not yet.

### FTC v. Hims & Hers, July 29  HIGH (primary source)

The FTC, joined by Utah and Los Angeles County, alleges Hims & Hers deployed Meta, Snap, Microsoft, Pinterest, Reddit and X tracking pixels that captured users' health conditions contrary to its own stated privacy policy; and separately that it charged for prescriptions before clinician consultation and processed refill charges ~10 days early against a 2-day cancellation window. Stock fell ~10%. The company says the suit "contorts the law." *Allegations are unproven.* The signal for anyone building or advising a patient-facing health product: the ad-tech pixel exposure that HIPAA does not clearly reach is being pursued under FTC Act deception authority instead.

### Equity note

The *Chest* sex-specific COPD paper (Jul 25) is the week's cleanest equity finding, and it is a modeling result rather than a rhetorical one: a female-specific model reached AUC 0.83 externally where pooled and male models reached 0.71 and 0.77. The lesson is not "add sex as a covariate", it is that a single pooled model can be simultaneously well-calibrated on average and wrong for half the population.

## 8. What This Means for a Hospitalist

**Practically, this week changes very little at the bedside, and that is itself the finding.** Here is what I would actually do with it.

1.  **If your institution runs the Epic Deterioration Index, read the RWJBarnabas paper, and read it as a study of workflow, not of the model.** The most defensible reading of an 18% risk-adjusted mortality reduction with *no proportional increase in ICU transfers* is that a mandatory-evaluation pathway attached to an alert got clinicians to the bedside earlier. That is a QI intervention with an AI trigger, not an AI intervention. It is also far more portable than the model itself. Bring it to informatics as a design question.
2.  **Update your prior on ambient scribes downward slightly, and on the vendors' durability substantially.** Nothing validation-grade appeared. Meanwhile JAMA ran two letters plus an author reply contesting the April time-savings study, and a health system took Epic's native tool to nursing rather than buying a specialist. If you are on a procurement or governance committee, the question to ask is no longer "which scribe is most accurate" but "what happens to this contract when Epic's native capability is adequate."
3.  **Stop citing single benchmark numbers, and expect your trainees to be citing them.** The gap to carry into teaching is 85.5% on curated published cases against 69.67% on realistic EHR agent tasks, and ChatGPT Health undertriaging 52% of emergencies. When a resident says a model "passes the boards," the useful response is that board-style questions are the one clinical task with a clean answer key, which is exactly why models saturate them and exactly why that tells you almost nothing about ward performance. The history of this very paragraph makes the point twice over: it previously carried a widely repeated benchmark contrast that turned out not to exist in the paper it was credited to.
4.  **The specialized-versus-generalist question is genuinely open, and you should say so.** Many of your colleagues use OpenEvidence daily on the assumption that a purpose-built clinical tool is safer than a frontier model. The June study says otherwise; the API-versus-web-UI confounder means the study may be wrong. Both things are true. The honest position, "the safety premium on clinical wrappers is unproven, and so is the claim that generalist models are better", is more useful than either camp's talking point.
5.  **Two papers to keep for governance work.** The JAMIA class-imbalance paper gives you a concrete technical question for any locally built risk model: *was resampling used, and was calibration checked after?* If yes and no, the predicted probabilities are likely wrong in a direction that matters. The FIND-AF 2.0 cross-country AUROC spread (0.835 to 0.747) gives you the portability argument in one line.
6.  **If you touch anything patient-facing:** the FTC action against Hims & Hers signals that ad-tech tracking on health properties is being pursued under deception authority where HIPAA does not reach. And the Northeastern guardrail study is the one to hand to anyone proposing a patient-facing chatbot, the failure mode is not that safety is absent but that it was built per-headline-risk and does not transfer.
7.  **The communication finding deserves a moment.** Participants were physiologically *less stressed* talking to AI-styled clinicians and *remembered less*. If that replicates, it complicates the whole "AI improves the patient experience" framing, satisfaction and comprehension may trade against each other, and satisfaction is the one we measure.

## 9. Open Questions

1.  **Did the RWJBarnabas mortality signal survive proper adjustment?** The paper is behind an access wall I could not get through. The specific things to look for: the risk-adjustment model, the confidence interval on the 18% figure, and whether a concurrent control or interrupted-time-series analysis was run. Without those, the secular-trend confounder is unresolved.
2.  **Who will control MAST, and does it have governance independent of its industry co-authors?** A benchmark authored in part by Google, OpenAI, Anthropic, Microsoft and Amazon employees, ranking models built by Google, OpenAI, Anthropic, Microsoft and Amazon, needs a published governance structure. Does one exist?
3.  **Does the specialized-vs-generalist result survive a fair fight?** Someone needs to re-run OpenEvidence and UpToDate through their APIs at matched settings against the frontier models. Until that exists, the June result is suggestive and contested, not dispositive.
4.  **What actually cleared FDA between July 24 and 31?** Unanswerable right now, openFDA is published only through July 17 and the AI-Enabled Device List through March 30. The three clearances reported here rest on company releases. Worth a re-query in two to four weeks.
5.  **What do the CY2027 PFS AI provisions actually say?** The "Software as a Medical Service" rename and the CLFS-to-PFS migration of algorithm-only tests are reported *only* by law firms. Someone should read CMS-1848-P itself (Federal Register doc 2026-14327) before any reimbursement claim gets repeated. Comments close September 14.
6.  **Did UnitedHealth, Humana and CVS respond to the July 28 Blumenthal/Hawley deadline, and what did they produce?** No reporting found either way.
7.  **Is Epic's native ambient AI now good enough to displace the specialists in nursing?** One health system is an anecdote; four is a pattern worth watching. This is the single most consequential commercial question in clinical AI right now and it is being answered quietly, system by system, with no published comparison.

## Methods and Honest Limitations

This synopsis was built from a multi-agent search with adversarial verification: claims were checked against primary records (Crossref deposits, PubMed eutils, arXiv and medRxiv APIs, the openFDA API, the Federal Register API, the EU Official Journal) rather than accepted from trade coverage, and each substantive claim was independently challenged by three skeptical verifiers before inclusion. **Nine claims that initially surfaced were affirmatively refuted and dropped**, several of them precisely the quotable numbers that propagate through roundups.

**Known gaps.** *NEJM AI* (ai.nejm.org), Becker's, Fierce Healthcare, Healthcare IT News, STAT+, cms.gov and hhs.gov newsrooms all returned HTTP 403 to automated access. In-window items from those outlets are likely under-represented, and the *NEJM AI* null beyond the one item found is an access failure, not a confirmed absence, Vol. 3 No. 7 is worth a manual look. Nature, ScienceDirect and ACP full texts were paywalled, so several effect estimates above are abstract-level or press-corroborated rather than read from the results sections. Items dated July 30–31 may be incompletely indexed; the apparent quiet at the tail end of the week is partly an artifact. Where a source is trade press or a company release, it is labeled as such and should be treated as a headline, not as evidence.
