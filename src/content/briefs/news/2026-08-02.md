## International

### Trump cancels Iran strikes, claims outlines of a deal

Late Saturday, Trump posted that he had canceled planned strikes on Iran after Iran and other regional states asked him to hold off, and that the “perimeters of a deal” had been agreed, covering the “Immediate, Complete, and Total OPENING OF THE HORMUZ STRAIT” and an end to Iran’s nuclear threat. He said Israel was party to the commitment and that US forces remain “locked and loaded.” This is the second pullback from the brink in two weeks. The war began in late February; more than five months on, neither strikes nor negotiation has curbed Iran’s nuclear program or reopened the strait, which Iran has throttled as its central bargaining chip. A late-June ceasefire collapsed within weeks. The most concrete corroboration is Saudi: SPA reported Sunday that Mohammed bin Salman urged Trump toward “dialogue to de-escalate.” The NYT also reports that few in Trump’s own circle favored escalation, partly because interceptor stocks are depleting.

**Flag, developing and contested.** Iran has given no indication an agreement is near. Mehr, citing unnamed military officials, called Trump’s account “simply a new lie”; Fars called it a “mixture of truth and falsehood,” said Hormuz reopens only when the US lifts its naval blockade, and denied any Oman transit deal exists. No Western outlet has seen a text. An AP poll this week found nearly two-thirds of Americans say the war isn’t worth fighting.

### Ceuta: 67 dead, and an EU rupture

Between roughly 50,000 and 60,000 people crossed from Morocco into the Spanish enclave of Ceuta on Thursday and Friday, most swimming around the breakwater. Spain put the toll at 67 Saturday, drownings and a stampede crush. Madrid installed a 500-meter floating barrier and deployed troops, and says most who entered have since returned to Morocco. The second-order story is the European one: Italy imposed temporary border controls on travel from Spain and France ordered extra checks, prompting Pedro Sánchez to condemn a “selfish, polarising and unlawful reaction” from partners. Leaders of 22 member states have called for an emergency meeting. The FT frames far-right parties as having set the terms of the response continent-wide.

### The Danube drought is now an energy event

Hungary powered down the four-reactor Paks plant Sunday, the first full shutdown in its 44-year history, because record-low Danube levels have starved its cooling intakes. Paks supplies close to half of Hungary’s electricity; output had fallen to 240 MW overnight against a normal 2,000 MW. The river is projected to hit −144cm at Paks, far past the −98cm record set in 2018, with no meaningful rain forecast for weeks. Romania shut both its reactors on July 29 for the same reason. Officials stress there is no safety threat, but Budapest is drafting plans to curtail large industrial and public-sector consumers. Pair this with the NYT’s reporting on how extreme heat has changed what summer means across European generations.

Bloomberg, AP, Euronews · Jul 30–Aug 2 · [bloomberg.com](https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-08-01/hungary-to-shut-its-only-nuclear-plant-for-first-time-on-drought)

### DRC Ebola outbreak is now the largest in the country’s history

The Bundibugyo virus outbreak confirmed in DRC and Uganda in May has reached 3,605 confirmed cases and 1,587 deaths as of July 30, a crude CFR of 44%, and the largest Ebola outbreak ever recorded in the DRC. It has spread from a single Ituri health zone to five provinces and 49 health zones. There is no licensed vaccine or specific therapeutic for the Bundibugyo species; candidate trials are ongoing. Virunga National Park has stood up six health checkpoints across a 300km forest corridor with 40 paramedics and 80 rangers, framing the park as both a transmission barrier and a shield for great ape populations. This has been badly undercovered relative to its scale.

Trump said Saturday he will keep Todd Blanche as acting attorney general and push ahead with the “Anti-Weaponization Fund” if Sens. Cornyn and Tillis refuse to back Blanche’s confirmation, throwing next week’s planned vote into doubt. ## Israel

### A disarmament deal announced, and a sequencing impasse underneath it

Trump announced Friday that his “Board of Peace” had reached an accord for the “complete disarmament of Hamas and all other armed groups in Gaza,” with a road map due within 14 days. Under the framework, Hamas would gradually transfer weapons to a technocratic National Committee for the Administration of Gaza, after which Israeli forces withdraw and an International Stabilization Force works alongside a new Palestinian police force. Islamic Jihad reportedly signed on as of Sunday. The framework is confirmed across AP, NPR, CNN, Reuters, ToI and Al Jazeera, but the order of operations is not agreed by anyone. An Israeli official told Reuters there will be no withdrawal absent “genuine disarmament”; Hamas says disarmament follows full withdrawal and the start of reconstruction, and ties heavy weapons to eventual statehood, which this Israeli government rejects. Israel has issued no formal acceptance and Netanyahu has stayed publicly silent.

### The Treasury intervenes in the yen, first time since 1998

The most underappreciated story of the week. The New York Fed sold euros to buy yen on the Treasury’s behalf Friday, through Goldman Sachs and Morgan Stanley, the first time Washington and Tokyo have jointly supported the yen via outright purchases in nearly 30 years. The Treasury had pre-briefed several Wall Street banks and was in contact with the ECB. Context: the yen hit its weakest level against the dollar since 1986 on July 23. The dollar fell about 4% Thursday on speculation Japan had intervened, analysts estimate roughly ¥8.45tn (\$52.8bn), and shed a further 1.9% Friday to ¥157.57. The BoJ held at 1% Friday, with Ueda saying he would not let the bank “fall behind the curve” and could accelerate hikes. Bessent posted warmly about Ueda ahead of the G20 finance meeting in North Carolina this month. The last US intervention to *strengthen* the yen was 1998; the 2011 action went the other way, after Tohoku.

### A hawkish Fed, and a bond market that doesn’t believe the hold

The FOMC held at 3.50–3.75% on Wednesday, 9–3, with Hammack, Kashkari and Logan all dissenting in favor of a *hike*, the first triple same-direction dissent since roughly 2016. Chair Kevin Warsh was pointed: “There is no soft inflation target. There’s only a target, and it’s 2%,” and, on the dissents, “I asked for a good family fight, and I got one.” Markets read the hold as deferral, not dovishness: the 30-year jumped from ~5.1% to 5.21% during the presser, its highest since 2007, the dollar slid, and the Dow fell 840 points. CME FedWatch put September hike odds near 56% post-decision. Note the shape: the curve bear-steepened, with the 2-year actually falling on the week while the long end sold off, a term-premium and inflation-credibility signal rather than a policy-rate one. Breakevens rose while stocks and the dollar fell together, the classic credibility-repricing signature.

## Also notable

### ZEUS: ziltivekimab lowers inflammation and changes nothing

The most consequential item here for your practice. Novo Nordisk’s phase 3 ZEUS trial, in more than 6,300 patients with ASCVD, CKD and residual inflammation, returned a hazard ratio of 0.99 (95% CI 0.88–1.11) for CV death, non-fatal MI or stroke. Target engagement was clean, free IL-6 and hsCRP fell as designed, so this is a genuine dissociation between biomarker and outcome rather than an exposure failure. Serious infections were more common on drug. It is the sharpest negative result for the residual-inflammatory-risk hypothesis since CANTOS came in positive, and it should cool enthusiasm for hsCRP-guided anti-inflammatory strategies. HERMES (HFpEF) and ARTEMIS (post-MI) are the field’s remaining tests, reading out in H1 2027.

### GLP-1 agonists and hair loss, now with a real design

A Penn Medicine EHR cohort of 12,004 GLP-1 RA users, published in *The BMJ*, found a 1.37x higher risk of non-scarring alopecia versus SGLT2 inhibitors (1.08–1.73) and 1.68x versus DPP-4 inhibitors (1.28–2.20), using active comparators and stable across subgroups. The pattern fits telogen effluvium from rapid weight loss and nutritional shifts rather than a direct follicular effect. It converts a heavily anecdotal, TikTok-driven signal into systematic evidence. Practical value is in pre-treatment counseling: hair loss is a common real-world reason patients quit, and framing it as transient and weight-mediated may preserve adherence.

## Worth reading

### The Myth of the Food Desert, Kelefa Sanneh, The New Yorker

Start here. Sanneh takes apart one of the most durable ideas in American public health: that poor diets in low-income neighborhoods are primarily a problem of retail geography. He marshals evidence that building supermarkets into “food deserts” mostly siphons business from existing stores without measurably changing what people buy, and that the causal arrow may run backward, stores following preferences rather than shaping them. The field reporting is uncomfortable: when Kenneth Kolb tried explaining the food-desert framework to residents, what came back was a much broader set of grievances about disinvestment and disrespectful local retailers, mostly unrelated to produce. Hanna Garth’s Los Angeles fieldwork is sharper, quoting activists who dismiss the term as something “some white liberal came up with” and documenting how food-justice programs can function as white racial projects, evangelizing kale to people who never asked. Sanneh’s own move is to take taste and dignity seriously as variables rather than obstacles. His conclusion, distance doesn’t matter much, because people will go to great lengths to eat what they want, is a direct challenge to a whole class of interventions clinicians refer patients into without much thought.

Aug 3, 2026 issue · [newyorker.com](https://www.newyorker.com/magazine/2026/08/03/food-justice-undone-hanna-garth-book-review)

### Johnny Knoxville, America’s God of Mischief, Molly Young, The New Yorker

Young takes seriously a question usually treated as a punchline: why would a rich, famous, 55-year-old father keep letting bulls hit him? Her answer runs closer to neurology than showbiz. She traces the compulsion to a Tennessee boyhood of asthma attacks treated with adrenaline injections, a repeated near-death-then-euphoria loop, and a father who ran elaborate pranks out of his tire company. The middle of the piece turns genuinely clinical: double-digit fractures, a urethral tear, an orbital fracture that luxated his eye, sixteen concussions, and the 2022 bull incident that caused a brain hemorrhage and ended his stunt career. The best line belongs to his own physician, who says the injuries aren’t a consequence of the work but in some sense its purpose. Young is careful about the aftermath, depression, antidepressants, a CTE question nobody can answer while he’s alive. Read as a case study in a patient who is fully informed and entirely unable to stop.
