## International

### Russia strikes Kyiv again; at least nine killed

Russian ballistic missiles and drones hit Kyiv before dawn Saturday, killing at least nine and wounding 28, four of them children, according to Mayor Vitali Klitschko. Witnesses described more than a dozen explosions; the first two floors of a residential building in Shevchenkivskyi district collapsed, trapping residents. Damage was reported across five districts to 18 apartment buildings and homes, two schools, a clinic and a metro station. Zelensky said the attack used North Korean KN-23 missiles. It was the second major strike on the capital in 48 hours, and it followed Trump backing away from a pledge on air defenses, Ukraine is short of Patriot interceptors and wants a license to build them, which Trump said Friday has not been agreed on technology-transfer grounds.

Developing: early wires said three dead; the toll climbed to nine through the morning and may move again.

Al Jazeera, AP via Washington Post, Reuters · overnight into Sat Aug 1

### The Iran war spills further into the Gulf

Iran's Revolutionary Guard said it hit US military assets at Kuwait's Ahmad Al-Jaber airbase with drones Friday, hangars, satellite communications, equipment warehouses, framing it as retaliation for US strikes on Qeshm island. Kuwait's armed forces separately said they intercepted hostile drones from dawn Saturday aimed at a government facility in the north and at vehicles on Bubiyan Island; shrapnel caused material damage, no injuries. CENTCOM has confirmed a heavy wave of strikes across Qeshm, Kish, and Fars, Bushehr, Khuzestan and Hormozgan provinces. The strategic fact underneath all of it: Strait of Hormuz traffic has fallen from roughly 130 vessels a day before the war to a handful. CNN reports 28% of Americans approve of Trump's handling of the conflict. The NYT's own analysis this morning is blunter still, arguing US allies now see a strategic defeat in the making, with none of Trump's stated aims accomplished and the strait closed.

Caveat: most specific strike claims come from IRGC and CENTCOM statements. Independent battle-damage confirmation is not available.

Al Jazeera, CNN, Gulf News, Middle East Eye, New York Times · Fri Jul 31 - Sat Aug 1

### Roughly 50,000-60,000 people breach the border at Ceuta

Beginning Thursday, tens of thousands crossed from Morocco into the Spanish enclave of Ceuta by land and sea, in what appears to be the largest such surge on record. Pedro Sanchez visited Friday after local officials asked Madrid to deploy the army, blaming trafficking networks that seized on what he called a self-serving reading of a Spanish Supreme Court ruling barring immediate deportation of sea arrivals, a rumor he said "spread like wildfire." Spain has installed a floating barrier. Ursula von der Leyen said the crossings "must stop immediately." By Friday, the NYT reports, most of those who crossed had left, hungry and disappointed.

Death tolls diverge badly: AP and the Washington Post reported at least 18, Al Jazeera as high as 57, many from drowning around the sea barrier. Treat the number as unsettled.

AP/Washington Post, Al Jazeera, New York Times · Thu Jul 30 - Fri Jul 31

### FIFA abandons the World Cup stake sale

FIFA said Friday it will not proceed with selling roughly 20% of a new unit housing its events, including the World Cup, a deal that would have raised up to \$4.2bn at a \$20bn valuation. The climbdown followed a unanimous Thursday vote by all 55 UEFA member associations to boycott FIFA tournaments until the plan was withdrawn, on the principle that the World Cup "cannot be treated as an investment product." CONCACAF and the Asian confederation joined. Infantino's senior adviser Carlos Cordeiro resigned. This is the most serious governance revolt of Infantino's tenure, and UEFA has said the withdrawal does not restore its confidence in him.

CNN, ESPN, Al Jazeera, The Athletic · Fri Jul 31

### Broad Peak avalanche

An avalanche swept a ten-member international expedition on Broad Peak, the 8,051m Karakoram peak, around midday Thursday. Pakistani rescuers using drones and helicopters have recovered four bodies; six climbers remain missing. The dead include Nadhira Al Harthy, the first Omani woman to summit Everest; Nepali climber Pur Bahadur Gurung, a ten-time Everest summiteer; and American Sarah Mallory Geis, on her first 8,000m peak. Among the missing is expedition leader Nirmal Purja, "Nims Dai," who holds the record for the fastest ascent of all fourteen 8,000m peaks. Weather is restricting helicopter flights.

AP, CNN, CBC, Al Jazeera · Thu Jul 30 - Sat Aug 1

### Sri Lanka's Easter bombing verdict

A three-judge High Court bench Friday sentenced former police chief Pujith Jayasundara and former defence secretary Hemasiri Fernando to death for criminal negligence, having convicted them of failing to act on advance intelligence, including a warning passed from India, before the April 21, 2019 Easter Sunday bombings that killed more than 260 at churches and hotels. In practice the sentence means life imprisonment: Sri Lanka has not executed anyone since 1976. Both can appeal. It is the most significant accountability ruling yet in a case that has also entangled former president Maithripala Sirisena.

AP via Washington Post, Al Jazeera · Fri Jul 31

## United States

### The deepest Colorado River cuts yet proposed

The Bureau of Reclamation released a proposal Friday requiring Arizona, California and Nevada to cut collective use by up to 3 million acre-feet a year in dry years through 2036, roughly equal to Arizona's and Nevada's entire combined allocations, and enough water for more than 25 million people. The four Upper Basin states face no mandatory cuts for now. This follows more than three years of failed negotiation among the seven basin states over guidelines expiring this year. Reaction split along the priority ladder: Arizona, holding the lowest-priority Lower Basin rights, called the proposal flawed; California called it "an important milestone but not the finish line"; Nevada's governor called the reductions unrealistic and devastating.

AP, NBC News, CBS News, The Hill · Fri Jul 31

### Water utilities hacked in seven states; the attribution fight goes public

The FBI said utilities in at least seven states have reported disruptions from malicious actors targeting drinking water systems since July 27, with more than 30 community systems affected in Minnesota alone. Most confirmed intrusions involved remote monitoring and control technology, including programmable logic controllers, and federal authorities reported loss of monitoring and control at critical infrastructure sites. US and state officials told the New York Times that Iranian hackers were likely responsible, citing tradecraft and the absence of any ransom demand, though no formal attribution has been made. At a Cabinet meeting at Camp David Friday, Trump said he does not believe Iran is responsible and instead blamed Minnesota, calling the state "grossly incompetent," without offering evidence; he and Gov. Tim Walz sparred publicly.

NBC News, CBS News, ABC News, New York Times · Fri Jul 31

## Israel

### A disarmament agreement Israel has not signed

Trump announced that his Board of Peace reached "a HISTORIC agreement for the COMPLETE DISARMAMENT of Hamas" and all other armed groups in Gaza, to be implemented in phases. The reported framework is a 15-point roadmap: a weapons inventory within 14 days of formal acceptance, a staged disarmament running roughly 200-300 days covering heavy weapons, production sites and tunnels, an early tranche for rockets and launchers, weapons held by a Palestinian National Committee rather than transferred to Israel, an International Stabilization Force of about 5,000 under an American general, and a new Palestinian police force alongside a technocratic administrative committee.

The problem is sequencing, and it is not a technicality. Hamas negotiator Ghazi Hamad said the group "will not implement any step" unless Israel first halts fire, ends targeted killings, withdraws to the Yellow Line and permits an aid surge. A senior Israeli official told CNN there will be "no IDF withdrawal from the current Yellow Line without the genuine disarmament of Hamas," and Netanyahu's office said the plan does not meet Israel's demand for full demilitarization. Each side has made the other's first move its own precondition.

Netanyahu had not commented personally as of Friday's liveblog, the "falls short" line came from his office, not from him. Israel has not publicly accepted the framework.

### 700 tons of explosives under the Beaufort Ridge

The IDF said it detonated roughly 700 tons of explosives around midnight Thursday to destroy key routes in a multi-level Hezbollah tunnel network beneath the Beaufort Ridge in southern Lebanon, built over nearly two decades and attributed to Hezbollah's Badr unit as infiltration infrastructure aimed at Galilee communities. Netanyahu and Defense Minister Israel Katz said jointly that Israel "will not accept any violation of the ceasefire agreement," citing a Hezbollah drone attack Wednesday on an unmanned Israeli bulldozer. ## Worth reading

### The Myth of the Food Desert, Kelefa Sanneh

Nominally a review of Hanna Garth's *Food Justice Undone*, actually a demolition of the two-decade bipartisan consensus that poor American diets are a problem of retail access. Sanneh traces "food desert" from a Scottish public-housing tenant in the early nineties through the 2008 farm bill and Michelle Obama's 23.5-million-Americans framing to Mamdani's city-owned groceries and RFK Jr.'s inverted food pyramid. The evidence, he shows, is thin: new supermarkets mostly siphon customers from existing ones and shorten commutes without changing carts, and the causality probably runs backward, stores reflect local tastes rather than create them. His sharpest move is reviving Orwell's *Road to Wigan Pier*: "the less money you have, the less inclined you feel to spend it on wholesome food... You want something a little bit 'tasty'", and noting Orwell blamed the class system, not the palate, while today's scholars evade preferences entirely. He needles the evasion from both sides: it is no less patronizing to say the poor are helpless before advertising than to say they like French fries. Both books suggest what residents actually want is not kale but newer, nicer stores, respect and investment. Directly relevant to how we talk about diet-related disease with patients. ### Forget Albums, Pop Stars Measure Their Careers in Eras Now, Mitch Therieau

This morning's Weekend Essay argues that pop careers are now measured in fan-coined "eras" rather than albums, reflecting two incompatible desires: we want stars to grow and take us somewhere, and we want them frozen at their peak. The Eras Tour was the hinge, deliberately nonlinear, which Swift described as feeling "like you're scrolling in an algorithm." Read generously it was a reclamation of her masters; read darkly it was "necro-pop," the exhumed past beating the present. Therieau finds the same morbidity in the Weeknd's post-apocalyptic staging and Grande's Eternal Sunshine tour, and grounds it in economics: top tour ticket prices rose about 50% between 2019 and 2024, so the only thing worth that money was a shared epic history. Too much history at once produces paralysis, even literal amnesia, fans reported blacking out at Eras. It ends on a Charli XCX loop that never resolves: "It's a slow apocalypse, but it's also a fashion show."
