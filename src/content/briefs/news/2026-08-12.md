## International

### Colombia: 7.4 quake kills 250+, and the toll is still moving

The quake struck early Monday at about 110 km depth, epicenter 5 km south of San José del Palmar in Chocó department, roughly 240 km west of Bogotá, Colombia's most destructive this century. Reported deaths climbed through Monday and Tuesday from 111 to 132 to 181 and past 250, with Pereira (101) and Cali (95) worst hit. Injury counts range wildly across outlets, from 570 to 2,595, and missing-persons figures from 195 to about 4,000. More than 100 aftershocks were logged by Tuesday; three airports closed and rescuers still could not reach the epicenter itself. President Abelardo De La Espriella, inaugurated days earlier, declared a state of national disaster and ordered government entities to stop releasing independent death tolls, which is itself much of why the numbers diverge so much. Reuters, Al Jazeera, NPR, Aug 10-12

**Developing.** Treat any single casualty figure as provisional; the spread across outlets is unusually wide and partly a function of the government's reporting order.

### DRC: Ebola passes 2,000 deaths, fastest-growing outbreak on record

Congolese officials confirmed 4,381 cases and 2,011 deaths across five provinces as of Tuesday, with roughly 1,000 of those deaths in just the past three weeks. The pathogen is the rare Bundibugyo strain, for which there is no approved vaccine or approved therapeutic, a critical distinction from the Zaire-strain outbreaks where Ervebo and monoclonals were available. The 2018-19 DRC outbreak took ten months to reach 2,000 cases; this one did so in about two. That makes it the second-largest Ebola epidemic ever behind West Africa 2014-16. Response is hobbled by strikes among unpaid health workers, misinformation, and burial practices; vaccine trials are underway with results months away. Israel is testing a returning traveler as a suspected case. Al Jazeera, Reuters, Aug 11

This is arguably the most underweighted story of the week relative to its scale, and the one most likely to reach your census eventually.

### Ukraine: proposals to Washington, drones on the ports

Zelenskyy said Tuesday that Kyiv has handed US negotiators written proposals for ending the war, without disclosing terms; secondary reporting describes a framework freezing fighting along current lines with demilitarized buffer zones and mutual pullbacks. Kyiv is simultaneously pressing for more pressure on Moscow and more air defense, and Zelenskyy warned of a possible renewed Russian mobilization wave. Overnight, a five-hour drone and naval-drone assault on Novorossiysk killed at least one person, reported as an eight-year-old, injured eight, and halted operations at NKHP, one of Russia's largest grain export terminals. Russian strikes killed two in Kherson the same night. Reuters, Al Jazeera, Moscow Times, Aug 11-12

**Sourcing note.** The specific peace terms are second-hand, Zelenskyy gave none. Drone-intercept and casualty counts from Russian-installed officials are single-source.

### Turkey: parliament pardons thousands of PKK fighters

A 468-88 vote Monday approved 12-article legislation creating a pardon-like mechanism for thousands of PKK members, the most concrete legislative step yet in the peace process. It suspends prison sentences and postpones investigations and trials for five or ten years depending on severity, and takes effect only after authorities verify full PKK disarmament. The PKK announced last year it was ending its insurgency and dissolving, following a call from the imprisoned Abdullah Öcalan. The conflict has killed tens of thousands since 1984. Watch the downstream effects in Iraq and in Damascus's effort to absorb SDF-held territory. AP, Aug 11

### Europe: fifth heatwave, and 71% of England in drought

The UK government declared Monday that 71.3% of England is formally in drought, the third such declaration in five years, after the driest July on record, England got 10% of its long-term average July rainfall and southern England saw its driest month in records back to 1836. Britain's fifth heatwave of 2026 opened over the weekend with mid-30s C forecast across Britain and France this week. Continent-wide, more than 25,000 heat deaths this year, wildfires in several countries, and reactors temporarily idled in Hungary and Romania because the Danube ran too low and too warm to cool them. Scientists describe a compounding spiral: June-July drought dried soils past the point where evaporative cooling functions, so each subsequent heatwave builds faster. Euronews, France 24, CNN, Aug 10

### Afghanistan: 2.4 million girls out of secondary school

UNESCO released a report Tuesday timed to five years since the Taliban's return, finding roughly 2.4 million girls excluded from secondary education and Afghanistan the only country on earth formally barring women from secondary and higher education. Near-zero female secondary enrollment in 2001 had grown to nearly a million girls by 2021. The Taliban has rejected appeals from the UN, rights groups, and several Muslim-majority states. UNESCO via AP, Aug 11

## United States

### Executive order shrinks the childhood vaccine schedule

Trump signed an order Monday afternoon directing federal agencies to cut routinely recommended childhood vaccines from 17 to 11 and to split the combined MMR into three separate injections at separate visits. Hepatitis A and B, COVID-19, and influenza come off the universal recommendation list. It builds on RFK Jr.'s restructuring of federal immunization policy and repeats the disputed vaccine-autism claim. Because states set school mandates, the order only advises states to align, but the practical bite runs through insurer coverage and VFC purchasing, which key off federal recommendations, and through state legislatures. The AAP responded that "there is no new evidence to justify significant changes to childhood immunization guidance." NPR, CNN, WaPo, CNBC, Aug 10

Outlets differ on the baseline count (17 vs 18) and implementation mechanics remain unspecified. For a hospitalist, the near-term signal to watch is whether ACIP's recommendation status changes trigger VFC and commercial coverage gaps, which is where access actually breaks.

### Meta's youth-addiction trial opens in Oakland

The Ninth Circuit dismissed as premature Meta's and TikTok's appeals seeking to kill more than 3,000 federal claims that the companies engineered addictive products for minors, holding that Section 230 "merely provides a defense to liability, not immunity from suit." Jury selection in the separate state-AG case began Wednesday in federal court in Oakland, with opening statements Aug 18 and a roughly six-week trial pointing to a verdict in early October. A 29-state coalition led by California, Colorado, Kentucky and New Jersey alleges Meta deliberately made Instagram and Facebook addictive to children and misled consumers about safety. Zuckerberg is on the plaintiffs' witness list. In March, a Los Angeles jury found Meta and Google negligent in the first such case to reach verdict, awarding \$6 million. Axios, CBS, Aug 10-12

The widely repeated "\$1.4 trillion" figure is a plaintiffs' demand, not an exposure estimate.

### Medicaid and CHIP to end coverage of minors' gender-transition care

Trump said Tuesday he had directed CMS Administrator Mehmet Oz to end Medicaid funding for gender-transition surgeries and hormone treatments for minors; CHIP will likewise stop covering them for those under 19. The reach matters clinically: in many states Medicaid and CHIP carry the bulk of pediatric coverage. GLAD's legal director signaled a challenge, saying the rule puts "barriers between parents and their ability to make the best medical decisions for their children." STAT, AP, Aug 11-12

**Developing.** The regulatory mechanism (guidance vs formal rule), effective date, and precise scope of covered services were not specified at announcement. Litigation is expected.

### State Department: more than 175,000 visas revoked

The department announced Monday it has revoked over 175,000 visas since January 2025, up from 100,000 announced in January, an acceleration from roughly 8,000 to about 10,000 per month. It attributed the majority to law enforcement encounters, with assault, DUI, theft and drug offenses leading, alongside visa-term violations, fraud, and national-security categories; charges rather than convictions suffice in many cases. Accompanying reporting noted ICE has detained more than 50 military family members and moved to deport 282 veterans after protections were rescinded in April 2025. WaPo, State Dept, The Hill, Aug 10

The headline numbers are the government's own and not independently verifiable; the veterans and military-family figures are single-thread reporting.

### A judge blocks Mamdani's pied-a-terre tax, and the homeowner list

State Supreme Court Justice Wayne Ozzi in Staten Island issued a TRO blocking enforcement of the new tax on non-primary residences valued at \$5 million or more, and separately ordered the city to take down a public list exposing names and addresses of more than 900,000 NYC homeowners. Plaintiffs argued the rollout created mass confusion and that the Sept 18 appeal deadline was inadequate. A hearing is set for Aug 31; the city moved immediately for leave to appeal and said it "will continue with the pied-a-terre's implementation." First real legal check on the administration's signature revenue measure, relevant if you are house-hunting in Brooklyn, though the tax targets second homes at a threshold well above most of the borough. CNN, Bloomberg, The Hill, Aug 10

### Alabama votes under the post-Callais map

Alabama held postponed special primaries Tuesday in the 1st, 2nd, 6th and 7th districts, after redrawing its map in May in response to the Supreme Court's April 29 ruling in *Louisiana v. Callais*. The 6-3 Alito opinion sharply narrowed Section 2 of the Voting Rights Act, and Alabama used it to eliminate one of its two Black-majority districts. In the redrawn 2nd, Rhett Marques led with about 54% to David Matthews' 24%; Gary Palmer took roughly 88% in the 6th and Ammie Akin about 77% in the 7th. Turnout was dismal. The broader Callais fallout across Southern states is the live story heading into November. WaPo, NPR, NBC, Aug 11

## Israel & Middle East

### Hormuz stays shut, and Iran's price keeps rising

Iran's Supreme National Security Council declared the strait will not reopen until the US "corrects its behavior," attaching conditions previously reserved for a permanent nuclear accord: permanent end to the war with Iran and its allies, lifting of the US naval blockade, US military withdrawal from the region, full war-damage compensation, sanctions relief, and unconditional release of frozen assets. Iran's new national security chief repeated Monday and Tuesday that it "will remain closed," and the IRGC says reopening is decoupled from the Oman-mediated track. The June 17 US-Iran memorandum on commercial transit collapsed over which routes vessels could use, and the roughly 60-day negotiating window expires within about a week. Trump says he is deliberately keeping a low profile while demanding compensation for "damage they've done over a 50-year period" and calling Iranian negotiators "very devious." Times of Israel, Al Jazeera, CNBC, France 24, Aug 8-11

### The Air Force One switch, and Ankara's counter-claim

Reporting Tuesday and Wednesday says Israel passed the US intelligence that Iran planned to use shoulder-fired SAMs against Trump's aircraft as he left Turkey after the July NATO summit, prompting the Secret Service to move him off Air Force One inside an airport catering truck to a smaller C-32A. Trump boarded the original aircraft in view of the press as a decoy; senior aides including Rubio stayed aboard. US officials reportedly took it seriously in part because civilian buildings sat as close as 500 meters from the air base. Trump confirmed the switch itself: "I go by Secret Service and the military. They wanted me to go in a different flight, different plane." Times of Israel, JPost, The Hill, Aug 11-12

**Sharp divergence.** Middle East Eye, citing unnamed Turkish officials, reports Ankara believes the intelligence was fabricated by Netanyahu's government to derail US-Iran negotiations and damage the Trump-Erdogan relationship, arguing Ankara was under total lockdown. One Ankara insider: "I guess they need heroes for an attack that never happened." Only the physical aircraft switch is independently confirmed. The plot itself is Israeli-sourced and denied by Turkey.

### Houthi missiles kill crew in the Bab al-Mandeb

The Yemeni-flagged, Egyptian-owned cargo vessel *Tihamah*, carrying food, was hit three times by ballistic missiles Tuesday and caught fire. Yemen's transport ministry reported four crew killed, three Pakistanis and one Indonesian, plus four wounded, and said the Houthis fired an additional missile at the ship as rescue teams approached, deliberately targeting the rescue. Wire services put the total at six when two rescuers are counted; that is a definitional difference, not a contradiction. Yemen's government called it a terrorist crime; Al Jazeera framed it as the first shipping fatalities since the US-Israel campaign against Iran began. The Houthis separately claimed an attack on a Saudi vessel in the same strait. AP, France 24, CBC, Al Jazeera, Aug 11-12

### Gaza: the Board of Peace lets the Yellow Line stay blurry

The US-controlled oversight body is deliberately declining to say whether Israel must pull back to the original October 2025 ceasefire line, using ambiguity to keep both sides at the table. Hamas approved the Board's 15-point disarmament proposal on July 30; after Board officials met Netanyahu in Jerusalem in early August, the Board said Israeli forces would not withdraw beyond the Yellow Line until Hamas fully completes decommissioning, a reversal of the immediate phased pullout Trump had announced. The Yellow Line left the IDF holding about 53% of the Strip; Netanyahu directed the IDF to occupy up to 70% during phase-two talks, and accounts conflict on whether troops actually advanced. Nickolay Mladenov is the Board's top Gaza envoy; Egypt, Qatar and Turkey are mediating, and Qatar publicly accused Israel Tuesday of "dragging its feet." Aid commitments of 600 trucks a day for two million people remain unmet. Times of Israel, Al Jazeera, Aug 4-12

### Qusra: a four-day settler standoff the IDF will not end

Settlers erected an outpost Sunday inside the yard of a Palestinian home in Qusra, south of Nablus, blocking the access road with stones. Qusai Abu Ridi and his son have been trapped in the house since Sunday, as has neighbor Youssef Hassan with his wife and two young daughters; Abu Ridi says settlers cut the water and electricity lines. The IDF publicly called the outposts "illegal, reprehensible and unacceptable" but did not demolish them, and in one documented instance troops prayed with the settlers rather than removing them. Border Police partially evacuated the site Tuesday, settlers clashed with soldiers, and forces withdrew again Wednesday. Haaretz reports its own crew was blocked and attacked en route while the IDF declined to intervene. The UN warns the West Bank is at a "breaking point." Haaretz, Times of Israel, JPost, Aug 10-12

### The Oct 27 election takes shape

Gilad Erdan and Yuli Edelstein named their new right-wing party "Unity," running on universal IDF conscription, Edelstein quit Likud over Haredi draft exemptions, and pledging to join only a broad consensus government. Netanyahu meanwhile tightened his grip on Likud's slate: Sa'ar and Katz got newly added reserved slots, two MKs are weighing quitting the primaries in protest, and Netanyahu waived tenure requirements to let ex-Otzma Yehudit MK Almog Cohen enter. Justice Minister Yariv Levin said Wednesday that liberal Supreme Court justices "will go extinct" if he is reelected, noting six of fifteen retire during the next government's term. Polling shows both blocs short of 61 with the new right-wing parties below threshold. Times of Israel, JPost, Ynet, Aug 11-12

**Handle with care.** Kan and Channel 12 reported Israel suspects two unnamed Arab states discussed normalization to "anesthetize" it while building for war. Identities are under censor blackout and no non-Israeli outlet has corroborated. Not established fact.

### Warsh's Fed and the most early dissent since 1970

Three FOMC members, Hammack, Kashkari and Logan, dissented in July in favor of a quarter-point hike, with nine including Chair Kevin Warsh voting to hold at 3.50-3.75%. Warsh has been categorical on the target: "There is no soft implicit target, not on this committee's watch," adding that "the five-plus years of inflation above target cannot be cured in nine weeks, or by a single month of modest price decreases." He has also deliberately pulled back forward guidance, arguing the bond market should do more of the pricing work. A weak July payrolls report with sizable downward revisions has since tempered near-term tightening bets. Axios, CNBC, US News, Jul 29 - Aug 11

### The AI buildout goes on the balance sheet

BofA now sees hyperscaler capex topping \$1.2 trillion over the next twelve months. ### 93% of health systems are running third-party AI. Almost none can govern it.

A new UPMC Center for Connected Medicine and KLAS survey finds 93% of health systems have deployed third-party AI tools, but 63% are still building the infrastructure to support them and are governing by ad hoc policy rather than an established framework; 44% have no dedicated data platform for testing AI at all. The report flags inconsistent data quality across departments and the absence of any universal metric for an AI tool's value, meaning most systems genuinely cannot tell whether what they deployed is helping. UPMC Enterprises' Ken Howard: go-live is the beginning of the work, not the end. WESA, Aug 11

Separately, CMS and FDA detailed a proposed RAPID pathway meant to carry breakthrough devices across the valley between clearance and reimbursement, historically where AI-enabled devices die commercially; the same STAT edition covers organized nursing pushing back on bedside AI expansion. Both behind STAT+. STAT, Aug 11

### Eclipse today

The moon's shadow crosses Greenland, Iceland, northern Spain and the Balearics, Europe's first totality in 27 years. Spanish observers get at most about 1 minute 50 seconds with the sun only a few degrees above the western horizon, which should be spectacular. It coincides with the Perseid peak and a six-planet pre-dawn alignment. NASA has separately had to publicly deny a viral claim that Earth loses gravity for seven seconds today. It does not. ## Worth reading (New Yorker & long-form)

### Jill Lepore, "Bot or Not", New Yorker, Aug 17 issue

Lepore's essay arrives alongside her new book, *The Rise and Fall of the Artificial State* (Norton, August 2026). Her argument, laid out at length in a TechCrunch interview Aug 9, is that private technology companies have quietly absorbed core functions of the state, infrastructure, speech regulation, surveillance, energy siting, without ever asking the public's consent, producing "a return to tyranny and mystification in the form of rule by algorithms, corporations, machines." She is pointed about Silicon Valley's leadership being bad readers who treat dystopian fiction as an instruction manual rather than a warning, and she argues the artificial state is self-defeating because it must consume the natural world it depends on. Her hope is unfashionably local: town meetings that vote down data centers. The reason it is worth your time is that it moves the AI argument off model capability and onto governance and consent, which is exactly the axis on which clinical AI is currently failing, see the UPMC finding above. newyorker.com

### Margaret Talbot, "The Delusions of John Roberts", New Yorker, ran online Aug 11

A Talbot critical profile built around the thesis in its title: that the Chief Justice's self-image as the institutionalist steward of a Court above politics, calling balls and strikes, is contradicted by the Court's actual output under his leadership. Talbot's method is to build the case from the subject's own words and opinions rather than partisan framing, which is what makes her pieces durable. Timely given *Callais* and the Alabama map. newyorker.com
