## International

### Thirty-seven dead outside Kyiv, and a Ukrainian investigation into Ukraine

A Russian drone struck a munitions warehouse in Myla, in the Bucha district west of Kyiv, overnight Friday into Saturday. The strike itself was one thing; the secondary detonations were the disaster. At least 37 people were killed, roughly 50 residential buildings damaged, fire spread to homes and a restaurant, and nearly 400 residents were evacuated. Among the dead was Taras Didych, mayor of the neighboring village of Dmytrivka. The toll climbed through Saturday from an initial 27. Russia launched at least two missiles and 267 drones nationwide in the same assault, killing three more civilians in Kyiv including a two-year-old.

The politically consequential part is what Zelenskyy did next: he ordered a criminal investigation into what he called "terrible negligence," since Ukrainian law bars storing ammunition near residential areas. Prosecutors are examining permits and storage practice, echoing a similar incident in Vyshneve in July. It is a rare instance of Kyiv publicly turning the blame inward mid-war. Al Jazeera, NPR/AP, Bloomberg, Aug 29. Confirmed across outlets.

### Ukrainian intelligence says Moscow has ordered planning for a northern offensive

Pavlo Palisa, deputy head of the presidential office, said Ukraine has verified from multiple sources that Russia's political leadership tasked its military command with drafting several variants of a ground operation toward Kyiv and Chernihiv. Palisa was careful: this is contingency planning, there are no dates, and there is no sign of Russian strike groupings massing near the northern border. Separately Zelensky called for scaling long-range drone strikes into Russia to 1,000 per day, up from roughly 300 now.

**Sourcing flag.** Multiple outlets carried this, but the underlying claim is single-sourced to Ukrainian officials describing their own intelligence. Treat it as an assertion with a purpose, not an established fact.

Bloomberg, Aug 29.

### Nepal-Tibet flood disaster: 750 dead, more than 3,000 missing

The toll from the August 26 glacier collapse and resulting flash floods reached 750 on Sunday, with 3,044 still missing. Nepal has recovered 734 bodies with 2,498 missing, including 589 foreign nationals; Chinese authorities confirm at least 16 dead in Tibet with 546 missing. The missing foreigners span 23 countries: 104 Nepalis, 49 Indians, 33 Latvians, 18 Americans, 15 Britons. Floods and mudslides tore through settlements along a 72km stretch of the Trishuli River across seven districts. The triggering landslide registered magnitude 5.2 on local seismometers, and scientists attribute the glacier collapse in part to warming. Rescue work has been repeatedly suspended since Friday over weather and the fear that a landslide-dammed lake on the Nepal-China border could burst; Nepal issued fresh flood alerts Sunday. CNN live, ABC, Al Jazeera, UN News, Aug 30. Confirmed.

### Iceland says no to the EU

Icelandic voters rejected opening accession negotiations 52.8% to 47.2% in Saturday's referendum. Prime Minister Kristrun Frostadottir had pledged that a No would take membership off the agenda for the rest of her mandate, which runs to 2028. Opponents ran on sovereignty and fisheries, and roughly 90% of Icelandic fishing companies opposed accession; supporters argued for lower interest rates and security guarantees against the backdrop of American rhetoric about Greenland. The vote was technically non-binding, but the government has committed to honoring it. Iceland remains in the EEA and Schengen. For Brussels, which had hoped for a showcase enlargement win and had signaled flexibility on fisheries through Enlargement Commissioner Marta Kos, it is a real setback. Al Jazeera, Euronews, Aug 30.

### Hormuz stays shut, and Iran's new supreme leader speaks

Ayatollah Mojtaba Khamenei issued a written message for Islamic Unity Week urging Gulf states to "identify your real enemy, understand his plan and confront it", reported as his first public communication since the February 28 US-Israeli strikes that killed his predecessor. He argued that anyone sowing division among Muslims serves Islam's enemies, and asked whether Palestinians would have been left "so helpless" had Muslims been united. Notably, the message never names the enemy; attributing it explicitly to the US or Israel is inference.

The material story is the strait. Deputy Foreign Minister Kazem Gharibabadi said flatly that "the Strait of Hormuz is closed," tying implementation of an Iran-Oman maritime corridor deal to Washington honoring June commitments. The IRGC Navy says the strait is shut to any vessel not coordinating with Tehran; CENTCOM insists it is open. The data favors Tehran's version: seven commodity vessels transited Thursday, against a ten-day average of 15 and a normal run rate near 85 per day. Roughly 400 ships and 6,000 seafarers remain trapped in the Gulf. Iran's annual inflation hit 66% in July and trade volumes are down nearly 35%. Qatar's prime minister visited Tehran Thursday; Oman, Qatar and Pakistan are all pushing mediation. Al Jazeera, Reuters syndication, Aug 30.

### Norway's King Harald V dies at 89

Harald V died peacefully in his sleep at 6:35am local time Friday at Oslo University Hospital Rikshospitalet, aged 89, after 35 years on the throne and a hospitalization on August 17 for haemolytic anaemia. His son immediately succeeded him as King Haakon VIII; Mette-Marit becomes Queen and Ingrid Alexandra is now Crown Princess and heir apparent. State funeral arrangements are underway. AP, Al Jazeera, NPR, Aug 28.

### Myanmar's junta is winning ground back

Resistance forces are losing key towns as the military recovers territory lost after 2023, aided by conscription-replenished units, intensified air strikes and Chinese diplomatic and military support. The Myanmar Internet Project counted 520 air strikes from April through June across 87 townships, killing at least 314 civilians including 41 children. In Karenni State, Operation Yan Naing Min has taken multiple towns since July, with Demoso falling August 19. In Tanintharyi, roughly 700 troops have advanced since July, burning villages and sealing off land marked for a Russia-backed special economic zone and port. Al Jazeera analysis, corroborated by Asia Sentinel and Burma News International, Aug 30.

## United States

### The Venezuela oil deal, announced by social media post

Trump announced Friday night an agreement negotiated by Rubio, Hegseth and Venezuelan acting president Delcy Rodriguez covering 17 oil fields with proven potential of 65 billion barrels. Per an anonymous US official, the structure creates a new private company, the US partnering with an unnamed private operator, with 100-year development rights, in which the US receives 55% of effective output including an ownership stake and the right to buy oil at cost. If accurate, that would make the US government the second-largest corporate holder of proven reserves after Saudi Aramco. Caracas projects roughly \$100bn in investment and \$209bn in taxes.

The context explains the urgency: gasoline averaged \$4.09/gal Friday against \$3.21 a year ago, the war with Iran has hit six months with Hormuz badly disrupted, and the SPR fell below 300 million barrels in early August. It comes roughly nine months after the US military operation that seized Nicolas Maduro to face narcoterrorism charges. Venezuelans protested in Caracas Saturday.

**Sourcing flag.** The announcement is confirmed. The *terms* come entirely from one anonymous US official, and the White House has released almost no documentation. A 100-year, 55%-of-output arrangement is an extraordinary claim; do not treat the specifics as verified.

AP, NPR, Guardian, Al Jazeera, Aug 28-29.

### The Canada tariffs rest on a statute nobody has ever used

A week ago Trump invoked Section 338 of the Tariff Act of 1930, the Smoot-Hawley statute, to impose a 50% tax on \$20 billion of Canadian imports over alleged discrimination against US dairy, auto and alcohol exports. No president has ever used the authority and it has never been litigated. Ryan Majerus, a King & Spalding partner and former US trade official, called it "literally a blank canvas." Some trade lawyers argue the Depression-era provision was superseded by later trade law, leaving genuine doubt the tariffs survive challenge. Ottawa is retaliating dollar-for-dollar: Carney has committed to hitting roughly \$28 billion of US goods starting September 8, concentrated in steel, dairy, appliances, agricultural equipment, pulp and paper, and electronics. AP, Boston Globe, CNBC, Aug 29-30.

### Two measles deaths, and a cabinet secretary suggesting they were invented

Pennsylvania reported two measles-associated deaths in unvaccinated residents, including a newborn from Lancaster County, the state's first in roughly 35 years and the first US measles deaths of 2026. Governor Josh Shapiro said vaccination could have prevented them. HHS Secretary Robert F. Kennedy Jr. responded on X that the deaths "may even have been altogether fabricated," suggesting a Shapiro staffer invented them, after claiming officials "cannot find any measles deaths in Lancaster County." Shapiro replied that "there's real-life consequences to spreading misinformation." Kennedy made materially similar claims about three measles deaths last year.

Amesh Adalja of the Johns Hopkins Center for Health Security put the cost plainly: "Now it's turned political. It's no longer about getting answers." The backdrop is the worst national measles surge in more than three decades. AP, The Hill, NBC, CNN. Deaths announced ~Aug 25; the political fight is this weekend's story.

### An Afghan man with judicial protection was deported to the Central African Republic

A deportation flight landed in Bangui on Saturday carrying dozens of people, including 12 Afghans, 8 Iranians, and several Nepalis and Nicaraguans, according to Savi Arvey of Human Rights First. Among them was an Afghan man in his early twenties who had been granted judicial protection against removal to Afghanistan over Taliban persecution. His attorney Alma David says one brother served in the Afghan National Army and now lives in the US, and another, a US-trained pilot, was killed by the Taliban. Advocates say thousands have been sent to roughly two dozen third countries under largely undisclosed agreements, which immigration lawyers describe as an indirect route to returning asylum seekers to the countries they fled. AP via NPR, Aug 30. Single wire, sourced to the attorney and an advocacy group. Developing.

## Israel

### A settler rampage in Qusra, a rare Netanyahu condemnation, and no arrests

Dozens of Israeli settlers stormed the Palestinian village of Qusra near Nablus on Saturday, capping nearly three weeks in which settlers besieged Palestinian homes and blocked supply routes. Circulated video shows settlers setting fires, barricading themselves inside a Palestinian home with the family still inside, and throwing stones while IDF soldiers stood nearby. Times of Israel reported that troops fired tear gas into the home and detained residents while the settlers left.

Netanyahu called it "criminal violence" by "a handful of rioters" that is "harming Israel's standing in the world," and the Foreign Ministry called the perpetrators "violent extremists" whose acts are "a disgrace." As of Sunday, ToI reported no arrests despite identifiable footage, only three vehicles confiscated. Earlier in August, US Ambassador Mike Huckabee called the Qusra settlers "terrorists," unusually sharp language from this administration. AP counts at least 1,103 Palestinians killed by Israeli forces or settlers in the West Bank since October 2023, and at least 48 Israelis killed.

AP, Times of Israel, ABC Australia, Aug 29-30. The soldiers-standing-by account rests on video plus residents' testimony; the IDF says it deployed to separate the sides and police opened an investigation.

### Gaza strikes continue despite American requests to scale back

Three Palestinians were killed Saturday: two in a strike on a motorbike in Tel al-Hawa in Gaza City, and a farmer by tank fire near Deir al-Balah, with several wounded near the Shuhada al-Aqsa hospital compound. On Sunday the IDF said it killed two Hamas Nukhba Force members in Khan Younis and one operative in Deir al-Balah, having named several Hamas commanders on Friday and Saturday and saying all were rebuilding capabilities in violation of the ceasefire. Gaza's health ministry says at least 1,313 Palestinians have been killed and 4,361 wounded by Israeli fire since the ceasefire took effect on October 10, 2025.

The diplomatic frame matters: the strikes come days after Nikolay Mladenov, high representative of Trump's Board of Peace, publicly criticized Israel at the UN Security Council, and after US negotiators asked Israel to draw down. Phase-two talks remain stuck on the sequencing of Hamas disarmament against Israeli withdrawal.

**Attribution.** IDF target identifications are Israeli official claims. Casualty totals are Gaza health ministry figures. Neither is independently verified.

Otzma Yehudit leader Itamar Ben Gvir rejected Netanyahu's public appeal to run on a joint slate with Smotrich's Religious Zionism, his party calling the decision "final and complete" with "no talks, or meetings, or discussions on the topic." ### Lebanon: talks stalled, strikes and demolitions continuing

A second round of Lebanon-Israel talks in Rome, from August 4, centered on "pilot zones" in which the IDF would withdraw gradually from occupied villages while the Lebanese army takes control and verifies Hezbollah disarmament. ## Economic & Markets

### Warsh puts a hike on the table, and every asset class reprices

The single story of the week. In his first Jackson Hole keynote as Fed chair, Kevin Warsh said inflation is "running above our 2% target" and that "the Fed's predominant focus right now should be on prices," calling PCE at 3.7% concerning and warning that better-than-expected summer readings do not show underlying trends have meaningfully improved. He said the Fed "must be confident that underlying inflation is moving to our objective clearly and at sufficient speed", about as close to pre-committing as a chair gets. He also noted that he and colleagues who held rates steady in July "thought the wiser course was to await new information," then said that evidence hasn't materialized.

Market-implied odds of a September increase jumped to roughly 60% from about 35% the prior day, against a current target range of 3.50-3.75%. Three officials had already dissented in favor of a hike at the August FOMC. The genuinely notable thing is the inversion: markets entered 2026 pricing cuts and are exiting August pricing hikes. The next CPI print, days before the September 16-17 meeting, is now the whole ballgame. Adam Posen of the Peterson Institute thinks Warsh has boxed himself in: "if you don't hike in September, people may ask what's going on." It also puts the chair in open conflict with a president demanding lower rates. Federal Reserve transcript, CNBC, AP, Washington Post, Al Jazeera, Aug 28. Hike-odds figures vary by source from 57% to 68%; ~60% is the fair read.

### Friday's tape

Equities faded into the close but held the week. The S&P 500 slipped 0.25% to 7,711.76, the Nasdaq fell 0.52% to 26,402.42, and the Dow was essentially flat at 53,559.99, off 9.45 points. All three still finished higher on the week, S&P +0.5%, Nasdaq +0.9%, Dow +0.5% for its first winning week in three. Rate-sensitive and high-multiple tech took the brunt of the afternoon selling, since stocks had rallied into the speech on the assumption Warsh would leave a cut on the table.

Rates showed a textbook hawkish flattening: the 2-year jumped roughly 8-11bp to 4.34%, the 10-year rose to about 4.72%, the 30-year to roughly 5.20%, and the dollar index gained 0.6% to 99.66. The short end sold off while the long end held comparatively firm, which is the market endorsing a chair who treats inflation as the primary problem. Worth remembering the 30-year touched above 5.3% earlier in August, its highest since 2007, before Bessent announced long-dated buybacks would at least double from \$2bn to \$4bn from September through November. Yahoo Finance, Barchart, CNBC, Seeking Alpha.

The Stripe/Advent International consortium abandoned its pursuit of PayPal after offering more than \$50 billion. The bid reportedly stood at \$60.50 per share, valuing PayPal above \$53 billion; the board deemed it too low and had not sent a formal reply. PYPL fell as much as 16% premarket and closed down roughly 12%. A completed deal would have ranked among the largest leveraged buyouts on record, and reporting suggests a renewed approach is possible if conditions change. Bloomberg-originated, picked up by 6+ outlets, Aug 28. Sourced as "people familiar," not a company announcement.

### The AI trade: a blowout is now the price of admission

Nvidia reported fiscal Q2 2027 revenue of \$96.22 billion, about 5.7% above the guidance midpoint, with non-GAAP EPS of \$2.22. Data Center was \$89.0 billion, roughly 92% of the total, and the company guided Q3 to \$108.0 billion, an \$11.8 billion sequential step. Two days later Marvell beat on both revenue and adjusted EPS and guided Q3 revenue about \$116 million above the Street, and the stock sank anyway. That divergence is the actual signal: after a roughly 180% year-to-date run, an in-line beat no longer clears the bar, and positioning is now punishing anything short of a blowout. Separately and single-sourced, Nvidia's contract server builders reportedly told Microsoft, Google and Oracle that AI server system prices will rise more than 15% on early-2027 Vera Rubin and Grace Blackwell shipments. Kiplinger, SiliconANGLE, Aug 26-27.

### Commodities and crypto take the hawkish hit

## Worth reading

### "The End of the European Summer", Eric Klinenberg, The New Yorker

Klinenberg is the NYU sociologist whose *Heat Wave: A Social Autopsy of Disaster in Chicago* essentially invented the modern study of heat mortality, the book that established that who dies in a heat wave is a question about social isolation and neighborhood infrastructure, not temperature. Here he returns to his defining subject at continental scale, arriving in Milan as Europe's third heat wave of the summer entered its fiercest stretch and traveling across a continent whose cities were never built for these temperatures and are now retrofitting themselves with trees, shade and water. Europe is warming more than twice as fast as the global average. The piece is sharpest on the political economy of adaptation, one line notes that losing some of the 160,000 tourists who visit Barcelona daily "ranks low on the priority lists of most officials and residents." It is the rare climate essay about municipal competence rather than doom, which is what makes it useful. Aug 24-31 issue, published online Aug 17. [Read it](https://www.newyorker.com/magazine/2026/08/24/the-end-of-the-european-summer/)

### "The Scientist's Children", Rachel Aviv, The New Yorker

This one is squarely in your wheelhouse and genuinely hard to read. Aviv investigates Nobel laureate Carleton Gajdusek, who won for his work on kuru in Papua New Guinea and then brought dozens of children from the field back to the United States, many of whom he sexually molested. It is a characteristic Aviv construction: less an expose than a study of how scientific prestige and institutional deference build a container in which abuse becomes unspeakable, and of what the field's celebration of an eccentric genius cost the specific people in his house. Pairs with her collection *You Won't Get Free of It*, out in July. Aug 24-31 issue, p. 36. I could not verify a direct newyorker.com URL for this one and will not publish a guessed slug, find it in the [Longreads listing](https://longreads.com/tag/the-new-yorker/) or the issue archive.
