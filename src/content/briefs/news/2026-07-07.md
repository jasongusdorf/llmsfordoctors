## International

### NATO summit opens in Ankara, on Trump's terms

The 36th NATO summit convenes today and tomorrow in Ankara, the alliance's first Turkish summit since Istanbul 2004, and it is already shaped by Donald Trump before he lands. Reporting from Al Jazeera and CNN indicates Ukraine has been deliberately pushed down the agenda, no Zelensky plenary address, while Trump renews his signature demand that members lift defense spending. Zelensky is expected to get a bilateral with Trump on the sidelines Wednesday rather than center stage. The optics are pointed: an alliance summit about European security in which the marquee war is a footnote. (Al Jazeera, CNN, CNBC, live/developing.)

### Russia's deadly strike on Kyiv sets the mood

Hours before leaders gathered, Russia hammered Kyiv with ballistic missiles and drones in one of the deadliest attacks in weeks, roughly 19 killed in the capital and six more in the surrounding region (some early counts put the citywide toll near 21). Zelensky attributed the death toll partly to a shortage of interceptors, and in the FT framed the conflict's trajectory as a "battle in the sky" that air defense and drones will decide. Death-toll figures still firming; treat exact numbers as provisional. (FT, CNBC, wire reports.)

### Iran buries Khamenei as his successor stays invisible

Iran is staging an enormous, days-long state funeral for Ali Khamenei, assassinated on Feb. 28 in a joint US-Israeli strike on his Tehran office at the start of this year's war, with processions across Tehran, Mashhad and into Iraq running July 4–9. CNN and Time describe the scale as a deliberate projection of strength and defiance aimed at Washington. The conspicuous hole in that message: the new supreme leader, Mojtaba Khamenei, has not appeared. Officials cite health complications; earlier US and Reuters reporting said he was gravely wounded, facial and leg injuries, possibly the loss of a leg, in the strike that killed his father. He communicates only through written statements. Successor's condition and whereabouts are single-thread and unverified. (CNN, Time, Al Jazeera.)

### Tanker struck in the Strait of Hormuz

Early Tuesday, the Qatari LNG carrier *Al Rekayyat* (owned by state shipper Nakilat) was hit by what security analysts assess as a drone or missile about eight nautical miles east of Limah, Oman. The strike directly tests the US-Iran understanding meant to keep the waterway open, and it reversed a recent slide in energy prices: Brent ticked up and European gas jumped as much as 4.5–6% in Asian trading. It's a reminder that the chokepoint remains the market's most sensitive nerve. (gCaptain, Rigzone, FT, Reuters.)

### Macron in Damascus, and explosions nearby

Emmanuel Macron became the first major Western leader to visit Syria under Ahmad al-Sharaa, and the visit was punctuated by two improvised blasts, one in a parked car, one in a trash container, near the Four Seasons and the tourism ministry, wounding at least 18, including four police. Macron's office said he did not hear the explosions and the meeting with al-Sharaa proceeded on schedule; Syrian authorities stressed the blasts fell outside the French delegation's security zone. Symbolically fraught for a government trying to convince the West it can hold order. (Al Jazeera, NPR, NBC.)

## US

### Johnson's border win exposes a restless GOP

Speaker Mike Johnson delivered Trump his biggest legislative victory of the year by getting a border-security crackdown to the floor, but only after cutting a deal with hardliners led by Chip Roy. CNN reports a center-right bloc is now threatening to attach its own demands, including a legal-residency pathway for long-settled undocumented immigrants and better farmworker visa programs. The episode is less a show of strength than of how narrow and unruly the majority remains. (CNN Politics.)

### Migration crackdown reaches citizens' spouses

NPR documents how the administration's drive to slow legal immigration is now hitting the spouses of US citizens: paused immigrant visas for people from some 75 countries, sharper scrutiny at green-card interviews, and a widened deportation net. The upshot is American families separated or afraid to engage the system at all, a politically potent second-order effect of policies pitched as targeting the border. (NPR.)

### Also on the domestic ledger

The Supreme Court cleared Texas's App Store Accountability Act to take effect while litigation continues, requiring parental consent for minors to download most apps. Separately, Memphis police said National Guard troops fatally shot an armed man in the early hours of July 5, and thunderstorms threatened efforts to contain a large wildfire in southern Colorado. (Democracy Now, PBS NewsHour, NPR.)

## Israel

### Hamas dissolves its Gaza government

In the region's most consequential political move of the day, Hamas announced it has dissolved the Government Emergency Committee that has run Gaza on its behalf for nearly two decades. Emergency-committee head Mohammed al-Farra submitted his resignation, and Hamas said it is ready to hand civilian administration to the National Committee for the Administration of Gaza (NCAG), the technocratic body envisioned under the peace plan; its head, Ali Shaath, said the committee will assume duties once resources are in place. The crucial caveat, stressed across Times of Israel, Al Jazeera and the Washington Post: Hamas is stepping back from day-to-day governance, not surrendering its political or military role, it still ties any discussion of disarmament to a broader Palestinian administration. A real step, but well short of the disarmament Israel demands. (Times of Israel, Al Jazeera, Washington Post, PBS.)

### Strikes continue under the "ceasefire"

Israeli air attacks killed at least six Palestinians and wounded more than 20 on Monday, Gaza medical sources said, as the truce declared last autumn continues to fray. Gaza's health ministry now attributes 1,072 deaths and roughly 3,463 injuries to strikes since the ceasefire took hold, figures Israel disputes and that cannot be independently verified, but which underline how nominal the calm has become. Casualty totals are from the Gaza health ministry; independently unverified. (Al Jazeera.)

## Economic & Markets

### The week's marquee read: Fed minutes

Attention turns to the minutes from June's FOMC meeting, Kevin Warsh's first as chair. The committee held rates at 3.50–3.75% and markets read the decision as hawkish: Warsh stripped the statement to roughly 130 words (from 300-plus), dropped easing-leaning forward guidance, and the dot plot swung from signaling a cut toward flagging a possible hike. With May CPI at 4.2%, a three-year high, and Warsh calling inflation "too high," the minutes will be parsed for how firmly the door to cuts has closed. (CNBC, Kiplinger, Chase.)

### Labor data and the tape

June US unemployment ticked *down*, per the FT, reinforcing the hawkish case and complicating any dovish read. Equities remain near records with the S&P 500 around 8,000. The standout single-stock move: Samsung fell about 10% even after posting record quarterly profit on the AI boom, a reminder that in this tape, guidance and cycle fears can outweigh a headline beat. The Hormuz tanker strike (above) added a geopolitical bid to oil and European gas. On the primary-market front, the FT flags skepticism that OpenAI and Anthropic could float smoothly, even as Wall Street turns bullish on SpaceX. (FT.)

## Also notable

**Football meets politics.** The US crashed out of the World Cup amid an extraordinary refereeing saga: a Trump phone call reportedly prompted FIFA to reverse a red-card suspension of a Team USA player, then reinstate it, drawing sharp UEFA criticism of an "incomprehensible" decision and forcing FIFA president Gianni Infantino to defend the body's integrity. (FT, NPR.)

**Defense and diplomacy.** Canada chose Germany's TKMS for a landmark submarine contract while telling the UAE it is not ready for its proposed C\$70bn investment; Trump renewed his attacks on Italy's Giorgia Meloni. (FT.)
