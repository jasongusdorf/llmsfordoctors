## International

The Himalayan flood

The single largest story of the last 36 hours. Around 8:40 a.m. local time Wednesday, an enormous block of ice broke off a glacier at roughly 5,200 meters and fell some 1,200 meters into the valley below, slamming into the Lhende River about 20 km northeast of the Nepal-China border crossing. The ice avalanche briefly dammed the river; when the dam failed, the Trishuli rose roughly nine meters in thirty minutes and tore through Nepal's Rasuwa, Nuwakot and Dhading districts. Nepal's foreign minister, Shisir Khanal, called it "one of the largest natural disasters we have experienced in recent memory," adding that "towns after towns have been completely wiped out in a span of a couple hours." Nepali officials report 270-360 dead and more than 800 missing, including a large contingent of foreign nationals (roughly 177 Indians, many on the Mount Kailash pilgrimage, plus reported Americans, Australians, Britons and Canadians); Chinese state media report three dead and over 550 missing in Gyirong County, Tibet. The key scientific detail: there was no downpour. The USGS initially logged a magnitude-5.2 seismic event and later concluded the signal was generated by the landslide itself. NYT surveillance footage verification shows crowds running ahead of the water at the Gyirong crossing before it engulfed buildings and vehicles. The Iran war, six months in

Qatar's prime minister, Sheikh Mohammed bin Abdulrahman Al Thani, was in Tehran Thursday meeting Foreign Minister Abbas Araghchi, President Masoud Pezeshkian and lead negotiator Mohammad Bagher Qalibaf, as the US-Iran war approaches the six-month mark with no clear end. The stated agenda was "conditions conducive to dialogue" and freedom of navigation in the Strait of Hormuz, including a proposed temporary corridor and demining. The mediation is strained: an Iranian general recently accused Qatar of detaining three pilots shot down early in the war, which Doha denies, and Qatar says it has absorbed more than 700 missile and drone attacks it blames on Tehran since the February 28 US and Israeli strikes. The June ceasefire Qatar helped broker has unraveled, largely over the strait, which both Tehran and Washington have blockaded. Separately, Trump declared the main shipping lane demined; Bloomberg reports US allies privately doubt that. Iran, meanwhile, is barring IAEA inspectors from the bombed nuclear sites outright, with Qalibaf saying inspectors "will not be granted any access." Ukraine: Moscow threatens Britain directly

Russian foreign ministry spokeswoman Maria Zakharova told reporters Thursday that "any British military installations and equipment in Ukraine and beyond its borders could be targeted" in response to Ukrainian strikes inside Russia using UK-supplied Storm Shadow cruise missiles, saying London is "one step away" from complicity in terrorism. It follows Moscow's claim that a Storm Shadow hit a shopping centre in Russian-held Donetsk, and PM Andy Burnham's August 24 announcement that Britain will share Storm Shadow production technology with Kyiv. This is the sharpest Russian threat yet aimed squarely at a NATO member. On the battlefield, Ukrainian drones struck the Lukoil NORSI refinery at Kstovo, roughly 800 km from Ukrainian territory, halting crude processing at Russia's fourth-largest refinery for the fifth time in under five months; Reuters reporting indicates all of Lukoil's main Russian refineries are now offline. Russia answered with mass strikes on Kharkiv, Kyiv and Odesa. Layered on top: CIA Director John Ratcliffe made an unannounced eight-hour trip to Moscow Tuesday, confirmed by Dmitry Peskov as intelligence-level talks with no Putin meeting. Mladic dies in The Hague

Ratko Mladic, the Bosnian Serb general convicted in 2017 of genocide, crimes against humanity and war crimes, died Thursday in custody at the UN Detention Unit. He was 83 by the NYT's account, 84 by the FT's and most wires; the discrepancy is unresolved. Mladic commanded the forces that killed roughly 8,000 Bosniak men and boys at Srebrenica in July 1995, the deadliest massacre in Europe since the Second World War and the only act judicially recognized as genocide in Europe since 1945, and conducted the 44-month siege of Sarajevo. He evaded capture for nearly sixteen years before his 2011 arrest in a village north of Belgrade. Judge Alphons Orie called his crimes "among the most heinous known to humankind"; the life sentence was upheld on appeal in 2021. ## United States

Immigration enforcement at record scale

ICE arrested 49,571 people in July, the highest monthly total of Trump's second term, up roughly 15 percent from June and about 70 percent above February, per government data released to the Deportation Data Project and analyzed by the AP. That is more than 1,580 arrests a day against a White House target of 2,000. CBS puts the share with no criminal record at a record 800-plus people per day, and NYT's interactive makes the same point: the growth is overwhelmingly in arrests of people without criminal convictions. Nearly 20,000 of July's arrests were in Texas and Florida, both heavy 287(g) participants; ICE has added roughly 12,000 officers in the past year. For scale, monthly arrests were just over 8,000 in December 2024. Two related threads: officials said Thursday they will accelerate deportations to Haiti, and a federalism fight is brewing after a Texas jail released an ICE agent accused of shooting a Venezuelan man rather than honor Minnesota's extradition request. The Fed independence fight reaches its deadline

Governor Lisa Cook's 21-day window to answer the White House's removal letter closed Wednesday, and her lawyers Abbe Lowell and Norm Eisen filed a rebuttal: "Governor Cook has never committed mortgage fraud or any intentional wrongdoing, and there is no legally cognizable cause for removing her." The dual primary-residence designations on a 2021 Atlanta condo and her longtime Michigan home were, they argue, "an entirely inadvertent oversight," supported by an expert opinion from Suffolk Law's Kathleen Engel finding no reliable inference of deceptive intent, and by the pointed observation that Trump himself took two Florida primary-residence mortgages seven weeks apart in 1993 while living in New York. The allegations originate in criminal referrals from FHFA head William Pulte; nothing has been tested in court. The Supreme Court ruled 5-4 against the first removal attempt in June, with Roberts holding Cook was entitled to notice and an opportunity to respond, which is precisely what this process is meant to satisfy. No president has removed a Fed governor since 1913. Her term runs to 2034; the decision now sits with Trump. [SCOTUSblog](https://www.scotusblog.com/2026/08/fed-governor-lisa-cook-responds-to-trump-administrations-letter-stating-that-it-is-considering-h/) · [Reuters](https://kfgo.com/2026/08/26/lawyer-for-feds-cook-targeted-by-trump-says-there-is-no-grounds-for-dismissal/)\

Mail ballots, ten weeks out

Health policy, and this one is yours

Pennsylvania confirmed **two measles deaths in Lancaster County**, both patients unvaccinated and one reportedly an infant. These are the first US measles deaths of 2026 and Pennsylvania's first in 35 years. The state has 393 confirmed cases across 28 counties, roughly half in Lancaster, with about 20 percent requiring hospitalization and zero confirmed cases among people who received both MMR doses. Nationally the US is past 2,700 cases, the most since 1991. That collides with the August 10 executive order, "Delivering Gold Standard Childhood Vaccine Recommendations for Americans," which directs cutting universally recommended childhood vaccines from 17 to 11, spacing remaining doses across more visits, splitting MMR into three separate injections (no monovalent products are currently marketed in the US), and directing DOJ to sue states that do not provide religious and medical exemptions. ACIP has still issued no flu or COVID guidance heading into respiratory season, while AAP, AAFP and ACP are all independently recommending routine vaccination and AAP has published a schedule diverging from CDC's. Practically: for the fall you should expect to counsel against a federal schedule your specialty societies do not endorse. ## Israel

Washington's own envoy criticizes Israel at the Security Council

The most consequential Israel item of the window, because of who said it. Nikolay Mladenov, high representative of the Board of Peace running Trump's 20-point Gaza plan, told the UN Security Council that the plan has "moved from the negotiating table to the engineering table," and then turned on Israel directly. On the near-daily strikes: "After three years of war and 20 years of military operations, will another strike on a munitions depot stop Hamas from rearming? It will not. It only delays the demilitarization that would happen." He called Israel's shelter policy "not acceptable" with winter approaching and roughly two million Palestinians still in tents, warning that "Gaza does not have it in it to have another catastrophe." He also held Hamas to account, noting it accepted the disarmament proposal on July 30 but that the commitment is "still to be met in full," with weapons circulating and tunnels operational. Erez stays shut; Europeans get shown the door

## Also notable

Medicine

The FDA approved **Revolution Medicines' daraxonrasib (Rasonque)**, a once-daily oral multi-RAS inhibitor, for adults with metastatic pancreatic adenocarcinoma after at least one prior systemic therapy or who are not candidates for multi-agent chemotherapy. Median overall survival was 13.2 months versus 6.7 on standard chemotherapy, roughly a doubling, and the approval landed about six and a half months ahead of its PDUFA date. RAS was considered undruggable for four decades; pancreatic adenocarcinoma has among the worst survival of any major malignancy. NYT already has a follow-on piece about the pipeline behind it ([FDA](https://www.fda.gov/news-events/press-announcements/fda-approves-first-class-targeted-therapy-metastatic-pancreatic-cancer) · [NYT](https://www.nytimes.com/2026/08/27/science/pancreatic-cancer-drug-fda-testing.html)). Separately, *Nature Medicine* published a quasi-experimental study exploiting the abrupt US switch from live-attenuated to recombinant shingles vaccine, comparing adults 60+ vaccinated just before versus just after the transition, a design that neutralizes healthy-vaccinee bias. Over seven years Shingrix was associated with a 9 percent reduction in overall cardiovascular burden, 10 percent lower heart disease, 12 percent lower heart failure and 12 percent lower stroke risk in men, with related dementia signals. Still observational, but the strongest evidence yet for off-target cardiovascular benefit from a vaccine ([Nature Medicine](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41591-026-04606-0) · [STAT](https://www.statnews.com/2026/08/26/shingles-vaccine-shingrix-reduces-heart-disease-dementia-risk-oxford-study/)).

Meta's \$17 billion

Judge Yvonne Gonzalez Rogers approved Meta's settlement with state attorneys general over claims it engineered Facebook and Instagram to addict minors. The headline number varies by outlet for a structural reason: \$12.1 billion is guaranteed over ten years, plus \$5 billion contingent on YouTube and TikTok settling and adopting comparable limits, which is how you get "\$17.1 billion" in some coverage and "up to \$18 billion" once Texas's separate \$1 billion deal is added. The remedies may matter more than the money: a default two-hour daily teen time limit, blocked access midnight to 6 a.m. without parental consent, notifications muted during school hours, enhanced under-18 age assurance, hardened barriers against adults contacting teens, and restrictions on like counts and cosmetic-surgery filters, all under an independent auditor. Meta admitted no wrongdoing; appeal rights were waived ([CNN](https://www.cnn.com/2026/08/26/tech/meta-states-settle-trial-children) · [FT](https://www.ft.com/content/21dcbd96-ee57-445c-a9ba-0938c49b91c0)).

## Worth reading

"The Children a Scientist Took Home," The New Yorker

This is the one for you. D. Carleton Gajdusek won the 1976 Nobel for his work on kuru, the fatal spongiform encephalopathy confined to the eastern highlands of Papua New Guinea, and in doing so opened the door to prion biology. He also brought dozens of boys home from the South Pacific and was later convicted of child molestation. The piece opens not with him but with Luwi Ikabala, now 57, who sets up a sewing machine each morning beside piles of sweet potatoes in the Goroka market, reads Dostoyevsky between customers, and has kept a journal for forty years at Gajdusek's urging, with entries headed "Air of freedom" and "Who am I?" He calls Gajdusek "Father of America." It is a study of what scientific greatness costs the people it is practiced on, and it holds both facts in view at once without resolving them for you. [Read it](https://www.newyorker.com/magazine/2026/08/24/the-scientist-who-lived-with-dozens-of-children-from-the-south-pacific)
