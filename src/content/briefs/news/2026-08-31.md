**TL;DR.** The US and Iran traded fire for the first time in over a month, ending a six-week lull in a war now in its sixth month: American strikes on Larak Island hit launchers Washington says were readying sea mines for the Strait of Hormuz, and Iran answered with missiles at US bases in Jordan. Markets read it immediately as an inflation story rather than a risk-off one, with Brent above \$90 and the 10-year Treasury above 4.75%, its highest since January 2025, because the Fed under Kevin Warsh is now openly debating a hike. Meanwhile the Supreme Court cleared Trump's \$400 million White House ballroom on standing grounds with Roberts in dissent, and Xi convened Putin, Modi and Pezeshkian in Bishkek in a pointed display of how little US economic pressure has isolated anyone.

## International

### The US and Iran resume direct fire

US aircraft struck two Iranian rocket launchers on Larak Island, inside the Strait of Hormuz, late Sunday into Monday, the first acknowledged American attack on Iran since July 29. Capt. Tim Hawkins of Central Command said Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps forces "were observed preparing to launch rockets with sea mines into Strait of Hormuz." The Times notes this would be an unusual weapon: a Fajr-5 variant dispensing a single free-floating mine roughly 333mm across, carrying perhaps dozens of pounds of explosives, which would barely scratch a double-hulled tanker but could hole a warship compartment or kill a fisherman. Iran retaliated with a missile and drone operation it called "Punishment of the Aggressor," aimed at the King Hussein and Al Azraq bases in Jordan and at US facilities in the UAE; Jordan's military said it intercepted eight missiles and reported no damage or casualties. NYT, Aug 31, 5:19 p.m. ET; FT; Al Jazeera; Washington Post.

Context worth holding onto: the war began with the combined US-Israeli assault on Feb 28, so this is month six. Shipping has largely abandoned the strait, and Saudi Arabia has rerouted crude through the Red Sea. Trump claimed on Aug 25 that all mines had been cleared from international waters. Axios reported Monday that he is weighing a CENTCOM plan, backed by Pete Hegseth, for limited strikes to stop Iran reconstituting anti-shipping radar, described by one official as "mow the lawn." He also posted an AI-generated video captioned "Kharg Island being blown to smithereens", there was no attack on Kharg, and an Iranian official called the post laughable.

**Flagged:** IRGC casualty claims from the Larak strike are uncorroborated and unquantified. The Axios reporting on the pending Hormuz decision is single-source and explicitly unresolved as of Monday evening.

### Xi's counter-summit in Bishkek

As G20 finance ministers gathered in Asheville, North Carolina, the leaders of China, Russia, India, Iran and six other nations convened in Kyrgyzstan for the Shanghai Cooperation Organization's 25th-anniversary summit. Paul Sonne and David Pierson frame it as a demonstration of exactly how much leverage two simultaneous wars have handed Xi Jinping, who as the world's largest crude importer and consumer-goods exporter has kept both Moscow and Tehran solvent. Xi brushed off Trump's threatened "economic D-Day" against countries supplying Iran, much as he did earlier threats over Russian oil that never materialized. Julian Gewirtz, formerly the NSC's senior director for China, put it bluntly: "After the past year, Trump has a fundamental credibility problem with Xi. Both his threats and his assurances appear to Beijing, as another world leader put it, to be written in pencil." Xi will nonetheless be careful with public support for Iran, with a Washington summit a month away. NYT, Aug 31, updated 4:11 p.m. ET; Al Jazeera.

### Ukraine: a new drone tempo

Russia has shifted from massed overnight barrages to near-hourly daytime strikes, launching close to 800 jet-powered drones over four days. These travel at up to 600 kph, roughly four times the speed of earlier Shahed models. Overnight into Monday, 121 long-range drones were launched and 96 intercepted. Seven people were killed and 45 injured across six oblasts in 24 hours. Kyiv had logged eight air-raid alerts by 1:30 p.m. Monday, after a record 13 on Aug 28. Separately, the toll from Friday's strike on an ammunition depot at Myla in Bucha district, about 5 km from central Kyiv, rose to 38 dead with four missing, the deadliest attack in the Kyiv region this year; hours of secondary explosions damaged roughly 50 buildings. Kyiv Independent, Aug 31; Al Jazeera, Aug 30.

Iceland rejected reopening EU accession talks 52.8% to 47.2% on 82.5% turnout, a defeat for PM Kristrun Frostadottir; the fishing industry led the No campaign over the common fisheries policy. ## United States

### The Court clears the ballroom

A divided Supreme Court allowed construction of the 90,000-square-foot, \$400 million White House ballroom to continue, ruling 5-4 that the National Trust for Historic Preservation lacked standing. The unsigned majority order held that the Trust's "aesthetic, cultural, and historical interests" did not suffice, and that the Court has "repeatedly held that mere offense, disagreement, or distaste does not qualify as a concrete and particularized injury." The majority pointedly did not address whether Trump exceeded his authority by building without congressional approval. Chief Justice Roberts dissented alongside the three liberals, writing that the construction was "likely unlawful." As a practical matter, the project, rising on the site of the East Wing he demolished last fall, now seems likely to be finished. NYT live coverage, Aug 31, 4:27 and 4:50 p.m. ET; FT; CNN.

### Grand Canyon

A flash flood tore through Bright Angel Canyon and Phantom Ranch beginning around 2:30 p.m. Saturday. Two bodies have been recovered, the first a 46-year-old man found near Crystal Rapids, and about 15 people remain unaccounted for after more than 20 were initially listed missing. The Park Service airlifted 62 people out. Roughly 40% of the Transcanyon Waterline, the park's entire water supply, was damaged or destroyed and is non-operational. More storms were forecast through Monday evening. NYT; NBC News; AP.

**Still developing:** the Grand Canyon missing count has moved repeatedly and should be expected to change again.

### Shutdown clock and the ballot fight

The House returned Monday with a vote tentatively set for Tuesday on the continuing resolution the Senate passed 90-6, which freezes most agency funding at current levels through Dec 11, conspicuously past the Nov 3 midterms, with plus-ups for SNAP, disaster relief, wildfire management, Navy shipbuilding and federal housing. Leadership plans to move it under suspension, needing two-thirds. The House has four session days this week; the Senate does not return until Sept 14, against an Oct 1 deadline.

The higher-stakes fight may be over mail ballots. Judge Indira Talwani blocked key provisions of the USPS rule implementing Trump's mail-voting executive order for at least 14 days, finding compliance "practically impossible" by November and citing an "overwhelming risk" of disenfranchisement. The administration noticed an appeal Aug 28. The first general-election mail ballots go out Sept 4. NBC News; Center Square; PBS NewsHour; NPR.

### Immigration and speech

Judge Noel Wise of the Northern District of California issued a 90-page declaratory judgment finding that State and DHS violated the First and Fifth Amendments in detaining and attempting to deport noncitizen students over pro-Palestinian speech and journalism, in a suit brought by The Stanford Daily and two international student writers. "Free speech, including the freedom to criticize the government and its leaders, is not a sign of our democracy's fragility," she wrote. A Ninth Circuit appeal is expected.

Meanwhile Sen. Richard Blumenthal estimated that ICE detained people in the hundreds across Connecticut last week, with 70-plus in Danbury alone, saying that of roughly 100 cases he had information on, six or seven had criminal histories. Danbury schools reported hundreds of absences. ICE made nearly 50,000 arrests in July, the highest monthly total of Trump's second term. CBS News separately obtained documents showing three deportation flights sent more than 100 people to eight African nations in about 10 days, Burundi, Cameroon, Central African Republic, Equatorial Guinea, Eswatini, Liberia, Rwanda and Sierra Leone, none of them nationals of the receiving countries. The Hill; Connecticut Public; CBS News.

**Flagged:** the Connecticut detention numbers are senator and advocacy estimates, not official ICE figures. The third-country deportation reporting is single-source to CBS.

IRS audit revenue fell 35% year over year, a \$3.5 billion drop, following Trump's staffing cuts, per a watchdog report. The Justice Department issued a grand jury subpoena to Daniel Richman, the Columbia law professor and longtime Comey confidant. And Trump called for NBC's Kristen Welker to be "reported to the FCC for rebuke or punishment" over a promo in which she called his primary endorsements "mixed results", a line that never aired on the program. NYT; The Hill; Variety.

## Israel and the region

### Generals against the settlers

The Times published a long investigation, by Azam Ahmed, Natan Odenheimer and Ronen Bergman, on a growing group of retired Israeli generals and intelligence officials who have begun touring the West Bank and describing what they find in language almost no mainstream Israeli figure has used. Maj. Gen. Eyal Ben-Reuven, who survived 1973 and commanded ground forces in the 2006 Lebanon war, said of settler violence: "This is more concerning to me than Iran, Gaza or Lebanon." Ephraim Sneh, a retired brigadier general and former health minister, called it "ethnic cleansing, to put it simply." Asaf Agmon, a retired Air Force general, went further, comparing the effort to erase Palestinian presence to what some Israelis' forebears endured, and said: "Once a society behaves this way, that society is doomed." The UN counts more than 3,200 Palestinians displaced by settler attacks this year, double last year's rate, and says injuries from settler attacks have gone from one every three days in 2020 to more than three a day in 2026. NYT, Aug 31, 20-minute read.

On the ground it continued over the weekend. Dozens of settlers raided Qusra on Saturday and attacked a Palestinian family and an NBC News crew in nearby Jalud; despite video showing identifiable faces, no arrests had been made as of Aug 30. Netanyahu issued a rare condemnation of "criminal acts of violence by a handful of rioters." Settlers attacked Burqa on Monday. Times of Israel; The National; France 24.

### Gaza and Lebanon

Palestinian health officials said at least five people were killed Monday in Gaza City, two near a park sheltering displaced families and three in a strike on a vehicle in Tal al-Hawa. The IDF said both targeted Hamas operatives, and separately said a strike in Sabra killed Omar Mohammed Roshdy Saraj, head of Hamas's supply and maintenance company in Gaza City. Al Jazeera reports 1,313 Palestinians killed since the ceasefire took effect in October 2025. In Lebanon, Israeli aircraft and artillery struck the Nabatieh district Saturday, including the Ali al-Taher ridge, after a Hezbollah drone attack on Israeli troops; UNIFIL has documented more than 10,000 Israeli ceasefire violations. Al Jazeera; Times of Israel; Haaretz.

**Attribution:** Gaza casualty figures come from the Hamas-run health ministry; militant identifications come from the IDF. Neither is independently verified. Israel maintains the ceasefire holds and that it is responding to violations.

### The October election

With voting set for Oct 27, polling has Gadi Eisenkot's Yashar party at 22-25 seats against Likud's 21-23, and one survey found 43% prefer Eisenkot as prime minister against 34% for Netanyahu. Neither bloc reaches 61 seats in any mainstream poll. Into that arithmetic came Monday's genuinely novel development: Mansour Abbas announced that Yoav Segalovitz, former head of the Israel Police Intelligence and Investigations Division, and until this week a Yesh Atid MK, will run second on Ra'am's list, the first Jewish candidate ever on an Arab party's slate. "To create change, you need partnership," Abbas said. Ra'am polls at five or six seats, which in a deadlocked Knesset is not nothing. FT; Times of Israel; i24; Jerusalem Post.

### The regime shift nobody should miss

The single most important framing for this market: the Fed is now debating hikes, not cuts. Kevin Warsh succeeded Jerome Powell as chair in May, and in his first Jackson Hole keynote on Aug 28 he called inflation figures "more concerning" and said Fed leaders have "work to do" if they are not confident inflation is heading to 2% "clearly and at sufficient speed." July PCE, released Aug 26, came in hotter than forecast: headline held at 3.7% against a 3.6% consensus, core at 3.3% against 3.2% expected, both up 0.2% on the month. Cleveland Fed's Beth Hammack added that "now is the time to act." Pricing for the Sept 15-16 meeting has moved sharply but not unanimously, CME FedWatch is around 66% for a 25bp hike, roughly double its pre-Jackson Hole level, while Kalshi sits closer to 47% and CNBC notes not every analyst reads Warsh as a firm endorsement. That single repricing explains Monday's otherwise puzzling simultaneity: bonds sold off, gold fell despite a geopolitical shock, and crypto slid. Federal Reserve; CNBC; Forbes; CBS News; Axios; FT.

### Rates elsewhere, and a trade date to diary

The hawkish turn is global. The 10-year JGB rose to 2.947%, near 30-year highs, with traders pricing an 87% chance of a 25bp Bank of Japan hike on Sept 17-18; Deputy Governor Ryozo Himino stressed "vigilance to inflation risks." The UK 10-year gilt held at 5.14%, near its highest since May, with UK CPI at 2.9% and expected to climb into year-end. On trade, the US implemented Section 338 tariffs on Canadian goods at rates up to 50% effective Aug 22, and Canada's reciprocal tariffs of 15%, 25% and 50% on more than 700 US-origin products take effect **Sept 8**, the cleanest near-term catalyst on the board. Separately, CBP has now accepted more than \$132bn in potential and certified IEEPA tariff refunds, but Phase 3 of the refund system, covering finally liquidated entries, has been delayed indefinitely, and DOJ argued in an Aug 10 Federal Circuit brief that CBP cannot reliquidate those entries on its own initiative. The tail of the roughly \$165bn refund pool remains contested. Trading Economics; Reuters; Thompson Hine trade roundup, Aug 28.

### Oil, yields and the fiscal bill

Brent rose 2.93% to \$90.69, touching \$91.52, and WTI gained about 4% past \$86 on the Hormuz mine threat; US average gasoline topped \$4 a gallon on a daily basis in August for the first time. The 10-year yield pushed above 4.75%, the highest since January 2025. FT research quantifies what that costs: G7 nations have locked in an additional \$16bn of sovereign financing costs since the war began in February, and face an estimated further \$34bn by the end of Q1 2027 if yields hold. The US is bearing the bulk, \$10.6bn so far and a projected \$21.7bn more. Nearly every G7 bond issue at every maturity now trades above its February level. American debt hit a record \$40tn earlier this month, and Bessent's increased purchases of longer-dated bonds have not arrested the selloff. FT, Aug 30 and Aug 31; Yahoo Finance; Fortune; Bloomberg.

### An awkward G20

Anton Siluanov made his first in-person G20 appearance since the 2022 invasion, on a US invitation that blindsided European capitals. He was quietly uninvited from the Monday family photo after most senior European officials declined to be pictured with him. German finance minister Lars Klingbeil called his presence "quite troubling" and said he "would have wanted greater clarity from the American side that he should not be received here as a normal guest." Treasury confirmed Bessent met Siluanov, saying he "reinforced the president's push for world peace", a reference to the 28-point Ukraine plan that would cap Ukraine's army at 600,000, bar NATO troops, grant de facto recognition of Donbas and Crimea, and readmit Russia to the G7. A Ukrainian official told the Kyiv Independent that Kyiv "does not know anything about this meeting." FT, Aug 31; NYT; Kyiv Independent.

**Flagged:** the Bessent-Siluanov meeting content is sourced to Treasury's own characterization plus single-source Kyiv Independent reporting on the peace-plan agenda.

### Corporate

The FTC and 22 states sued Amazon in the Western District of Washington, alleging it covertly inflated advertising auction prices for more than 1.2 million customers over seven years, with damages the agency puts at \$20 billion or more; Amazon shares fell about 3% and the company said the FTC "cherry-picked" its findings. Nvidia put \$3.5 billion into MediaTek via convertible bonds, giving the Taiwanese firm access to NVLink Fusion so it can build custom accelerators for hyperscalers, ceding some chip ground while keeping the interconnect. ## Worth reading

**"The MAHA Custody Battle," Ruby Cramer, The New Yorker, Aug 31.** The one to read. Cramer follows Michael Lee Horrocks and his ex-wife Leah Stelly, a divorced West Virginia couple whose custody fight turns on whether their three children are vaccinated, including five-year-old Donnie, who has spinal muscular atrophy and later develops what a doctor calls textbook measles. What makes it more than a case study is Cramer's argument that vaccine hesitancy operates less as a discrete belief than as an aesthetic and spiritual sensibility: crunchy-mom wellness, angel numbers, anti-corporate distrust. A Democratic strategist calls it "two truths and a lie," which is exactly the problem, much of it is genuinely appealing. She traces the emotional lineage from the retracted 1998 Lancet paper through Jenny McCarthy's 2007 Oprah appearance and "mommy's instinct" to Kennedy at HHS. Given the measles numbers above, it is the most useful thing you could read this week. [Open →](https://www.newyorker.com/magazine/2026/09/07/the-maha-custody-battle)

**"Anatomy of a Siege: What Happened in Minneapolis," Emily Witt, The New Yorker, Aug 31.** A reconstruction of Operation Metro Surge, the DHS campaign that put two thousand immigration agents into Minnesota beginning Dec 1, and of the January morning that broke it open. Witt builds the scene through residents who had taken to patrolling their own neighborhoods: a union steward and nurse in Powderhorn, a therapist who followed a convoy of tinted SUVs to Thirty-fourth and Portland and found a woman on the ground receiving CPR and a doctor screaming that agents had pointed a gun at him and would not let him take her pulse. A former Assistant US Attorney describes the plan she was told about: "They were, like, 'Occupy Minneapolis like it was Falluja.'" [Open →](https://www.newyorker.com/magazine/2026/09/07/what-happened-in-minneapolis)

**"Who's Afraid of A.I. Music?" Kelefa Sanneh, The New Yorker, Aug 31.** Worth your time for the musician half of your brain. Sanneh starts with rapper Fenix Flexin's viral single "Rubberz," whose vocals sounded nothing like him and which a producer reproduced in an AI app with the prompt "synthwave 80s," then argues the backlash is not really about copyright but about a charge of fraudulence. He historicizes it well: Sousa testifying to Congress against "canned music" in 1906, the musicians' union's 1930 ads against "soulless mechanization," and defensive album credits from Queen's "nobody played synthesizer" to the White Stripes. The implication is that Sousa was wrong about the phonograph killing music and right that it would relocate the authentic artifact from the performance to the recording.
