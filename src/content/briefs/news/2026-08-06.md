## International

### Iran and Oman converge on a Hormuz corridor, on Tehran's terms

Al Jazeera / CNN / NYT / FT · Aug 4–5, still developing

Iran's foreign ministry said Wednesday it has reached the "final stage" of drafting an agreement with Oman on safe commercial transit through the Strait of Hormuz, with both sides having agreed the geographic coordinates. The reported shape: inbound vessels through northern lanes in Iranian waters, outbound ships hugging the Omani coast, no transit fees, mines cleared from the main lane within about 30 days, and a 60-day interim term with possible extension. Qatar says draft language is circulating; Rubio confirmed US involvement while cautioning nothing is final; Trump said a deal could land within 48 hours and a senior Gulf official put Friday odds at roughly 50-50. Tehran publicly denies direct talks with Washington.

The FT's analysis is the one worth sitting with. Since attacking Iran in February, Trump's demands have collapsed from a sprawling list, nuclear program, ballistic missiles, proxies, to a single ask: reopen the strait. Rubio said it plainly on Tuesday: "The denuclearisation of Iran is the ultimate deal. The immediate deal… is the strait." Any settlement would effectively ratify Iranian control over a waterway that carried a fifth of the world's oil, and leaves open the possibility Tehran eventually charges for passage. Aaron David Miller: "What was once free and unfettered is no longer." Meanwhile US interceptor stockpiles are badly drawn down, petrol is above \$4 a gallon and about 35% above prewar, and the midterms are under three months out. ### The AI capex reckoning arrives via, of all things, SpaceX's first earnings

CNBC / FT / Yahoo Finance · Aug 4 close, spreading Aug 5–6

SpaceX reported its maiden public quarter after the Aug 4 bell and beat cleanly, revenue \$7.81B against \$6.93B expected, up 92% year over year; a 9-cent loss against 26 expected; adjusted EBITDA up 191% to \$3.5B. Compounding it: SpaceX's first insider lock-up expires *today*, freeing roughly 911.5 million Class A shares, something on the order of \$101–116 billion notional, with insiders able to sell up to the first 20% of eligible shares. ## Worth reading

### Scott Stossel, "What If Freud Was Right?" · The Atlantic

September issue, posted Aug 4

The one I would actually put in front of you. Stossel reviews Mark Solms's "The Only Cure: Freud and the Neuroscience of Mental Healing," in which the neuroscientist who identified the forebrain pathway generating dreams and disproved the REM-dependence of dreaming argues that psychoanalysis is not merely defensible but the only approach that actually cures emotional disorders. The essay is best on why such a claim can get a hearing in 2026: it walks through the successive collapse of everything that replaced Freud, the catecholamine hypothesis, the 1990s serotonin "chemical imbalance" story, the assumption that fMRI and standardized instruments would deliver an objectivity the couch could not. Stossel concedes Popper's unfalsifiability charge was fair and that Freud the man deserved much of the demolition, which makes the rehabilitation argument more interesting than nostalgia. Read it as an argument about what counts as evidence in psychiatry, and about a field that may have spent fifty years mistaking measurement for mechanism. [Read →](https://www.theatlantic.com/magazine/2026/09/freud-psychoanalysis-theories-brain-science/687957/)

### Evan Osnos, "The Future, Made in China" · The New Yorker

Letter from Beijing, Aug 10 issue, posted Aug 3

Osnos returns to Beijing to report on how completely the psychological balance of the US–China technology race has flipped. He opens with Kai-Fu Lee, who once ran Google China and has since closed his American office, "It's two different universes now", and builds outward: while Washington outsourced AI strategy to Silicon Valley and cut federal research funding, Beijing pursued what Lee calls an "all-hands-on-deck approach to national innovation" aimed at world leadership by 2030. The numbers stack up hard: 70%+ of the world's drones, EVs, lithium-ion batteries and solar cells; more industrial robots deployed than the rest of the world combined; more registered clinical trials than the US; roughly 200 times American shipbuilding capacity; and a July Moonshot model matching frontier US performance at a fraction of the cost. The clinical-trials line alone should interest you. But Osnos's sharper point is about self-perception, Chinese officials now speak with the confidence of people who assume the cycle has turned, while Americans, as one retired PLA colonel puts it, have lost any historical sense that nations rise and fall. [Read →](https://www.newyorker.com/magazine/2026/08/10/the-future-made-in-china)

### Kai Bird & Susan Goldmark, "The Mentor: How Roy Cohn Taught Donald Trump Everything" · The New Yorker

American Chronicles, Aug 10 issue, posted Aug 3

Drawn from a forthcoming biography and including a fresh 2025 interview with Trump himself, this builds an origin story out of a single scene: the 1973 meeting at Le Club between a 25-year-old outer-borough developer and the McCarthy-era fixer who helped send the Rosenbergs to the chair. Bird and Goldmark set Trump's self-mythologizing accounts against the likelier reality, that he approached Cohn because the Justice Department had just sued the family firm for barring Black tenants from 39 buildings. Cohn's answer, "Tell them to go to hell and fight the thing in court," becomes the template for a career. The curriculum, as David Cay Johnston summarizes it: ignore the rules, leave no paper trail, rat out others to save yourself. Biography as political explanation rather than psychologizing, and the Fred Trump material, the 1927 Queens Klan arrest, Woody Guthrie's "Old Man Trump", gives it a longer generational arc than the usual Cohn retelling. ### Alexis Okeowo, "The Man Who Was Kidnapped Twice" · The New Yorker

Our Far-Flung Correspondents, Aug 10 issue, posted Aug 3

Okeowo follows Izuchukwu Anyanwu, a political-theory lecturer outside Abuja, seized from his home in 2023 by seventeen armed men, held in a cave, beaten during ransom calls so his family could hear, told to choose which leg he would lose, and then, after escaping, abducted a second time. The narrative is unsparing but the ambition is structural: at least 4,700 Nigerians were kidnapped in a single year, and the practice is now routine enough that Chimamanda Ngozi Adichie's father and a former finance minister's mother are among the taken. She shows kidnapping as a business, with pricing, profiling of hostages by profession, and negotiated discounts. She also handles carefully how the abduction of Nigerian Christians has become a cause on the American right, and what that framing gets wrong about a crisis closer to state collapse and rural economics than religious war.
