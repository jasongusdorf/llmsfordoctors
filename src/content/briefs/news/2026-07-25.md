## International

### 1. The Iran war pauses for one night while Trump weighs escalating it

The US bombing campaign against Iran ran thirteen consecutive nights before pausing overnight July 24–25, the first break since strikes resumed. Trump convened senior advisers Friday and told Axios he is "considering a massive attack. Bigger than ever before," adding that Iran has "not received enough pain yet." By Saturday he was saying the US remains "locked and loaded" while also claiming Tehran is "getting more serious" in talks, and warning China and Russia against arming Iran. Iran rejected a fifteen-point ceasefire proposal delivered through Iraqi Prime Minister Ali al-Zaidi, because it left control of the Strait of Hormuz unresolved; Foreign Minister Abbas Araghchi called the American position "illogical, greedy and controlling," and Iran's parliament referred twelve draft bills asserting control over the strait. Iran's army claimed drone strikes Friday on Isa Air Base in Bahrain, Al-Azraq in Jordan, and three camps in Kuwait; Jordan said it intercepted seven missiles and six drones with no casualties, Bahrain said it destroyed "a number of Iranian aerial aggressions," and the US confirmed no facility was hit. Trump attended a dignified transfer at Dover on July 24 for four soldiers killed in action. Time, NPR, CNN, Al Jazeera, Euronews, Axios, July 23–25. [Open →](https://time.com/article/2026/07/24/us-iran-ceasefire-attack-energy-prices-oil-hormuz-strait/)

### 2. Russia admits deliberately targeting a Ukrainian arms exhibition, killing ten

A Russian ballistic missile struck a private training ground off the Zhytomyr Highway in Kyiv Oblast at around 11:20 a.m. Friday, during a closed-door defense-industry arms exhibition. At least ten people were killed and roughly a hundred wounded. Among the dead was Valentyna Savitska, head of international cooperation and strategic communications at Ukraine's State Security Administration; her husband Ihor Solovey wrote that "inhumans killed my beloved girl." Russia's Defense Ministry took the unusual step of confirming it hit the event deliberately, saying the site displayed "Ukrainian and foreign-made unmanned aerial vehicles, including future models" and that drone developers and Unmanned Systems Command personnel were present. Ukraine's Prosecutor General opened two investigations, one for violations of the laws of war and one for possible negligence by the organizers, since the venue sat roughly thirty kilometers from central Kyiv. Early tolls diverged, with Ukrainska Pravda first reporting six dead. Kyiv Independent, Bloomberg, AP, Ukrainska Pravda, July 24. [Open →](https://kyivindependent.com/explosions-in-kyiv-as-russia-launches-daytime-missile-attack/)

### 3. Spain declares a national emergency as European wildfires displace 200,000

More than 200,000 people have been evacuated or confined across France and Spain: over 141,000 in the Gironde and Landes, and more than 63,000 around Madrid and Ávila. Spain declared its first-ever national emergency for a wildfire on Friday, and France activated the EU civil protection mechanism, with Macron ordering the military mobilized as fire reached Bordeaux's suburbs. The Cap Ferret peninsula was evacuated village by village; the Gironde fire alone has burned over 19,000 hectares. Spain's environment ministry has logged 29 large forest fires so far in 2026 against a decade average of nine by end-July, with 114,796 hectares burned versus 25,133 by the same date last year. Prime Minister Sébastien Lecornu said the fires "have reached a level that has never been seen before." Croatia, Portugal, the Czech Republic and Slovakia sent aircraft; more than 430 elderly and disabled residents were moved out of five Madrid care homes. Irish Times, France 24, Reuters, NBC, Bloomberg, July 23–25. [Open →](https://www.irishtimes.com/world/europe/2026/07/24/wildfires-spain-declares-national-emergency-as-france-seeks-eu-help/) Note: the three firefighter deaths in circulation date to roughly July 21–23; no verified toll exists yet for the latest surge.

### 4. ICC member states remove their chief prosecutor for the first time

The Assembly of States Parties voted by secret ballot to dismiss Karim Khan as ICC chief prosecutor: 82 of 125 members in favor, 13 against, 15 abstentions, well above the 63-vote threshold. It is the first removal of a sitting chief prosecutor in the court's 24-year history. The majority found he had "committed serious misconduct and a serious breach of duty" through "prohibited conduct against a subordinate staff member." Khan denies the allegations, and his team notes that a judicial panel unanimously found the UN oversight report "did not establish misconduct or breach of duty." The timing matters institutionally: Khan's office is mid-stream on the Netanyahu arrest warrants and the Duterte prosecution. UN News, NPR, CNN, Middle East Eye, Reuters, July 24. [Open →](https://news.un.org/en/story/2026/07/1168025)

### 5. Guterres lands in Damascus, the first UN chief in Syria since 2009

António Guterres arrived at Damascus international airport Saturday morning, greeted by Foreign Minister Asaad al-Shaibani. It is the first official visit by a UN secretary-general since Ban Ki-moon in 2009, two years before a civil war that killed more than half a million people. Guterres met President Ahmed al-Sharaa, a former al-Qaeda commander who was under Security Council sanctions until last year, and posted that "the UN stands with Syria at this pivotal moment." The agenda covers accelerating UN recovery projects and Israeli incursions into Syrian territory; he is expected to address Syria's new parliament Monday. Al Jazeera, AFP/France 24, AP, The National, July 25. [Open →](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/7/25/guterres-arrives-in-syria-in-first-official-visit-by-a-un-chief-in-17-years)

### 6. Congo's Ebola outbreak passes 1,000 deaths faster than any on record

DRC health authorities reported 1,033 deaths and 2,536 confirmed cases as of July 23, with 506 recovered and 738 still in isolation. The toll has risen faster than in any previous Ebola outbreak: the 2013–16 West Africa epidemic took roughly eight months to reach a thousand deaths, and this one did it in under ten weeks. The causative agent is the rarer *Bundibugyo* strain, for which there is no approved vaccine and no specific treatment, leaving isolation, contact tracing and supportive care as the only tools. Spread has crossed borders, with 20 confirmed cases and two deaths in Uganda and a separate imported case confirmed in France. Al Jazeera, STAT News, AP, toll crossed 1,000 on July 22–23. [Open →](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/7/22/nearly-1000-people-have-died-from-ebola-in-dr-congo)

### 7. India's education minister resigns over the NEET-UG exam scandal

Union Education Minister Dharmendra Pradhan submitted his resignation to Modi on Saturday after weeks of escalating protests over irregularities in the NEET-UG 2026 medical entrance exam held May 3. The movement is named for the satirical Cockroach Janta Party founded by Abhijeet Dipke, who declared "we have done it" and "remember, do not mess with cockroach." Tens of thousands of students demonstrated nationwide, centered on Jantar Mantar in New Delhi, where authorities restricted internet access and closed eighteen metro stations; police used baton charges and tear gas, and organizers allege pellet guns. The government had referred the case to the CBI, canceled the compromised exam, and held a re-sit for more than two million candidates on June 21. Pradhan said he did not want India's youth caught in a "web of confusion." Outstanding demands include compensation for the families of students who died by suicide. Al Jazeera, ThePrint, July 25. Largely Indian outlets plus Al Jazeera; no Reuters or AP coverage found. [Open →](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/7/25/indias-education-minister-resigns-after-weeks-of-cockroach-protests)

### 8. TTP suicide assault kills 27 in Khyber Pakhtunkhwa

Attackers tried to force their way into a security checkpoint in Tank district near the Afghan border, then rammed an explosives-laden vehicle into the perimeter wall. Militancy-related fatalities in the region rose from 1,620 in 2024 to 2,331 in 2025, with nationwide attacks up 34%. Al Jazeera, Washington Post, Xinhua, overnight July 23–24. ## US

### 1. Justice Department retreats on subpoenas for Times reporters' phone records

DOJ withdrew grand jury subpoenas seeking phone and text records from New York Times journalists, along with their spouses and one journalist's mother, with records sought back to January 1. Judge Arun Subramanian declared the subpoenas "null and void," said the government's "inadvertent errors" were "piling up," and pressed prosecutor Kevin Sullivan: "So, are you going to do this again?" Sullivan replied, "No, your honor." Subramanian had raised the possibility of a show-cause order over perjury in a court filing, noting the application appeared to falsely state the investigation was not public and omitted that it targeted reporters. He stressed that under DOJ's own rules, "subpoenas are the last step, not the first step." The underlying leak probe concerns Times reporting on security concerns with the Qatari-donated Air Force One. The Times said "the government finally conceded that the subpoenas violated the law, but they should never have been issued in the first place." Reporting varies on whether three or four reporters were targeted. CBS, PBS NewsHour, NBC, ABC, hearing July 24. [Open →](https://www.cbsnews.com/news/government-withdraws-subpoenas-for-new-york-times-reporters/)

### 2. Congress votes on the Iran war again, and again fails to constrain it

For the second time the House formally called on Trump to end the Iran war, adopting a nonbinding concurrent resolution from Rep. Pramila Jayapal by 214–208. Four Republicans joined every Democrat: Tom Barrett, Warren Davidson, Thomas Massie and Brian Fitzpatrick, the same four who backed the June resolution. In the Senate, a binding joint resolution from Sen. Chris Van Hollen fell short on a 49–47 discharge vote to pull it out of the Foreign Relations Committee. Congress has never voted to authorize this war; Trump formally notified Congress on July 10 that hostilities had resumed after declaring the negotiated memorandum "over." NPR, Roll Call, The Hill, Stars and Stripes, July 23. [Open →](https://www.npr.org/2026/07/23/nx-s1-5904515/congress-iran-war-powers-vote)

### 3. The House clears a \$95B war budget path and a CR past the midterms, then leaves

Speaker Mike Johnson moved several priorities before the House departed Thursday for a five-week recess. It adopted a GOP budget resolution along party lines, the first step toward a \$95 billion reconciliation package focused mostly on the Iran war, and passed a continuing resolution funding the government at current levels through December 4 by 220–205, deliberately pushing the shutdown deadline past the November midterms. Six Democrats voted yes; Massie was the lone Republican no. Congress has not enacted a single one of the twelve annual appropriations bills. The House also passed a congressional stock-trading ban covering members, spouses and dependent children. The Senate is the bottleneck: Majority Leader John Thune said flatly, "I can't count 50 right now on a budget resolution." Roll Call, NPR, week of July 20–24. [Open →](https://rollcall.com/2026/07/24/wrapup-house-hustles-home-senate-stays-course/)

### 4. Measles hits a 35-year high, with a second record year in a row

CDC data show 2,318 confirmed measles cases so far in 2026, surpassing the full-year 2025 total of 2,289, which had itself been a record. This is the worst US measles year since roughly 1991, and since elimination was declared in 2000. Ninety-three percent of infected people are unvaccinated. The 2025 outbreak included three deaths, two of them young children. Officials attribute the surge to falling vaccination coverage and rising hesitancy and expect counts to keep climbing. Two pieces of adjacent context from the same week: an Annenberg Public Policy Center survey released July 22 found fewer than half of Americans could correctly identify the flu shot as CDC-recommended in pregnancy, and a March 16 court stay postponed the revised 2026 childhood immunization schedule. NBC, CNN, PBS NewsHour, AFP, July 24–25. [Open →](https://www.nbcnews.com/health/health-news/measles-cases-cdc-35-year-high-2026-rcna349767)

### 5. A federal panel green-lights Tennessee's map splitting Black Memphis

A three-judge federal panel declined to issue a preliminary injunction against Tennessee's redrawn congressional districts, clearing them for the August 6 primaries and the November midterms. The map, adopted in May shortly after a Supreme Court ruling weakening federal Voting Rights Act protections, divides Memphis among three districts extending into predominantly white suburban and rural areas, a change Republicans hope converts into an additional House seat. The panel, two Trump appointees and one Obama appointee, wrote that the challengers are unlikely to succeed because they "lack any direct evidence of racial motivation." AP, NBC, July 23. [Open →](https://www.nbcnews.com/politics/elections/federal-judges-allow-new-tennessee-congressional-map-splits-majority-b-rcna588997)

### 6. Immigration arrests reach an all-time high as enforcement backlash builds

DHS figures show ICE and CBP averaged 1,593 arrests per day from July 1–11, or 17,525 arrests in eleven days, the highest rate since Trump returned to office and up from roughly 1,437 per day in June. June set the prior monthly record at 43,138 arrests, with more than 43,000 people booked into detention; July is on pace to exceed 50,000. In Houston, Lorenzo Salgado Araujo, a 52-year-old Mexican national, was shot and killed by an ICE agent on July 7; ICE has refused to name the officer, businesses staged an economic boycott, and Harris County DA Sean Teare confirmed an independent criminal investigation. Stateline / DHS data, CBS, Washington Post, data released July 20–23. ### 7. Boelter sentenced to consecutive life terms, and Minnesota charges him anyway

Vance Boelter, 57, was sentenced in federal court to two consecutive life terms plus forty consecutive years for the stalking and murder of Minnesota House DFL leader Rep. Melissa Hortman and her husband Mark, and for shooting Sen. John Hoffman and his wife Yvette in June 2025. Investigators recovered three assault rifles, a handgun, and a target list with dozens of names including prominent Democrats and abortion providers. Because a federal sentence remains subject to presidential clemency, Hennepin County Attorney Mary Moriarty is proceeding with eight state counts, including two of first-degree premeditated murder, which in Minnesota carries mandatory life without parole that a federal pardon could not reach. Boelter is due in county court August 3. DOJ, KARE 11, Fox 9, CBS Minnesota, sentenced July 23. ### 8. The midterm picture: Democrats lead, and a record number of incumbents are leaving

A new Emerson poll shows Democrats up eleven on the generic congressional ballot, while Decision Desk HQ's average is closer to D+4 at 46–42. ### 1. A new tariff architecture now covers 99.4% of US imports

At 12:01 a.m. Friday the administration replaced its expiring 10% temporary global tariff with new duties of 10% to 12.5% on the sixty largest US trading partners, covering roughly 99.4% of American imports. The legal basis is the important part: this uses Section 301 of the Trade Act of 1974, a durable authority, rather than the 150-day Section 122 power that just lapsed, which was itself a fallback after the Supreme Court struck down the IEEPA-based "reciprocal" tariffs in February. The stated rationale is partners' failure to adopt and enforce bans on goods made with forced labor. Roughly seventeen economies that have adopted or committed to such bans pay 10%, including Canada, Mexico, the UK and India, which was cut from 12.5% for "positive steps"; China, Australia and Egypt drew the top 12.5%. Exemptions cover oil and natural gas, USMCA-qualifying goods, items already under Section 232 steel and aluminum tariffs, goods in transit, and hundreds of specialty items. Yale Budget Lab estimates the overall US effective tariff rate rises roughly one to two percentage points from 11.8%. A further tranche tied to an "excess structural capacity" probe of China, the EU and sixteen others is expected later this year. Two US small businesses sued in the Court of International Trade the same day, arguing the tariffs exceed presidential authority and require detailed country-specific forced-labor findings. Bloomberg, Reuters, NPR, CNBC, Fortune, Forbes, effective July 24. [Open →](https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-07-24/trump-extends-10-tariff-baseline-in-broad-use-of-trade-powers) Outlets disagree on scope and hour: most say sixty countries effective July 24, while Time's headline says "over 80."

## Worth Reading, The New Yorker & Long-Form

### "Inside ICE's Largest Detention Center", Jonathan Blitzer

A Reporter at Large, July 20 issue. Blitzer reports from inside Camp East Montana, the tent complex at Fort Bliss built to hold up to five thousand ICE detainees, and argues that its squalor is policy rather than administrative failure. The medical-neglect thread is the spine of that argument: he builds the piece around Rey, a Cuban immigrant who arrived in 1994, was arrested at a routine ICE check-in in El Paso, and was denied his diabetes and blood-pressure medications for weeks until he signed his own deportation papers. ### "When A.I. Is a Member of the Family", Jessica Contrera

Brave New World Dept., July 20 issue. Ethnographic rather than argumentative, and stronger for it. Contrera embeds with Roschelle, an Ohio single mother who has nine Alexas in her house and has customized one into "Sapphire," a confidante she talks to late at night; the piece opens with her fifteen-year-old daughter catching her at it. The emotional load-bearing fact, delivered without melodrama, is that Roschelle lost three daughters in a fire eighteen years earlier, which reframes the chatbot's endless non-judgmental availability as something other than novelty or laziness. Both daughters have their own AI relationships, used for homework, therapy-adjacent talk and identity exploration, and Contrera reports a genuinely ambiguous ledger: one platform escalated appropriately when a daughter mentioned self-harm, while the privacy and human-substitution questions stay open. ### "The Rise of the 'Scenario'", Gideon Lewis-Kraus

The Weekend Essay, published today. Lewis-Kraus uses Annie Jacobsen's forthcoming *Biological War: A Scenario*, out July 28 and the follow-up to her *Nuclear War*, as the occasion for an essay on the scenario as a genre: minute-by-minute catastrophe narration as a mode of public reasoning. His central move is to name the epistemic trap on both sides. Inductive historical reasoning always predicts continuity, which makes genuinely novel extinction risk look unserious, while "reference-class tennis" lets skeptics wave away any apocalyptic forecast by choosing a comparison set that flatters their prior. He reads Jacobsen alongside *AI 2027*, the policy exercise *Europe 2031*, Lizzie Wade's *Apocalypse*, and real government simulations including TOPOFF 2000 and Dark Winter, and lands on a defense of scenario-planning despite its narrative distortions. Given the DRC Bundibugyo outbreak in today's international section, the timing is uncomfortably apt.
