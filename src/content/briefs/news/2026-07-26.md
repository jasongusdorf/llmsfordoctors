## International

### The bombing stops, at least for now

For the first time since the campaign resumed in early July, US Central Command announced no new strikes on Iran on Friday night, then none again Saturday. A Defense Department source told CNN operations are "on a hold." Iranian army spokesman Amir Akraminia confirmed the mirror-image de-escalation on Sunday: "Americans stopped their attacks over the past two nights. Since our strategy has been entirely retaliatory, we also stopped our retaliatory operations." What changed appears to be an argument inside the White House. At a Friday meeting, Vice President JD Vance and Joint Chiefs Chairman Gen. Dan Caine both raised concerns about widening the war, with Caine flagging depleted US munitions stockpiles; the *Times* reported Trump shelved a broader campaign partly over dwindling air-defense interceptors in theater. Trump said Iran seemed to be getting "more serious" while insisting the military remains "locked and loaded." Negotiators are reportedly circling a compromise in which Iran would supervise transit through the Strait of Hormuz under looser restrictions. This morning Iran said it had made progress in talks with Oman on exactly that, after rounds between deputy foreign ministers in Tehran on Friday and Saturday covering "common principles and operational mechanisms" for safe passage, while simultaneously accusing the US of trying to force open a new route bypassing Iranian authority, arguing the June memorandum gives Tehran the right to set transit routes. **This is a pause, not a ceasefire**; the Vance/Caine reporting rests on anonymous sourcing via CNN and the *Times*, and the Oman channel is the swing factor for Monday's open. ### France's largest peacetime evacuation

Roughly 267,000 people have now fled wildfires across southwestern France and central Spain. In France about 197,000 were moved from around Bordeaux, 167,000 in the Gironde and 31,000 in the Landes, in what Interior Minister Laurent Nuñez called probably the largest peacetime evacuation in French history. Some 44,000 left the Cap Ferret peninsula alone as its single access road came under threat. Nearly 98,000 hectares have burned in France this year, a record; Prime Minister Sébastien Lecornu said the fires "have reached a level that has never been seen before," and Macron has mobilized the military. In Spain, about 70,000 are affected across the greater Madrid region, with roughly 10,000 hectares burned near the capital and 13,000–15,000 in Ávila. Madrid's emergency chief Carlos Novillo said the fire near the capital was "at its peak and is currently beyond the capacity of firefighters to contain." Spain has 2,737 personnel and 21 aircraft deployed, with crews arriving from Greece, Italy and Portugal, and has recorded 14 deaths this month with ten still missing. Figures are climbing hourly and differ between outlets: CNN's Sunday live coverage puts total displacement above 300,000, with Spain under a national emergency. ### Van attack at Berlin Pride

A white van was driven at speed into crowds in Tiergarten park near the Brandenburg Gate around 10 p.m. Saturday during Berlin's Christopher Street Day celebrations, killing one woman and injuring at least 16 before hitting a tree. Berlin's fire service reported three people with life-threatening injuries, eight seriously hurt and five slightly. Police also said several people appeared to have been stabbed. The driver fled on foot. On Sunday police and prosecutors issued a public wanted notice for a 21-year-old identified as Abdul B., described as known to police and "believed to be associated with Islamist circles," warning he may be armed and dangerous; an overnight search of an apartment in Schöneberg found no one. The event, which had drawn hundreds of thousands, was called off. **Motive has not been officially established**, the Islamist characterization is a police statement, not a charge, and the manhunt is ongoing. ### India's 'Cockroach' movement wins a scalp

Education Minister Dharmendra Pradhan resigned Saturday after 36 days of youth-led protests, announcing it on X just as police fired tear gas at demonstrators in New Delhi. The movement began in late June after national exam papers leaked, forcing a resit affecting two million students; protesters adopted the label "cockroaches" after Chief Justice Surya Kant used the word for unemployed youth in May. The satirical Cockroach Janta Party called off demonstrations saying the government had "accepted" all its demands, including reform of the cheating-prone exam system. It is the worst political crisis of Modi's third term, and it peaked this week when thousands marched on parliament. The open question is whether a resignation addresses what the protests were actually about, which is youth unemployment. ## United States

### A court blocks the mail-in voting order, 101 days out

The First Circuit ruled 2–1 on Saturday against the Justice Department's request to stay a June district-court injunction blocking key provisions of Trump's March executive order on mail voting. Judges Gustavo Gelpí and Julie Rikelman formed the majority; Judge Joshua Dunlap dissented in part, saying he would have allowed the DHS voter-list provision. The blocked provisions directed DHS to prepare voter-eligibility lists for states and gave the Postal Service authority to redesign election mail and require state coordination on mail-ballot recipient lists. The suit was led by California with roughly two dozen states and DC; Judge Indira Talwani had held Trump lacked the authority. Twelve Republican attorneys general intervened in support. DOJ has signaled it may seek Supreme Court review, but for now implementation is barred heading into November. [CNBC](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/07/25/us-appeals-court-rules-trump-cannot-implement-mail-in-voting-order.html)

### Tariffs return through a different door

New Section 301 tariffs of 10% and 12.5% took effect July 24 on goods from 60 trading partners, 59 countries plus the EU, together accounting for more than 99% of US imports, on the grounds that each failed to impose or effectively enforce a ban on goods made with forced labor. The mechanism matters: after the Supreme Court ruled on February 20 in *Learning Resources, Inc. v. Trump* that IEEPA does not authorize tariffs, Section 301 became the administration's primary remedy, and the forced-labor investigations launched in March were the route back. The split is 10% for partners that have adopted or committed to import prohibitions, including the EU, Indonesia and Mexico, and 12.5% for those that have not, including China, Australia and Egypt. Exemptions cover Section 232 steel and aluminum goods, USMCA-covered items, oil and natural gas, and goods in transit. The Yale Budget Lab puts the resulting effective US tariff rate at 11.8%, expected to rise another one to two points, and further "excess structural capacity" tariffs are expected later this year. Allies were not gentle, Australian Trade Minister Don Farrell called the 12.5% rate "unjustified" and "an extremely disappointing decision," and the EU rejected the rationale outright, but most signaled they would negotiate rather than retaliate. ### Congress inches toward a war powers fight

On Thursday the House passed, 214–208, a resolution requiring congressional approval for continued military action against Iran, the second such vote, with four Republicans crossing over: Tom Barrett of Michigan, Brian Fitzpatrick of Pennsylvania, Warren Davidson of Ohio and Thomas Massie of Kentucky. Hours later the Senate killed its companion measure 47–49, with Susan Collins the lone Republican in favor and John Fetterman the lone Democrat opposed. Both votes are symbolic given the veto math, but the margins are the story: this is the narrowest the president's Hill support has been since the war began in February. It is also the backdrop to CNN's framing of the conflict as "America's first one-party war." ### The fire season hit its ceiling in July

The National Interagency Fire Center has held national preparedness at Level 5, the top of the scale, since July 18, unusually early and the first time this season. Saturday's situation report indicates roughly 88 large fires with about 21 new large incidents in 24 hours and some 23,065 personnel committed, including 511 crews, 1,297 engines and 141 helicopters. Year to date about 41,779 fires have burned more than four million acres, against a ten-year average nearer three million. The largest include the Crosswhite Fire in Wheeler County, Oregon (~143,000 acres, 19% contained), Kaiser Canyon in Okanogan County, Washington (~107,000 acres, 13%), the Babylon Fire near Monticello, Utah (~96,600 acres, 0%) and Colorado's Aspen Acres Fire, now the seventh-largest in state history, with evacuations in Pueblo and Custer counties. In Minnesota the entire Boundary Waters Canoe Area Wilderness remains closed with roughly 8,000 people evacuated; the Forest Service may reopen 37 entry points around July 30 if conditions hold. ### The press, subpoenaed and then roasted

Two related threads. On Thursday Trump publicly defended DOJ subpoenas of *New York Times* reporters in a leak investigation over reporting on security concerns with the Qatari-donated Boeing 747-8: "We're after leakers, and the way you find them is through the journalists." At least four *Times* journalists were subpoenaed, some served at their homes, and Google was subpoenaed as well; NPR reported Thursday that DOJ withdrew the grand-jury testimony and phone-records subpoenas under pressure from a judge. *Times* counsel David McCraw said agents appearing at reporters' homes "should shock the conscience." ## Worth Reading, The New Yorker

### 'The Rise of the "Scenario"', Gideon Lewis-Kraus

Published Friday. Lewis-Kraus uses Annie Jacobsen's forthcoming *Biological War: A Scenario* as a way into a larger question: what is this booming genre of hyper-detailed catastrophe forecasting actually doing to us? Jacobsen's book is a chimera of investigative reporting and reality-adjacent fiction, built on interviews with presidential advisers, DoD officials, physicians, microbiologists, epidemiologists and CIA WMD officers, and it opens on the Soviet Biopreparat program, the Biological Weapons Convention's "defensive research" loophole, and the 2019 explosion at Russia's Vector BSL-4 lab. His sharpest move is to turn the genre's own credibility against it: an account of doomsday that feels plausible can also feel *domesticated*, translating the horror of genuine uncertainty into "the language of manageable risk, risk that somebody, somewhere, has the responsibility and foresight to manage." He notes that the real tabletop exercises, TOPOFF 2000 and Dark Winter, were quietly abandoned once their implications became too dreadful to sit with. The critique transfers cleanly to AI risk forecasting and, closer to home, to clinical prediction models that convert irreducible uncertainty into a decimal. [The New Yorker, July 25](https://www.newyorker.com/culture/the-weekend-essay/the-rise-of-the-scenario-a-trendy-way-of-forecasting-our-dystopian-future)

### 'One Man's Quest to Fix Every Deadly Guardrail', Charles Bethea

In 2016 Steve Eimers's seventeen-year-old daughter Hannah drifted off I-75 in Tennessee, sober and under the speed limit, and hit a guardrail terminal end, the energy-absorbing box designed to give way and curl the rail ahead of the car. It did not give way. It speared the Volvo and cut her in half. Tennessee DOT then mailed the family a \$3,000 bill, addressed to Hannah, for the guardrail that killed her. Eimers, a window-washer and father of ten, has since driven some 40,000 miles through 25 states inspecting guardrails, "I can identify types of guardrails from satellite view; it's urban bushcraft", assembling a one-man surveillance apparatus around a defective safety device installed nationwide. It is a story about a preventable-death device that failed silently at scale, and about what it costs one person to make a regulatory system notice. Anyone who has tried to get a hospital to act on a near-miss will recognize the shape of it. [The New Yorker, July 27 issue](https://www.newyorker.com/magazine/2026/07/27/one-mans-quest-to-fix-every-deadly-guardrail)
