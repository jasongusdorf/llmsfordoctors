## International

### Turkey, Saudi Arabia and Pakistan sign a mutual-defense pact in Mecca

Erdogan, Crown Prince Mohammed bin Salman and Prime Minister Shehbaz Sharif signed a trilateral Strategic Mutual Defence Agreement in Mecca on Friday morning, capping nearly a year of quiet negotiation. The text, per Pakistan's foreign ministry, provides that an armed attack on any one of the three is an attack on all three, which is Article 5 language and the first time Pakistan's nuclear-armed military has been formally bound to Gulf security alongside Turkey. Turkish officials called it purely defensive, aimed at no specific actor, open to other regional states, and not superseding existing bilateral arrangements. The backdrop is the US–Iran war that began February 28 and the Houthi strikes now reaching into Saudi territory; a senior Saudi official told Reuters on Wednesday that Riyadh expects imminent coordinated Iraqi-militia and Houthi attacks supervised by the IRGC. Implementation details, force commitments and whether the pact covers non-state attacks were not published. The nuclear-umbrella question is being widely speculated about and is not stated in the text as reported. Al Jazeera, Reuters, France 24, Aug 7.

### Iran says the Hormuz arrangement will not fully reopen the strait

Iranian officials said Thursday that the near-final agreement with Oman on Strait of Hormuz traffic will not restore free passage, insisting the waterway "will never return to its pre-war status." Three weeks of talks have produced broad agreement on designated inbound and outbound lanes, technical arrangements protecting both states' sovereignty, and a proposed joint coordination center to manage traffic and collect vessel data; foreign ministry spokesman Esmail Baghaei says the coordinates are settled, with ships entering via an Iranian-controlled route and exiting via an Omani one, subject to service fees. Tehran maintains it will not reopen the strait until the US naval blockade of Iranian ports is lifted, and a parliamentary committee is reviewing a bill barring US and Israeli vessels entirely. Trump said this week "I'm involved in the negotiations, it could be soon," while acknowledging the US Navy currently controls the waterway. The UK and France have resisted US pressure to join a Hormuz naval mission on de-escalation grounds. Genuinely contested and fast-moving; Iranian messaging has been inconsistent and no deal is done. Washington Post, NPR, Al Jazeera, Aug 6.

### School shooting outside Bangkok kills seven

A 14-year-old student opened fire at Debsirin School in Bang Kruai district, Nonthaburi province, on Friday morning local time, killing three students and three teachers and wounding roughly fifteen before taking his own life. Police say he first shot dead his grandmother and grandfather at their home in Bang Bua Thong before traveling to the school; investigators recovered evidence of at least 26 rounds fired and 34 rounds unused. Counting the grandparents, Thai outlets report eight dead including the shooter, though wire copy ranged from five to eight through the morning as figures were revised. It is Thailand's deadliest mass killing since the October 2022 Nong Bua Lamphu daycare massacre, which killed 38. Developing; treat the exact toll as provisional. Washington Post, CNN, Bangkok Post, Aug 7.

### Europe's drought becomes an energy crisis

Record-low water on the Danube forced Hungary to shut all four reactors at Paks, roughly 2,000 MW and nearly half of national electricity output, for the first time in the plant's history. Prime Minister Peter Magyar said it may stay down for weeks and asked households and businesses to shift consumption away from the 5–10pm peak; emergency supply talks opened with Austria, Slovakia and Germany. In Poland, Enea cut output at the Kozienice and Polaniec coal plants after Vistula flow near Kozienice fell to 135 cubic meters per second against a cooling requirement of 80–100, removing about 1.3 GW. July hydropower ran far below normal across the continent: Austria at 51 percent of normal, Switzerland 48, Germany 63, France 68. Insurers put this summer's European wildfire damage at 15.6 to 19.1 billion euros. The through-line is that thermal generation, not just renewables, turns out to be weather-dependent. Euronews, CNN, Bloomberg, Aug 1–7.

### Ukraine hits two major Russian refineries; UK adds 19 sanctions listings

Ukraine's General Staff and Zelenskyy confirmed overnight drone strikes Thursday on the Slavneft-Yanos refinery at Yaroslavl, about 700 km from the border and one of Russia's five largest at 15 million tonnes a year, and on Bashneft's Novoil plant in Bashkortostan roughly 1,300 km from the front. Direct hits and three separate fires were recorded at Yaroslavl. In the same 24 hours Russian attacks on Kharkiv, Sumy and Odesa killed six and injured 28, including strikes on a train station and a vessel. Britain separately added 19 entries to its Russia list: six banks including Ozon Bank and the Russian Export-Import Bank, six shadow-fleet vessels, four Russian suppliers, businessman Aleksander Zhdanov, and India-based Frion Ship Management over an LNG transfer. Both sides are at record tempo, Kyiv on drone counts and Moscow on missile salvos. Euronews, Bloomberg, Ukrinform, Aug 6.

### WHO says the DR Congo Ebola outbreak is outpacing the response

Tedros Adhanom Ghebreyesus said this week that new cases are doubling in some hotspots nearly three months into the Bundibugyo-virus outbreak. As of August 5 the DRC reported 3,973 confirmed cases and 1,801 deaths, up 99 cases and 52 deaths on the prior report, with new cases from Ituri, North Kivu and Haut-Uele. It is already the second-largest Ebola epidemic on record and the fastest-growing ever measured. The virus has reached displaced-persons camps in the conflict-affected east, where density and population movement make contact tracing acutely difficult, and cases have appeared in neighboring Uganda. A reported WHO internal estimate putting true infections above 10,000, three to four times the confirmed count, is unpublished and single-sourced. UN News, AP, Aug 5–7.

## United States

### Two executive orders narrowing birthright citizenship

Trump signed two immigration orders at the White House Thursday afternoon. The first directs agencies to treat certain categories of US-born children as ineligible for birthright citizenship; Stephen Miller cited "alien enemies of the United States," members of designated foreign terrorist organizations, and people acting on behalf of foreign governments including some diplomatic staff. The second delegates authority to the Secretaries of State and Homeland Security to halt commercial birth tourism, tightening visa scrutiny of pregnant applicants. This is the second attempt after the Supreme Court's June 2026 ruling rejecting the effort to deny citizenship to US-born children of people here illegally or temporarily; the White House frames the new orders as codifying "historical exceptions recognized by the Supreme Court" rather than reinterpreting the Fourteenth Amendment. Immigration lawyers signaled litigation immediately, though no suit had been filed as of Thursday night. NPR, CNN, ABC, CBS, Aug 6.

### Senate committee votes to hold Fauci in contempt

The Senate Homeland Security and Governmental Affairs Committee, chaired by Rand Paul, voted 8–7 on party lines to hold Anthony Fauci in contempt of Congress. The vote followed a hearing last week in which Fauci declined to answer more than 100 questions, invoking the Fifth Amendment, as Republicans probed the federal COVID response and origins question. Paul says he will send the referral directly to the Justice Department and the attorney general's office, bypassing a full Senate floor vote, a procedurally contested move. Fauci holds a preemptive pardon issued by Biden in January 2025 covering conduct from 2014 through January 2025, which his lawyers argue forecloses prosecution; the pardon does not by itself bar a contempt referral. Whether DOJ acts is unresolved and complicated by the still-unfilled attorney general post. NPR, Washington Post, PBS, Aug 6.

### Tennessee primaries: a rare Trump endorsement loss

Rep. Andy Ogles lost the Republican primary in Tennessee's 5th District to Charlie Hatcher, a Williamson County farmer and former state agriculture commissioner, 53.2 to 46.8 percent. Trump had endorsed Ogles in all three of his races, making this a notable setback for the president's endorsement record heading into the midterms; Ogles had faced an FBI campaign-finance inquiry. In the governor's race, Marsha Blackburn took the GOP nomination with 43.6 percent against John Rose's 32.9 and Monty Fritts's 23.5, an eleven-point margin wider than polling suggested. Memphis City Councilmember Jerri Green won the Democratic nomination with about 69 percent, guaranteeing Tennessee will elect a woman governor in November. This was the first primary under the state's new congressional map, redrawn in a May special session after the Supreme Court's *Louisiana v. Callais* decision. CNN, Roll Call, Washington Post, Aug 6–7.

### Thune refuses to cancel recess; Blanche confirmation on a knife edge

Trump publicly demanded Senate Republicans cancel the August recess, scheduled to begin today, until they pass the SAVE America Act, which would require documentary proof of citizenship to register and photo ID to vote. Thune rejected it outright: "We could stay here until Christmas and the Democrats aren't voting for this and Republicans are not getting rid of the legislative filibuster." Republicans hold 53–47, short of 60, and lack votes for a rules change; voting-rights groups estimate roughly 20 million eligible Americans lack ready access to the required documents. Separately, acting Attorney General Todd Blanche cleared Judiciary 12–10 on August 4 after rescinding a \$1.8 billion anti-weaponization fund to satisfy Cornyn and Tillis. Susan Collins will vote no, leaving Blanche able to lose only one more Republican; Murkowski remained undecided as of Thursday. Floor-vote timing was still in flux Friday morning. Washington Post, PBS/AP, Axios, The Hill.

### HHS proposes stripping most Head Start regulations

HHS announced a proposed rule Thursday eliminating a large share of the federal performance standards governing Head Start, which serves roughly 700,000 low-income children including those who are homeless, in foster care, or disabled. Officials framed it as shifting authority to local grantees and cutting paperwork. The proposal would drop the cap on administrative expenses from 15 to 5 percent of a grantee's budget and add nutrition provisions championed by Secretary Robert F. Kennedy Jr. A 60-day comment period runs before the rule can take effect. Early-childhood researchers warn the deregulation would erode Head Start's quality benchmarks, specifically teacher credentialing, class-size ratios and health screenings. It follows the administration's unsuccessful 2025 proposal to eliminate the program's funding entirely. Single AP wire origin, widely syndicated. AP via PBS/ABC, Aug 6.

### Water-system cyberattacks spread to twelve states

The FBI and EPA warned July 30 that hackers had compromised water and wastewater systems in at least seven states since July 27; by August 4 the count had grown to at least twelve. Attackers targeted internet-facing Rockwell Automation industrial control devices, changing IP addresses and passwords and locking operators out of monitoring and control; the FBI says some incidents caused drops in water pressure and localized flooding. Minnesota alone reported attacks against more than 30 systems. US intelligence agencies consider Iran the leading suspect per the Washington Post, but there has been no public attribution. Minnesota's IT agency says there is no indication any municipal supply was contaminated. Attribution is intelligence-sourced and unconfirmed; state counts have moved daily. NBC News, Axios, Washington Post, Aug 1–6.

### NTSB investigating a Marine One near-miss

The NTSB and FAA opened investigations into a momentary loss of separation on August 4 between Marine One, carrying Trump, and a departing regional airliner near Reagan National. Marine One lifted from the Ellipse at about 2:33 pm; a minute later Envoy Air Flight 3742, an Embraer E170 bound for Pensacola, was cleared for takeoff from Runway 19 and passed within roughly 1.5 miles of the Ellipse per Flightradar24. Both aircraft landed safely with no evasive maneuver reported. Investigators are focused on why DCA departures were not held while the presidential helicopter operated in the adjacent corridor, given the stricter ground-hold protocols imposed after the January 2025 Black Hawk collision that killed 67. The 1.5-mile figure is from public flight-tracking data, not an official finding. CNN, PBS, Fox 5 DC.

## Israel

### Cabinet hawks push to reject the Gaza roadmap while the IDF quietly pulls back

Israel's security cabinet met Thursday evening to weigh the Board of Peace's 15-point roadmap for phase two of the Gaza ceasefire, which Hamas accepted July 30. Ministers including Smotrich, Strock and Dichter reportedly pressed Netanyahu to state publicly that Israel rejects phase two and to resume offensive operations, arguing the published terms differ from what the cabinet was shown. Netanyahu says Israel has not accepted the roadmap, has drawn up amendments, and that the IDF will not withdraw before Hamas is "completely disarmed." Cutting the other way, a foreign official involved in implementation told the Times of Israel that "after a challenging start, we've seen some steps in the right direction," citing restraint and a pullback in operations, with rules of engagement tightened to require chief-of-staff approval for strikes. The core dispute is territorial: the roadmap requires withdrawal to the October 2025 Yellow Line, while Israeli control has expanded from about 53 to over 60 percent of Gaza. Cabinet deliberation details rest on unnamed officials. Times of Israel, Foreign Policy, Aug 6–7.

### Eight Muslim-majority states issue a joint condemnation

Foreign ministers of the UAE, Qatar, Jordan, Indonesia, Pakistan, Turkey, Saudi Arabia and Egypt issued a joint statement Thursday expressing their "strongest condemnation" of continuing Israeli operations in Gaza, singling out strikes affecting healthcare facilities and medical personnel as a grave breach of international humanitarian law and warning of "reigniting the cycle of escalation." What gives the statement operational weight is the signatories: the UAE, Qatar, Indonesia and Turkey are all prospective contributors to the International Stabilization Force. Gaza's Hamas-run Government Media Office claims 4,091 Israeli ceasefire violations over 300 days, killing 1,254 Palestinians; those figures are from the Gaza authority and are not independently verified. UNICEF separately says at least 300 children have been killed in Gaza in the 300 days since the ceasefire took effect. Al Jazeera, Times of Israel, Aug 6.

### Israel strikes southern Lebanon as Rome talks stall

Two Israeli reservists were killed and four seriously wounded by an explosive device at Majdal Zoun on Tuesday; the IDF resumed strikes Wednesday, calling it a blatant Hezbollah ceasefire violation. Lebanon's National News Agency reported one killed and 12 wounded in Tebnine, with an evacuation warning issued for Mansouri beforehand. Israel has not publicly produced evidence for the specific violation it alleges. The strikes landed mid-negotiation: the seventh round of US-brokered talks ran August 4–6 at the US Embassy in Rome, focused on pilot zones in Zawtar al-Gharbiyeh, Srifa and Froun where the Lebanese Armed Forces would verify Hezbollah disarmament ahead of phased Israeli withdrawal. State said progress was made on the pilot-zone process, but Israeli officials signaled no further withdrawals this round, with disputes over verification, demolitions, and control of Ali Taher Hill. Naim Qassem called the direct talks a source of "shame, humiliation, and successive compromises." Al Jazeera, Times of Israel, UPI, Aug 5–6.

### Domestic politics: Likud primary in limbo, the draft law splits the army from the government

Knesset elections must be held by October 27 and the campaign is fully underway. Likud's primary, pushed from August 4 to August 17, remains unresolved amid a legal fight over Netanyahu's demand for reserved slots on the slate, and could still be canceled outright. The Haredi conscription law, passed 58–54 to suspend arrests of ultra-Orthodox draft evaders, is the central domestic fault line: Chief of Staff Lt. Gen. Eyal Zamir opposed it "completely and unequivocally," warning it would suppress recruitment across all sectors, and was reported Wednesday to have called the law warped and accused the government of ignoring the army's professional position. Aryeh Deri accused Zamir of trying to help the left-wing bloc. Smaller moves: MK Yoav Segalovitz left Yesh Atid, and Yair Golan declined to reserve a Democrats slot for hostage-families activist Einav Zangauker. Ynet, Jerusalem Post, Times of Israel, Aug 5–7.

### Also on the Israel file

Palestinian minors in Israeli administrative detention rose from 103 to 191 over the past year, with an eightfold increase in those held longer than a year, per data reported Friday; settler attacks in the West Bank are up about 45 percent year-on-year in the first half of 2026, with more than 1,020 incidents recorded by the UN and Save the Children. Uganda's parliament approved deploying troops to the Gaza stabilization force Thursday, with Morocco expected to deploy first. And Israeli security officials warned Wednesday of rising attempts to fly explosive drones into Israel, with "no current effective operational solution."

## Economic & Markets

### The jobs report, and a Fed debate that has inverted

The July employment report publishes at 8:30 this morning, the same minute this lands in your inbox, so treat it as the day's pending catalyst rather than a result. Consensus is roughly +80,000 nonfarm payrolls after June's weak +57,000, with unemployment expected to hold at 4.2 percent and wage growth stagnant. Worth noting beneath the headline: the household survey's employment level has fallen roughly 833,000 over the course of 2026 even as the unemployment rate stays flat. The more striking development is that the Fed conversation has flipped. Core PCE has climbed from 3.0 percent in December 2025 to 3.3 percent in June, a fifth straight year above target, and with the oil shock from Hormuz layered on, CME FedWatch odds of a rate *hike* at the September 15–16 meeting sat near 57 percent Thursday. The July 29 FOMC held at 3.50–3.75 percent on a 9–3 vote, its most divided since 2016. July CPI on August 12 and PPI on August 13 are the next inputs. The 57 percent figure is a Thursday snapshot and moves intraday. BLS, Reuters, CNBC, CME FedWatch.

That reversed Wednesday through Friday as Iran floated banning hostile vessels and fining violators and Houthi attacks on Saudi Arabia added risk premium: Brent climbed nearly 4 percent Thursday to above \$83 and held there into Friday. Thursday's closes: Dow 53,885.10, down 464.02 or 0.85 percent, snapping its run from a record 54,349.12 set earlier in the week; S&P 500 7,709.96, off 0.18 percent; Nasdaq 26,348.35, off 0.06 percent; Russell 2000 3,001.55. Gold around \$4,290 an ounce and up 1.2 percent, on track for its best week since mid-January on Chinese ETF inflows and PBoC buying. Jobless claims ticked up to 199,000 while July layoffs hit a two-year low. Reuters, CNBC, Globe and Mail, Aug 6–7.

## Worth reading

### Can Extreme Morning Sickness Be Cured?, Anna Russell, The New Yorker

Russell reports on hyperemesis gravidarum, the severe pregnancy vomiting that hospitalizes and occasionally kills, and the long medical embarrassment of having attributed it to maternal ambivalence, hysteria and rejection of the pregnancy. She traces the work identifying a biological driver and the emerging path to treatment, writing partly from inside her own second pregnancy. It is the ideal genre for you: a condition most internists learned as a footnote, reframed as a case study in how diagnostic authority gets misapplied to women for decades before the biology arrives, and a useful thing to have chewed on before the wards. [Read →](https://www.newyorker.com/news/essay/can-extreme-morning-sickness-be-cured)

### The Future, Made in China, Evan Osnos, The New Yorker

Osnos argues from Beijing that China has been quietly retooled to compete with Silicon Valley, and that Chinese leaders read a territorially-minded American foreign policy as actively helping them. He borrows Rush Doshi's frame: in the eighteenth century Russia fixated on conquering Eurasia while Britain built the steam engine, and only one of those bets compounded. The detail that sticks is that by 2023 seven in ten countries traded more with China than with the United States, a reversal nobody announced. It doubles as the geopolitical backdrop to the AI-capex story driving markets. [Read →](https://www.newyorker.com/magazine/2026/08/10/the-future-made-in-china)

### Are Psychedelics Right-Wing Now?, Michelle Lhooq, The New Yorker

Lhooq follows Bryan Hubbard and Americans for Ibogaine, with Rick Perry as chairman and Joe Rogan as amplifier, converting psychedelics from a countercultural cause into a Republican one, pitched through veterans, Navy SEALs and Texas oil money. The vehicle is ibogaine, which has a real opioid-detox signal and a real cardiotoxicity problem. It is sharp political ethnography about how a drug becomes a coalition, and directly relevant to a hospitalist who will eventually be asked about it at the bedside.
